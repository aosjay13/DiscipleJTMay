The Moedim in the Church

Keeping the Commandments of God · Holding the Faith of Jesus

✦

Foundation

As we open the Scriptures for this study, we look directly to Revelation 14:12: "Here is the patience of the saints: here are they that keep the commandments of God, and the faith of Jesus." This establishes the perfect harmony of our walk, resting entirely in the salvation provided by the Son while faithfully walking in obedience to the Father.

Before we walk through the appointed times one by one, we first settle what Scripture itself says happened at the cross. Colossians 2:14 is the verse most often used to say the feasts were nailed there, so we read it in its own sentence. Then we let the book of Hebrews name, in its own words, what actually changed. With that foundation laid, we study each feast, look at how Jesus and the apostles treated these days after the cross, and answer the objections most often raised against keeping them.

Introduction

The Moedim and the Commandments of God

Defining the Terms

To rightly divide the word of truth regarding God's appointed times and His laws, it is helpful to look at the original languages.

Moedim: the Hebrew word for "appointed times" or "divine appointments." In the KJV it is frequently translated as "feasts" or "seasons." We see this introduced in Leviticus 23:2, "Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, Concerning the feasts of the LORD, which ye shall proclaim to be holy convocations, even these are my feasts."
Miqra: the Hebrew word translated "convocation." It means a calling together, an assembly, and even a public reading. When God calls His days "holy convocations," He is calling His people to gather before Him on those days.
Torah: while often simply translated as "law," this word fundamentally means "instruction" or "teaching" from a loving Father to His children.
Mitzvot: this word translates to "commandments," referring to the specific precepts given by God.

"Even These Are My Feasts"

Leviticus 23 lays out the whole calendar of God's appointed times, and the first thing God does is claim them as His own.

Leviticus 23:1–4 1And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, 2Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, Concerning the feasts of the LORD, which ye shall proclaim to be holy convocations, even these are my feasts. 3Six days shall work be done: but the seventh day is the sabbath of rest, an holy convocation; ye shall do no work therein: it is the sabbath of the LORD in all your dwellings. 4These are the feasts of the LORD, even holy convocations, which ye shall proclaim in their seasons.

God calls them "my feasts." They are not Israel's feasts, and they are not Moses' feasts. They are the feasts of the LORD. Notice, too, which appointed time heads the list: the weekly Sabbath, called "an holy convocation" in the same chapter as Passover, Pentecost, and Tabernacles. Whatever we conclude about the annual feasts, Scripture has already tied them to the Sabbath.

God also said how long these days would stand. Of the Day of Atonement He said, "it shall be a statute for ever throughout your generations in all your dwellings" (Leviticus 23:31). Of Tabernacles He said, "It shall be a statute for ever in your generations" (Leviticus 23:41).

The Commandments of God

At the heart of God's instruction stand the Ten Commandments. God spoke them aloud from the mount, and as Exodus 31:18 reveals, He wrote them with His own finger on "tables of stone." They reflect His unchanging character, detailing exactly how to love Him and how to love our fellow man. Faith in Jesus does not abolish these commandments. Instead, His grace and the indwelling of the Holy Spirit empower us to keep them.

But Scripture never teaches that the finger of God is the dividing line between what stands and what was nailed to the cross. God Himself did not draw the line there. When He called Moses up the mountain, He promised him "tables of stone, and a law, and commandments which I have written" (Exodus 24:12). When Jesus quoted a command that Moses wrote, He called it "the commandment of God" and "the word of God" (Mark 7:9–13). Both of the greatest commandments He named are found in the books Moses wrote, not on the tables of stone (Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18). Scripture even calls the same book "the law of Moses" and "the law of the Lord" in back-to-back verses (Luke 2:22–24). Moses was the writer; the LORD was the Author.

This matters for our study, because the Moedim were given through Moses. When Revelation 14:12 speaks of the saints who "keep the commandments of God," we will not shrink that phrase down to fewer commandments than Jesus meant by it. Chapter IX takes up this question in full.

Chapter I

The Handwriting of Ordinances

No verse is quoted more often to say the feasts ended at the cross than Colossians 2:14. So before we study a single feast, we need to read that verse carefully, and the first rule is simple: always read a verse in its own sentence. Verse 14 is the middle of a thought that begins in verse 13 and carries through verse 17.

Colossians 2:13–17 13And you, being dead in your sins and the uncircumcision of your flesh, hath he quickened together with him, having forgiven you all trespasses; 14Blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross; 15And having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it. 16Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days: 17Which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ.

The Subject Is Forgiveness

Verse 13 ends with "having forgiven you all trespasses." Verse 14 then explains how those trespasses were forgiven: by blotting out the handwriting and nailing it to the cross. The subject of the passage is our sin and its record. The subject is not the feast calendar.

"Blotting Out" Is the Language of Erasing Sin

The Greek word for "blotting out" is exaleiphō. It is the same word Peter used when he preached that sins would be wiped away.

Acts 3:19 Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out, when the times of refreshing shall come from the presence of the Lord;

What gets blotted out in the New Testament is sin and its record, not God's commandments.

"Handwriting" Is a Record of Debt

Cheirographon: the Greek word translated "handwriting." It appears only here in the entire New Testament. It means a handwritten document, and in common use it described a signed note of debt: an IOU, or a certificate of what was owed.

What was nailed to the cross was the record of what we owed, the charges written against us because we broke God's law. It was not the law itself.

An Illustration When a debt is paid, the bank stamps the note "paid in full." The law against theft does not disappear. The note of what you owed disappears.

"Against Us" and "Contrary to Us"

Paul describes the thing nailed to the cross as something that was against us. Scripture never describes God's commandments that way. It says the very opposite.

Deuteronomy 10:12–13 12And now, Israel, what doth the LORD thy God require of thee, but to fear the LORD thy God, to walk in all his ways, and to love him, and to serve the LORD thy God with all thy heart and with all thy soul, 13To keep the commandments of the LORD, and his statutes, which I command thee this day for thy good?
Romans 7:12 Wherefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good.
Psalm 19:7 The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple.

The commandments were given "for thy good." The thing that was "against us" was the record of our guilt.

"Ordinances" Are Decrees, and Paul Defines Them

Dogma: the Greek word translated "ordinances." It means a decree. In the New Testament the noun appears only five times, and its verb form once more.
  • Luke 2:1: "there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed."
  • Acts 16:4: the decrees of the apostles and elders at Jerusalem.
  • Acts 17:7: "these all do contrary to the decrees of Caesar."
  • Ephesians 2:15: "the law of commandments contained in ordinances" (answered in Chapter IX).
  • Colossians 2:14: "the handwriting of ordinances."
  • Colossians 2:20: the verb form, "subject to ordinances."

Outside of Colossians 2:14 and Ephesians 2:15, the two passages in question, the word is never used for the law God gave through Moses. And Paul tells us exactly what kind of "ordinances" were troubling the Colossians, just six verses later.

Colossians 2:20–23 20Wherefore if ye be dead with Christ from the rudiments of the world, why, as though living in the world, are ye subject to ordinances, 21(Touch not; taste not; handle not; 22Which all are to perish with the using;) after the commandments and doctrines of men? 23Which things have indeed a shew of wisdom in will worship, and humility, and neglecting of the body; not in any honour to the satisfying of the flesh.

"Touch not; taste not; handle not" is not a quotation from Moses. Paul himself calls these "the commandments and doctrines of men." He gave the same warning at the start of the chapter.

Colossians 2:8 Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit, after the tradition of men, after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ.

The problem in Colossae was man-made asceticism and philosophy, not the feasts of the LORD.

What Was Nailed to the Cross

Not the law of God, and not His appointed times. What was nailed to the cross was the record of our debt, the written charges that stood against us because we broke God's law. That record was blotted out when our trespasses were forgiven. The commandments that showed us our sin still stand, and they were always given for our good.

"Let No Man Therefore Judge You"

Paul's next words, beginning with "therefore," are the ones most often used against the feasts. Read carefully, they work in the opposite direction.

The Sabbath Is in the Same List

Verse 16 names "an holyday," "the new moon," and "the sabbath days" in one breath, and it treats all three the same way.

The Dilemma

If Colossians 2:16 means these days are done away, then "the sabbath days" are done away too, and the fourth commandment, written by the finger of God, has been removed along with them. Anyone who holds that the Ten Commandments still stand cannot read the verse that way.

If Colossians 2:16 does not remove the Sabbath, then by the same grammar it does not remove the holyday or the new moon either. The verse cannot be used to keep one and discard the others. It treats them identically.

"Let No Man Judge You" Is Not "You Are Released From"

Paul does not say "you no longer need to keep these." He says do not let anyone sit in judgment over you in respect of them. In a congregation surrounded by pagan philosophy (Colossians 2:8) and ascetic rule-makers (Colossians 2:20–23), those keeping God's days were the ones likely to be judged. Paul is defending the believers who kept these days, not forbidding them.

"Which Are a Shadow of Things to Come"

Here Paul uses the Greek word skia for "shadow" and soma for "body." The Moedim are a prophetic shadow outlining God's entire plan of redemption, and Christ is the substance that casts it. But notice the tense. Paul wrote this after the cross, and he does not say these days were a shadow. He says they are a shadow, present tense, of things to come, future.

Some of those things are still to come: the trumpet and the resurrection of the dead (1 Corinthians 15:52; 1 Thessalonians 4:16), and God dwelling with His people (Revelation 21:3). A shadow of things still to come is still casting its shadow. That is why, in the chapters ahead, we will find the spring feasts looking back to what Christ has done and the fall feasts looking forward to what He will yet do.

Teacher's Note · "The Body Is of Christ"

In the KJV, the word "is" in "but the body is of Christ" is printed in italics, which means the translators supplied it. The Greek reads more literally "but the body of Christ." Some read the verse this way: let no man judge you in these things, but let the body of Christ, the church (Colossians 1:18, 24), do so.

This reading is possible but contested. It is worth mentioning, but do not make the argument rest on it. The dilemma of verse 16 and the present tense of verse 17 carry the argument without it.

Chapter II

What Changed at the Cross

If the feasts were not nailed to the cross, every believer rightly asks the next question: then what did change? We do not have to guess. The book of Hebrews is the one place in Scripture that explains in detail what changed at the cross, and it names the change plainly.

Using Scripture's Own Words

Scripture never says "the handwriting of ordinances" means "whatever Moses wrote." Scripture never says "only what the finger of God wrote remains." That dividing line is an inference, not a statement of the text. So when we describe what changed, we will use only the words Scripture itself uses:

  • "The priesthood" (Hebrews 7:12)
  • "The law of a carnal commandment" (Hebrews 7:16)
  • "Gifts and sacrifices" (Hebrews 9:9)
  • "Carnal ordinances" (Hebrews 9:10)
  • "Offering for sin" (Hebrews 10:18)

Every one of those terms points at the Levitical priesthood and the altar. None of them points at the Sabbath, the feasts, or the holy convocations.

The Priesthood Changed, and the Law of the Priesthood With It

Hebrews 7:11–14 11If therefore perfection were by the Levitical priesthood, (for under it the people received the law,) what further need was there that another priest should rise after the order of Melchisedec, and not be called after the order of Aaron? 12For the priesthood being changed, there is made of necessity a change also of the law. 13For he of whom these things are spoken pertaineth to another tribe, of which no man gave attendance at the altar. 14For it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe Moses spake nothing concerning priesthood.

The "change also of the law" in verse 12 is explained immediately in verses 13 and 14: it concerns which tribe serves at the altar. Jesus is from Judah, and Moses gave Judah no priesthood. The law governing the priesthood had to change for Jesus to be our High Priest.

Hebrews 7:15–19 15And it is yet far more evident: for that after the similitude of Melchisedec there ariseth another priest, 16Who is made, not after the law of a carnal commandment, but after the power of an endless life. 17For he testifieth, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec. 18For there is verily a disannulling of the commandment going before for the weakness and unprofitableness thereof. 19For the law made nothing perfect, but the bringing in of a better hope did; by the which we draw nigh unto God.

"The commandment going before" in verse 18 is "the law of a carnal commandment" in verse 16: the command that made men priests by physical descent from Aaron. That is what was disannulled. Our High Priest is made "after the power of an endless life."

The Carnal Ordinances of the First Tabernacle

Hebrews 9:8–10 8The Holy Ghost this signifying, that the way into the holiest of all was not yet made manifest, while as the first tabernacle was yet standing: 9Which was a figure for the time then present, in which were offered both gifts and sacrifices, that could not make him that did the service perfect, as pertaining to the conscience; 10Which stood only in meats and drinks, and divers washings, and carnal ordinances, imposed on them until the time of reformation.

The "carnal ordinances" are defined by their setting: the first tabernacle, where "gifts and sacrifices" were offered. The "meats and drinks" here are the meat offerings and drink offerings of the altar service, not the daily diet. They were "imposed on them until the time of reformation," and in Christ that time has come.

Word Note · Two Different Words The word for "ordinances" in Hebrews 9:1 and 9:10 is dikaiōma, a different Greek word from the dogma of Colossians 2:14. The two passages should not be merged as if they used the same term.

No More Offering for Sin

The sacrifices for sin ended because the one sacrifice was complete.

Hebrews 10:11–12 11And every priest standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins: 12But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God;
Hebrews 10:18 Now where remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin.

The Law Written on the Heart

In the same letter that describes the change of the priesthood, God promises to write His laws on the heart.

Hebrews 8:10 For this is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after those days, saith the Lord; I will put my laws into their mind, and write them in their hearts: and I will be to them a God, and they shall be to me a people:
Jeremiah 31:33 But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the LORD, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people.

When Jeremiah spoke, "my law" meant the law his hearers already had: the law given through Moses. The New Covenant does not shrink that law down to two tables of stone. It moves it from stone and parchment onto the heart.

What Hebrews Names, and What It Never Names

Named as changed: the Levitical priesthood, the carnal commandment of priestly descent, the gifts and sacrifices, the carnal ordinances of the first tabernacle, and the offering for sin.

Never named as changed: the Sabbath, the feasts of the LORD, the holy convocations, or anything written by Moses outside the priesthood and the altar.

The Days and Their Offerings

We need to be honest about one thing. The feasts in the law came with offerings at the altar (Numbers 28–29), and the three pilgrim feasts were kept at the place God chose (Deuteronomy 16:16). Those offerings belong to the priesthood and the altar, and they are exactly what Hebrews says was fulfilled.

What remains is everything that never depended on the altar: the holy convocation, the rest from work, the remembrance, and the rejoicing before the LORD. Paul's "let us keep the feast" (1 Corinthians 5:8) was written to a congregation in Corinth, far from any altar. Our liberty is in how a day is kept now that the altar service is fulfilled, not in whether it is kept.

Walking in Spirit and Truth

Today, as believers keeping the faith of Jesus, we observe the Sabbath and the Moedim not to earn our salvation, nor by shedding the blood of animals. We observe them to honor our Creator, to remember His acts of deliverance, and to rehearse the prophetic events of Christ's return. The Sabbath remains a holy convocation, a weekly appointed time, where we rest in the finished work of our Savior while joyfully keeping the commandments of God. With that foundation laid, we turn to the feasts themselves.

Chapter III

Passover & Unleavened Bread

As we look at the Moedim, or appointed times of God, we must always anchor our understanding in Revelation 14:12: "Here is the patience of the saints: here are they that keep the commandments of God, and the faith of Jesus." This verse beautifully defines the walk of the believer. We do not choose between the law of the Father and the grace of the Son. Rather, the faith of Jesus empowers us to walk in obedience to the commandments of God.

The spring feasts begin with Passover (Pesach) and immediately transition into the Feast of Unleavened Bread (Chag HaMatzot). These are not merely ancient historical markers. They are profound divine appointments that reveal the very heart of the gospel and God's redemptive plan.

Leviticus 23:5 In the fourteenth day of the first month at even is the LORD's passover.

Historically, Passover remembers the mighty deliverance of Israel from the bondage of Egypt. The blood of a spotless lamb was placed on the doorposts of the Israelites so that the judgment of death would pass over their homes.

Prophetically, the events of Egypt and the sacrifices of the earthly tabernacle were a skia (the Greek word for shadow) of the ultimate deliverance. Jesus is the soma (the Greek word for body or substance) of this shadow. John the Baptist recognized this immediately, calling Him the Lamb of God. As the Apostle Paul confirms in 1 Corinthians 5:7, "For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us."

The Passover lamb was slain at the altar, and that altar service belonged to the priesthood that Hebrews says has changed. Jesus shed His blood once and for all to deliver us from the bondage of sin, and "where remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin" (Hebrews 10:18). Because He is our sacrifice, we no longer shed the blood of lambs, but we absolutely continue to remember and honor the day of His deliverance.

Immediately following Passover is the Feast of Unleavened Bread.

Leviticus 23:6 And on the fifteenth day of the same month is the feast of unleavened bread unto the LORD: seven days ye must eat unleavened bread.

In Scripture, leaven (in Hebrew, chametz) is consistently used as a symbol for sin, pride, hypocrisy, and false teaching that puffs up. God commanded His people to search their homes and remove all leaven.

Because we have the faith of Jesus, we do not view this appointed time as a burden, but as a joyful commandment. Paul specifically exhorted the Gentile believers in Corinth to continue observing this very appointed time.

1 Corinthians 5:8 Therefore let us keep the feast, not with old leaven, neither with the leaven of malice and wickedness; but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.

As believers, we physically remove the leaven from our homes during these seven days as an outward, physical expression of an inward, spiritual reality. Through Christ our past sins are washed away, and by the Holy Spirit we are actively walking in obedience to the commandments of God, purging the remaining sin from our daily lives. We do not remove leaven to earn our salvation. We do it because we have been saved and freed by the perfect Passover Lamb, and we desire to walk in holiness.

Bonus · Rabbinic Traditions

Over the centuries, Rabbinic Judaism developed the Passover Seder, an ordered meal and liturgy designed to retell the Exodus story. One of the most striking traditions involves a specific piece of matzah (unleavened bread) called the Afikoman. During the meal, three pieces of matzah are placed together in a special pouch. The middle piece is taken out and broken. Half of it is hidden away, wrapped in a cloth. Later in the meal the children search for it, and once found it is brought back and eaten as the final taste of the Passover meal. For those holding the faith of Jesus, this tradition is a profound, albeit unintended, picture of the Messiah. He is the second person of the Godhead who was broken for our transgressions, wrapped in linen and hidden in the tomb, and then resurrected and brought forth.

Bonus · Early Church Fathers' Traditions

In the early centuries of the church there was a major historical conflict known as the Quartodeciman Controversy. "Quartodeciman" comes from the Latin word for "fourteenth." Early church leaders in Asia Minor, such as Polycarp (a direct disciple of the Apostle John) and later Polycrates, insisted on observing Passover strictly on the fourteenth day of the biblical month of Nisan, exactly as commanded in the law. They refused to adopt the Roman church's newly instituted practice of observing a resurrection Sunday festival, which later became known as Easter, that ignored the biblical calendar. Polycrates wrote a letter to the bishop of Rome firmly defending their fourteenth-of-Nisan practice, stating they were simply following the Gospel and the traditions handed down directly by the Apostles. This historical record shows that the earliest believers, guided by the Apostles, continued to faithfully keep the biblical Moedim.

Chapter IV

First Fruits and Pentecost

Continuing our study through the lens of Revelation 14:12, we transition from the deliverance of Passover to the harvest appointed times: First Fruits and Pentecost (the Feast of Weeks). These appointed times perfectly connect the physical agricultural cycle of Israel to the profound spiritual realities of the Messiah and the Holy Spirit.

The Scriptural Command: When and How to Count

The instructions for these two appointed times are intrinsically linked in Scripture by a literal countdown.

First Fruits

God instructed the Israelites to bring the very first harvest of their barley crop to the priest.

Leviticus 23:11 And he shall wave the sheaf before the LORD, to be accepted for you: on the morrow after the sabbath the priest shall wave it.

Pentecost (Feast of Weeks)

From that exact day of First Fruits, a specific count is commanded to reach the next appointed time.

Leviticus 23:15–16 And ye shall count unto you from the morrow after the sabbath, from the day that ye brought the sheaf of the wave offering; seven sabbaths shall be complete: Even unto the morrow after the seventh sabbath shall ye number fifty days; and ye shall offer a new meat offering unto the LORD.

Because "Pentecost" is simply the Greek word for "fiftieth," the day arrives precisely fifty days after First Fruits, aligning with the wheat harvest.

The Prophetic Fulfillment

Just as Passover foreshadowed the crucifixion, First Fruits and Pentecost perfectly mirror the resurrection and the giving of the Holy Spirit.

  • Christ our First Fruits: the "morrow after the sabbath" is Sunday. Jesus resurrected on the exact day the high priest was waving the first ripened barley before God. The Apostle Paul explicitly confirms this in 1 Corinthians 15:20: "But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept." Jesus is the firstborn from the dead, guaranteeing a much larger harvest of believers to follow.
  • The Fire of Pentecost: fifty days later, as the people gathered in Jerusalem to offer the new grain of the wheat harvest, God poured out His Spirit. Acts 2:1–4 records, "And when the day of Pentecost was fully come, they were all with one accord in one place... And they were all filled with the Holy Ghost."
  • The Harmony of Sinai and Zion: historically, the children of Israel arrived at Mount Sinai exactly fifty days after leaving Egypt. At Sinai God gave His law (His instruction): the Ten Commandments written by His own finger on tablets of stone, and His statutes and judgments given through Moses. On the exact same day in Acts 2, the Holy Spirit was given to write that very same instruction upon the fleshy tables of the heart, just as God promised: "I will put my laws into their mind, and write them in their hearts" (Hebrews 8:10). Pentecost is not a replacement of God's law, but the empowerment to walk in it.

Bonus · Rabbinic Traditions

During the Second Temple period a massive theological conflict erupted between the two ruling classes, the Pharisees and the Sadducees, regarding exactly how to start the count for Pentecost. The entire debate centered on how to interpret the phrase "the morrow after the sabbath" in Leviticus 23:11.

  • The Sadducees' Literal Count: the Sadducees interpreted "sabbath" to mean the weekly seventh-day Sabbath. Thus "the morrow after the sabbath" was always a Sunday. By counting fifty days from a Sunday, Pentecost would also always fall on a Sunday.
  • The Pharisees' Annual Count: the Pharisees interpreted the word "sabbath" as the annual high holy day of Unleavened Bread (Nisan 15). Therefore they began the fifty-day count on Nisan 16, regardless of what day of the week it fell on.

Modern Rabbinic Judaism follows the tradition of the Pharisees. To this day, the "Counting of the Omer" begins on the second night of Passover. In rabbinic tradition this forty-nine-day count is viewed as a period of intense spiritual preparation and refinement, counting down the days from the physical deliverance of Egypt to the spiritual revelation of the law at Mount Sinai.

Bonus · Early Church Fathers' Traditions

The early church fathers attached immense joy and theological weight to the fifty-day period between First Fruits and Pentecost, viewing it not just as a countdown but as a continuous season of victory.

  • No Fasting or Kneeling: because Christ had conquered the grave, early church leaders mandated that the entire fifty-day season be treated with the same reverence as Resurrection Sunday. Tertullian and others noted the tradition that during the fifty days of Pentecost, believers were forbidden to fast or even to kneel during prayer, standing upright to represent the resurrection life.
  • The Symbolism of Fifty: the early scholars loved the biblical numerology of the count. Clement of Alexandria likened the fifty days to the width of Noah's Ark, viewing the season as a symbol of hope and divine pardon.
  • The Connection to the Jubilee: Origen connected the fifty days of Pentecost directly to the fiftieth year of Jubilee found in Leviticus 25. Just as the Year of Jubilee required the release of captives, the forgiveness of debts, and the return of property, the early church viewed Pentecost as the ultimate spiritual Jubilee, where the Holy Spirit brings true liberty, cancels the debt of sin, and restores humanity to the Father.
Chapter V

Feast of Trumpets

As we continue our study, we cross a profound prophetic threshold. The spring appointed times (Passover, Unleavened Bread, First Fruits, and Pentecost) found their great fulfillment in the first coming of the Messiah, though Jesus Himself said the Passover still waits to be "fulfilled in the kingdom of God" (Luke 22:16). Now we arrive at the fall appointed times, which prophetically outline the exact events of Jesus' glorious return. This prophetic shift begins with the Feast of Trumpets.

The Scriptural Command: A Day of Shouting

In the biblical calendar a massive shift occurs in the seventh month.

Leviticus 23:24 Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, In the seventh month, in the first day of the month, shall ye have a sabbath, a memorial of blowing of trumpets, an holy convocation.
Numbers 29:1 And in the seventh month, on the first day of the month, ye shall have an holy convocation; ye shall do no servile work: it is a day of blowing the trumpets unto you.

The Hebrew word used here for "blowing of trumpets" is Teruah. It literally translates to a massive shout, an alarm, or an awakening blast. It is a day specifically appointed by the Father to sound an alarm. Historically, the blowing of the silver trumpets or the shofar (ram's horn) in Israel was used to gather the assembly, to signal the breaking of camp, or to sound the alarm for war.

The Prophetic Fulfillment: The Return of the King

For those holding the faith of Jesus, the Feast of Trumpets is the ultimate rehearsal for the return of our King and the resurrection of the righteous dead. Jesus' return is intrinsically linked to the blowing of a trumpet.

1 Thessalonians 4:16 For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first.
1 Corinthians 15:52 In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed.

Just as the early believers kept the spring feasts looking backward in remembrance of Christ's sacrifice, we observe the Feast of Trumpets looking forward in joyful anticipation of His return. It is a day of rest, a holy convocation, where we rehearse the sounding of the alarm that will one day shatter the sky.

Bonus · Rabbinic Traditions

In Rabbinic Judaism the Feast of Trumpets underwent a significant transformation during the Babylonian exile. Today it is almost exclusively referred to as Rosh Hashanah, which means "Head of the Year," serving as the Jewish civil New Year. There are several deeply profound traditions attached to this day.

  • The Hidden Day: this is the only biblical feast that falls exactly on the first day of a month (the New Moon). Because the ancient biblical calendar relied on the visual sighting of the crescent moon by two witnesses, it was a day that could not be known with certainty in advance. Some teachers connect this with Jesus' words, "of that day and hour knoweth no man" (Matthew 24:36). Hold that connection loosely. The Lord's point was readiness, not date setting, and no feast gives us the day of His return.
  • The 100 Blasts: tradition dictates that the shofar must be blown precisely one hundred times during the service. The blasts are broken into different rhythms: Tekiah (one long blast), Shevarim (three broken blasts), and Teruah (nine rapid, staccato blasts). The hundredth and final blast is known as the Tekiah Gedolah, the great, long blast. Some teachers compare it to Paul's "last trump" (1 Corinthians 15:52). Scripture does not command these blasts, and the custom took its present form in the synagogue after the temple fell, so treat the comparison as an illustration, not as the source of Paul's words.
  • The Days of Awe: in Judaism the Feast of Trumpets begins a ten-day period of intense introspection, repentance, and making right with one's fellow man before the coming judgment on the Day of Atonement. Tradition states that on this day God opens the Book of Life to begin judging the world.

Bonus · Early Church Fathers' Traditions

While the early church focused heavily on the spring feasts due to their direct historical connection to the crucifixion and the giving of the Spirit, they viewed the Feast of Trumpets through a highly prophetic and typological lens.

  • The Trumpets of Jericho: early church fathers such as Irenaeus of Lyons frequently connected the end-time trumpets to the historical account of Joshua at the battle of Jericho. Just as Joshua commanded seven priests to blow seven ram's horns for seven days before the walls of the enemy collapsed, the early church viewed this as a prophetic shadow. They tied this directly to the Book of Revelation, understanding that Jesus would return at the sounding of the final, seventh trumpet to utterly destroy the kingdoms of this world.
  • The Awakening Call: scholars like John Chrysostom wrote extensively on the "Trumpet of God," identifying it not just as a loud noise but as the literal voice of God vibrating through the earth to awaken the dead. They viewed the biblical trumpet blasts as the divine mechanism that would reconstitute the physical bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep.
Chapter VI

Day of Atonement

Following the awakening blast of the Feast of Trumpets, the fall appointed times progress into the most solemn and holy day of the biblical calendar: the Day of Atonement. If the Feast of Trumpets is the alarm of the King's return, the Day of Atonement prophetically points to the final Day of Judgment, the cleansing of the sanctuary, and the ultimate eradication of sin.

Reading this day in the light of Hebrews, we see that the gifts and sacrifices of this day, the blood of bulls and goats offered by the Levitical high priest, were completely fulfilled by our Great High Priest. Yet the divine appointment of the day remains for the saints as a solemn rehearsal of God's final justice and mercy.

The Scriptural Command: A Day of Affliction

Leviticus 23:27–28 Also on the tenth day of this seventh month there shall be a day of atonement: it shall be an holy convocation unto you; and ye shall afflict your souls, and offer an offering made by fire unto the LORD. And ye shall do no work in that same day: for it is a day of atonement, to make an atonement for you before the LORD your God.

The command to "afflict your souls" is universally understood biblically as fasting, abstaining from food and water to demonstrate complete reliance on God and deep repentance. It is a day of absolute rest, a Sabbath of Sabbaths.

The priestly service for this day is detailed in Leviticus 16. These are the "gifts and sacrifices" and "carnal ordinances" of the first tabernacle that Hebrews says were "imposed on them until the time of reformation" (Hebrews 9:9–10). It was the only day of the year the High Priest was permitted to enter the Holy of Holies. He cast lots over two goats. One was the "Lord's goat," whose blood was shed and sprinkled on the Mercy Seat to temporarily cover the sins of the nation. The other was the "scapegoat." The High Priest would lay his hands on the scapegoat, confess the sins of Israel over it, and send it away alive into the wilderness, visually removing the sin from the camp.

The Prophetic Fulfillment: The True Mercy Seat

The Book of Hebrews masterfully explains how the earthly rituals of the Day of Atonement were a shadow of Jesus' heavenly ministry. The blood of animals could only temporarily push sin forward; it could never erase it.

Hebrews 9:11–12 But Christ being come an high priest of good things to come, by a greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands, that is to say, not of this building; Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us.

Jesus fulfilled the role of both the sacrifice and the High Priest. When He ascended to the Father, He presented His own blood in the true, heavenly Holy of Holies. Prophetically, while Christ has already paid the price for our sins, the ultimate fulfillment of this day looks toward the end of the age: the final judgment of the earth, the cleansing of the universe from the stain of sin, and the banishment of the adversary, often typified by the scapegoat being cast into the desolate wilderness, mirroring the binding of Satan in Revelation 20.

Bonus · Rabbinic Traditions

The Day of Atonement is the culmination of the "Days of Awe" that began on the Feast of Trumpets. In Rabbinic Judaism there are stunning traditions surrounding this day that unintentionally bear witness to the Messiah.

  • The Crimson Thread: the Talmud (Tractate Yoma 39b) records a remarkable historical phenomenon from the Second Temple period. When the scapegoat was sent into the wilderness, a crimson thread was tied to the sanctuary doors. When the goat reached the wilderness, the thread would turn white, fulfilling Isaiah 1:18 ("though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow"). However, the Talmud explicitly records that for the final forty years before the Temple was destroyed, the thread stopped turning white. The Temple was destroyed in 70 AD. Going back exactly forty years points to 30 AD, the exact time Jesus, the true Lamb of God, was crucified. God was signaling that the blood of goats was no longer accepted, because the perfect sacrifice had been made.
  • The Closing of the Gates: the final service of the Day of Atonement as the sun goes down is called Ne'ilah. Tradition teaches that during this hour the gates of heaven are closing, and God seals the Book of Life for the coming year. It is a moment of intense, desperate prayer, reflecting the prophetic reality that a day is coming when the door of grace will shut, just as God shut the door of Noah's Ark.
  • Wearing the Kittel: many observant Jewish men wear a white linen robe called a kittel on this day. It is the same garment they will eventually be buried in. It serves a dual purpose, representing the purity of the angels and reminding the wearer of their mortality, essentially dying to the flesh through fasting and repentance.

Bonus · Early Church Fathers' Traditions

The early Christian apologists frequently used the typology of the Day of Atonement to explain the Gospel to their Jewish contemporaries.

  • The Two Goats as the Two Comings: in the Epistle of Barnabas (written between 70 and 132 AD) and later in the writings of Justin Martyr (in his Dialogue with Trypho), the early church fathers taught that the two goats of Leviticus 16 represented the two advents of Christ. The first goat, slaughtered as a sin offering, represented His first coming in humility, suffering, and death. The second goat, sent away bearing the sins of the people, represented His second coming. They noted that both goats had to be identical in appearance, prophetically pointing to the fact that the same Jesus who was pierced and rejected will be the exact same Jesus who returns in glory, recognized by those who pierced Him (Zechariah 12:10).
  • The True Fast: while acknowledging the physical fast, early theologians like Origen emphasized that the true "affliction of the soul" was abstaining from sin. They taught that keeping the physical fast was meaningless if the believer did not also fast from malice, greed, and worldly desires, marrying the physical appointed time with the spiritual transformation required by the commandments of God.
Chapter VII

Feast of Tabernacles and the Eighth Day

We now reach the joyful climax of the biblical calendar: the Feast of Tabernacles and its companion, the Eighth Day. If Passover represents our justification, and Pentecost our sanctification by the Spirit, then Tabernacles represents our ultimate glorification. It is the great harvest festival, completely transitioning from the solemn fasting of the Day of Atonement into a mandated season of overwhelming joy.

As those holding the faith of Jesus, we recognize that this appointed time points directly to the Millennial Kingdom, when the Messiah will physically dwell (tabernacle) with His people on earth.

The Scriptural Command: A Season of Joy

Leviticus 23:34, 39–43 Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, The fifteenth day of this seventh month shall be the feast of tabernacles for seven days unto the LORD... Also in the fifteenth day of the seventh month, when ye have gathered in the fruit of the land, ye shall keep a feast unto the LORD seven days... And ye shall take you on the first day the boughs of goodly trees, branches of palm trees, and the boughs of thick trees, and willows of the brook; and ye shall rejoice before the LORD your God seven days... Ye shall dwell in booths seven days; all that are Israelites born shall dwell in booths.

God commanded His people to leave the comfort of their permanent homes and live in fragile, temporary shelters (booths) constructed of branches. This physically reminded them of their dependence on God during the wilderness wanderings.

Immediately attached to this seven-day feast is a distinct, one-day appointed time known as the Eighth Day.

Leviticus 23:36 ...on the eighth day shall be an holy convocation unto you; and ye shall offer an offering made by fire unto the LORD: it is a solemn assembly; and ye shall do no servile work therein.

The Prophetic Fulfillment: God with Us

The Feast of Tabernacles perfectly encapsulates both the first and second comings of Jesus.

  • The Word Made Flesh: the Apostle John uses explicit Tabernacles imagery to describe the incarnation. John 1:14 says, "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." The Greek word used for "dwelt" literally translates to "tabernacled" or "pitched his tent." Jesus left His eternal home to temporarily dwell in the fragile booth of a human body.
  • The Millennial Reign: prophetically, the seven days of the feast picture the coming thousand-year reign of Christ on earth. Zechariah 14 makes it abundantly clear that this appointed time was not nailed to the cross, nor was it meant only for the Jews. Speaking of the future earthly reign of Jesus, Zechariah 14:16 prophesies: "And it shall come to pass, that every one that is left of all the nations which came against Jerusalem shall even go up from year to year to worship the King, the LORD of hosts, and to keep the feast of tabernacles."
  • The Eighth Day (Eternity): in biblical numerology, seven represents completion (the Millennial Kingdom), while eight represents a new beginning. The Eighth Day points past the Millennium to the New Heavens and the New Earth, where time as we know it ceases. Revelation 21:3 perfectly captures this reality: "And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God."

Bonus · Rabbinic Traditions

During the Second Temple period the celebration of Tabernacles was magnificent, and Jesus directly used these traditions to declare His messiahship.

  • The Water Libation Ceremony: each morning of the feast the High Priest would draw "living water" from the Pool of Siloam in a golden pitcher. He would carry it in a joyful procession to the Temple and pour it on the altar to pray for the winter rains. John 7:37–38 records exactly what Jesus did at this precise moment: "In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink. He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water."
  • The Illumination of the Temple: during the feast, massive seventy-five-foot-tall menorahs were erected in the Court of the Women. Rabbinic literature states that the light from these lamps was so brilliant it illuminated every courtyard in Jerusalem. It was in the direct aftermath of this spectacular tradition that Jesus declared in John 8:12, "I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life."
  • Welcoming the Guests: it is a tradition to symbolically welcome holy guests, such as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, into the booth each evening. For believers this echoes Jesus' promise in Matthew 8:11: "That many shall come from the east and west, and shall sit down with Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, in the kingdom of heaven."

Bonus · Early Church Fathers' Traditions

The early church fathers saw the Feast of Tabernacles as the ultimate goal of the Christian walk.

  • Methodius of Olympus (ca. 300 AD): in his work The Banquet of the Ten Virgins, Methodius wrote extensively on the Feast of Tabernacles. He explicitly taught that the literal, physical resurrection of the dead is the true Feast of Tabernacles. He reasoned that our current bodies are fallen tents, but in the resurrection God will "rebuild the tabernacle of David" by giving us glorified, immortal bodies that will never decay. He instructed believers to celebrate the feast by living holy lives now, preparing their tabernacles for eternal life.
  • The Seventh Millennium: early theologians like Irenaeus and Justin Martyr heavily utilized the concept of the "Sabbath Millennium." They taught that just as there were six days of creation followed by a day of rest, there would be six thousand years of human history followed by a thousand-year Sabbath rest, the Messianic Kingdom. They viewed the seven days of the Feast of Tabernacles as the ultimate prophetic rehearsal of this glorious seventh millennium of peace.
  • The Eighth Day as the Eternal State: the early fathers consistently referred to the eternal state beyond the Millennium as "The Eighth Day." They understood it not just as an add-on to the feast, but as the transition from the temporal world (governed by the seven-day week) into the infinite, unbounded presence of the Father.
Chapter VIII

The Feasts After the Cross

We have walked through each appointed time and seen how it points to Christ. Now we ask a practical question: how did Jesus and the apostles treat these days after the cross? If the feasts had been nailed to the cross, we would expect the apostles to set them aside. The record shows the opposite.

Jesus Expects to Keep the Passover Again

Luke 22:15–16 15And he said unto them, With desire I have desired to eat this passover with you before I suffer: 16For I say unto you, I will not any more eat thereof, until it be fulfilled in the kingdom of God.

Jesus does not say He will never eat the Passover again. He says "until." He looks ahead to eating it again when it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God (see also Matthew 26:29).

Paul Told a Gentile Congregation to Keep the Feast

1 Corinthians 5:7–8 7Purge out therefore the old leaven, that ye may be a new lump, as ye are unleavened. For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us: 8Therefore let us keep the feast, not with old leaven, neither with the leaven of malice and wickedness; but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.

Christ our Passover is sacrificed. Paul's conclusion is not "therefore the feast is over." It is "therefore let us keep the feast," with a heart free of malice. This was written to Corinth, far from any altar.

Paul and Luke Marked Time by the Feasts

Acts 20:6 And we sailed away from Philippi after the days of unleavened bread, and came unto them to Troas in five days; where we abode seven days.
Acts 20:16 For Paul had determined to sail by Ephesus, because he would not spend the time in Asia: for he hasted, if it were possible for him, to be at Jerusalem the day of Pentecost.
Acts 27:9 Now when much time was spent, and when sailing was now dangerous, because the fast was now already past, Paul admonished them,

"The fast" in Acts 27:9 is the Day of Atonement, the one fast day commanded in the law (Leviticus 23:27). Luke marks the season of the year by it without needing to explain it.

Acts 18:21 But bade them farewell, saying, I must by all means keep this feast that cometh in Jerusalem: but I will return again unto you, if God will. And he sailed from Ephesus.
Teacher's Note · Acts 18:21 The words "I must by all means keep this feast that cometh in Jerusalem" are in the KJV and the Received Text, but they are missing from many older Greek manuscripts and from most modern translations. Expect someone to point this out. Use Acts 20:16 and 1 Corinthians 5:8 as the primary evidence, and treat Acts 18:21 as supporting only.

Paul Believed All That Was Written in the Law

Acts 24:14 But this I confess unto thee, that after the way which they call heresy, so worship I the God of my fathers, believing all things which are written in the law and in the prophets:
Acts 25:8 While he answered for himself, Neither against the law of the Jews, neither against the temple, nor yet against Caesar, have I offended any thing at all.

If Paul had taught that the writings of Moses were nailed to the cross, he could not have said either of these in his defense before Felix and Festus.

The Feasts and Sabbaths Continue Into the Age to Come

Zechariah 14:16–19 16And it shall come to pass, that every one that is left of all the nations which came against Jerusalem shall even go up from year to year to worship the King, the LORD of hosts, and to keep the feast of tabernacles. 17And it shall be, that whoso will not come up of all the families of the earth unto Jerusalem to worship the King, the LORD of hosts, even upon them shall be no rain. 18And if the family of Egypt go not up, and come not, that have no rain; there shall be the plague, wherewith the LORD will smite the heathen that come not up to keep the feast of tabernacles. 19This shall be the punishment of Egypt, and the punishment of all nations that come not up to keep the feast of tabernacles.
Isaiah 66:22–23 22For as the new heavens and the new earth, which I will make, shall remain before me, saith the LORD, so shall your seed and your name remain. 23And it shall come to pass, that from one new moon to another, and from one sabbath to another, shall all flesh come to worship before me, saith the LORD.

A feast that was nailed to the cross would not be kept by all nations under the reign of the King.

Chapter IX

Answering the Objections

Believers who keep God's appointed times will be asked about it, and Peter tells us to "be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15). This chapter takes up the objections most often raised, beginning with the one that shapes most of the others.

The Finger of God Objection

Stated fairly, the position is this:

  1. Only the Ten Commandments were written by the finger of God (Exodus 31:18).
  2. Everything else was written by the hand of Moses.
  3. Colossians 2:14 says the "handwriting of ordinances" was nailed to the cross.
  4. Therefore everything Moses wrote is done away, including the feasts and the holy convocations.
Exodus 31:18 And he gave unto Moses, when he had made an end of communing with him upon mount Sinai, two tables of testimony, tables of stone, written with the finger of God.

The Ten Commandments truly were written by the finger of God. Nobody disputes that. The question is whether Scripture ever teaches that this is the dividing line between what stands and what was nailed to the cross. Chapter I showed that the "handwriting" of Colossians 2:14 is the record of our debt, not the writing of Moses. Now we look at how Jesus and the apostles treated what Moses wrote.

The Standard Cuts Both Ways

Those who hold this view often ask that we not use labels Scripture does not use. That is a fair request, and we should honor it completely. But the same rule has to apply in both directions. Scripture never says "the handwriting of ordinances" means "whatever Moses wrote," and it never says "only what the finger of God wrote remains." That dividing line is an inference, not a statement of the text. If we are held to Scripture's own vocabulary, so is the finger-only position.

Jesus Refuses to Separate Moses From God

The finger-only position depends on separating "what God wrote" from "what Moses wrote." Jesus Himself refuses that separation.

He Calls the Words of Moses "the Commandment of God"

Mark 7:8–13 8For laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men, as the washing of pots and cups: and many other such like things ye do. 9And he said unto them, Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition. 10For Moses said, Honour thy father and thy mother; and, Whoso curseth father or mother, let him die the death: 11But ye say, If a man shall say to his father or mother, It is Corban, that is to say, a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me; he shall be free. 12And ye suffer him no more to do ought for his father or his mother; 13Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered: and many such like things do ye.

Look closely at verse 10. Jesus quotes two commands. "Honour thy father and thy mother" is from the Ten (Exodus 20:12). "Whoso curseth father or mother, let him die the death" is not from the Ten. It is from the writing of Moses.

Exodus 21:17 And he that curseth his father, or his mother, shall surely be put to death.

Jesus introduces both with "Moses said," and He calls both "the commandment of God" (verse 9) and "the word of God" (verse 13). To Jesus, what Moses wrote is what God commanded.

He Ties Belief in Himself to Belief in Moses' Writings

John 5:45–47 45Do not think that I will accuse you to the Father: there is one that accuseth you, even Moses, in whom ye trust. 46For had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed me: for he wrote of me. 47But if ye believe not his writings, how shall ye believe my words?

The Two Greatest Commandments Are in Moses' Writing

Matthew 22:36–40 36Master, which is the great commandment in the law? 37Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. 38This is the first and great commandment. 39And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. 40On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.

Neither of these is in the Ten Commandments. Both come from the books Moses wrote.

Deuteronomy 6:5 And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might.
Leviticus 19:18 Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD.

If everything outside the finger of God was nailed to the cross, then the first and great commandment was nailed to the cross. No one believes that.

Paul Sums Up the Ten With a Command From Moses' Writing

Romans 13:8–10 8Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law. 9For this, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. 10Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law.

Paul uses Leviticus 19:18 as the summary of the Ten. He did not treat the writing of Moses as a lesser, expired category.

"The Law of Moses" Is "the Law of the LORD"

Nehemiah 8:1 And all the people gathered themselves together as one man into the street that was before the water gate; and they spake unto Ezra the scribe to bring the book of the law of Moses, which the LORD had commanded to Israel.
Nehemiah 8:8 So they read in the book in the law of God distinctly, and gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading.
2 Chronicles 34:14 And when they brought out the money that was brought into the house of the LORD, Hilkiah the priest found a book of the law of the LORD given by Moses.
Luke 2:22–24 22And when the days of her purification according to the law of Moses were accomplished, they brought him to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord; 23(As it is written in the law of the Lord, Every male that openeth the womb shall be called holy to the Lord;) 24And to offer a sacrifice according to that which is said in the law of the Lord, A pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons.

Luke calls it "the law of Moses" in verse 22 and "the law of the Lord" in verses 23 and 24. Same law, two names. Moses was the writer; the LORD was the Author.

God Said He Wrote More Than the Tables

Exodus 24:12 And the LORD said unto Moses, Come up to me into the mount, and be there: and I will give thee tables of stone, and a law, and commandments which I have written; that thou mayest teach them.

God speaks of the tables of stone and "a law, and commandments which I have written." God does not draw the line where the finger-only position draws it.

If Only the Tables Remain

Jesus Protected the "Least" Commandments

Matthew 5:17–19 17Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. 18For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled. 19Whosoever therefore shall break one of these least commandments, and shall teach men so, he shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven: but whosoever shall do and teach them, he shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven.

No one would call the Ten Commandments "the least commandments." Jesus is protecting the smallest commands of the law, which means He is speaking about far more than the two tables.

Consider What Would Disappear

If only the words written by the finger of God remain, then none of the following would have any standing, because none of them are in the Ten:

  • Love the LORD with all thine heart (Deuteronomy 6:5)
  • Love thy neighbour as thyself (Leviticus 19:18)
  • The prohibitions against incest (Leviticus 18:6–18)
  • The prohibition against giving children to Molech (Leviticus 18:21)
  • The prohibition against bestiality (Leviticus 18:23)
  • The prohibitions against witchcraft and consulting the dead (Deuteronomy 18:10–12)

A Test Case

The New Testament never names bestiality directly. The only explicit prohibitions in all of Scripture are in the writings of Moses (Exodus 22:19; Leviticus 18:23; Leviticus 20:15–16; Deuteronomy 27:21). Under the finger-only position, that prohibition was nailed to the cross.

And Paul enforced the writing of Moses in the church at Corinth.

1 Corinthians 5:1 It is reported commonly that there is fornication among you, and such fornication as is not so much as named among the Gentiles, that one should have his father's wife.
Leviticus 18:8 The nakedness of thy father's wife shalt thou not uncover: it is thy father's nakedness.

That standard is not in the Ten. It is in the writing of Moses, and Paul treats it as binding on a Gentile congregation after the cross.

The Sabbath Assembly Is in Moses' Writing, Not on the Tables

The fourth commandment on the tables commands the Sabbath rest.

Exodus 20:8–11 8Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy. 9Six days shalt thou labour, and do all thy work: 10But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work, thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, thy manservant, nor thy maidservant, nor thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates: 11For in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that in them is, and rested the seventh day: wherefore the LORD blessed the sabbath day, and hallowed it.

The command that the Sabbath is "an holy convocation," a commanded assembly, is in Leviticus 23:3, which we read in the Introduction. Under the finger-only position, the Sabbath rest remains but the Sabbath assembly was nailed to the cross. Yet Jesus kept that assembly as His custom, and the apostles expected the Gentiles to be in it.

Luke 4:16 And he came to Nazareth, where he had been brought up: and, as his custom was, he went into the synagogue on the sabbath day, and stood up for to read.
Acts 15:21 For Moses of old time hath in every city them that preach him, being read in the synagogues every sabbath day.
Hebrews 10:24–25 24And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: 25Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is; but exhorting one another: and so much the more, as ye see the day approaching.

God Forbade Taking Away From His Words

Deuteronomy 4:2 Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish ought from it, that ye may keep the commandments of the LORD your God which I command you.

Paul Called Moses' Writings Profitable After the Cross

2 Timothy 3:15–17 15And that from a child thou hast known the holy scriptures, which are able to make thee wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus. 16All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: 17That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.

The "holy scriptures" Timothy knew from a child were the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings. Paul, decades after the cross, calls all of it profitable "for instruction in righteousness."

Ephesians 2:15: "The Law of Commandments Contained in Ordinances"

This passage uses the same word dogma as Colossians 2:14, so it often comes up.

Ephesians 2:14–16 14For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us; 15Having abolished in his flesh the enmity, even the law of commandments contained in ordinances; for to make in himself of twain one new man, so making peace; 16And that he might reconcile both unto God in one body by the cross, having slain the enmity thereby:
  • What is abolished is "the enmity." The whole passage is about hostility between Jew and Gentile, and the result is "one new man" at peace, not a man without God's law.
  • The law God gave never built a wall to keep the stranger out. It did the opposite.
Numbers 15:15–16 15One ordinance shall be both for you of the congregation, and also for the stranger that sojourneth with you, an ordinance for ever in your generations: as ye are, so shall the stranger be before the LORD. 16One law and one manner shall be for you, and for the stranger that sojourneth with you.

A "middle wall of partition" did exist in the temple of Paul's day, and it nearly cost Paul his life. He was accused of bringing a Gentile past it, and the Gentile named was from Ephesus, the very city this letter was written to.

Acts 21:28–29 28Crying out, Men of Israel, help: This is the man, that teacheth all men every where against the people, and the law, and this place: and further brought Greeks also into the temple, and hath polluted this holy place. 29(For they had seen before with him in the city Trophimus an Ephesian, whom they supposed that Paul had brought into the temple.)

Paul wrote Ephesians as a prisoner (Ephesians 3:1; 4:1), in the imprisonment that began with this accusation. Josephus, a first-century Jewish historian, describes that barrier.

Josephus · Wars of the Jews, Book 5, Chapter 5, Section 2 (Whiston) "When you go through these [first] cloisters, unto the second [court of the] temple, there was a partition made of stone all round, whose height was three cubits: its construction was very elegant; upon it stood pillars, at equal distances from one another, declaring the law of purity, some in Greek, and some in Roman letters, that 'no foreigner should go within that sanctuary;' for that second [court of the] temple was called 'the Sanctuary;'"
Teacher's Note · The Middle Wall Paul does not explicitly say "the middle wall" is the temple barrier, so treat that identification as a strong historical reading, not a stated fact of the text. The argument does not depend on it. The passage itself says what was abolished was "the enmity," and the law of God itself commanded "one law" for the Israelite and the stranger (Numbers 15:16). A barrier that excluded the stranger on pain of death was built by men, not commanded by Moses.

Deuteronomy 31:26: "A Witness Against Thee"

The book of the law was placed beside the ark as a witness against Israel. Some teach that this book is the "handwriting" nailed to the cross.

Deuteronomy 31:24–26 24And it came to pass, when Moses had made an end of writing the words of this law in a book, until they were finished, 25That Moses commanded the Levites, which bare the ark of the covenant of the LORD, saying, 26Take this book of the law, and put it in the side of the ark of the covenant of the LORD your God, that it may be there for a witness against thee.
  • That book contained the Ten Commandments themselves (Deuteronomy 5:6–21), along with the Shema and the first and great commandment (Deuteronomy 6:4–5). If the whole book was nailed to the cross, then the words of the finger of God recorded in it went with it, and so did the first and great commandment. The finger-only position cannot use this verse without harming itself.
  • What the book was, in verse 26, was a witness against Israel: the testimony of their guilt when they broke the covenant. That is exactly what Colossians 2:14 says was taken away, the record that was "against us." The witness against us was removed. The words God commanded were not.

Galatians 4:10: "Ye Observe Days, and Months, and Times, and Years"

Galatians 4:8–11 8Howbeit then, when ye knew not God, ye did service unto them which by nature are no gods. 9But now, after that ye have known God, or rather are known of God, how turn ye again to the weak and beggarly elements, whereunto ye desire again to be in bondage? 10Ye observe days, and months, and times, and years. 11I am afraid of you, lest I have bestowed upon you labour in vain.

These were Gentiles who formerly served "them which by nature are no gods" (verse 8). Paul says they are turning again to what they came out of. Gentiles cannot turn "again" to the feasts of the LORD, because they never kept them before they knew God. They were returning to the pagan calendar they had left behind. For more on that calendar, see A Warning Against Pagan Holidays.

Romans 14:5: "One Man Esteemeth One Day Above Another"

The dispute in Romans 14 is between those who eat meat and those who eat only herbs (Romans 14:2), over meat that may have been offered to idols, and over private fast days. The Sabbath and the feasts of the LORD are never named in the chapter. See Which Laws Remain for the full treatment.

Summary

The Teaching in Brief

  1. The Moedim are the LORD's feasts. God calls them "my feasts," and the weekly Sabbath heads the list as a holy convocation (Leviticus 23:1–4).
  2. Colossians 2:14 is about forgiveness. The "handwriting" (cheirographon) is the record of our debt, blotted out when our trespasses were forgiven (Colossians 2:13; Acts 3:19).
  3. What was nailed was "against us." God's commandments were given "for thy good" (Deuteronomy 10:13). The record of our guilt was against us.
  4. Paul defines the "ordinances" in the same chapter as "the commandments and doctrines of men": touch not, taste not, handle not (Colossians 2:20–22).
  5. Colossians 2:16 lists the Sabbath with the feasts. If the verse removes the feasts, it removes the fourth commandment too. The finger-only position cannot stand on it.
  6. They "are" a shadow of things "to come." Present tense, written after the cross (Colossians 2:17).
  7. Hebrews names what changed: the priesthood, the carnal commandment of descent, the gifts and sacrifices, and the offering for sin (Hebrews 7:12–18; 9:9–10; 10:18). It never names the Sabbath, the feasts, or the convocations.
  8. Our liberty is in how, not whether. The altar offerings of each feast are fulfilled in Christ. The convocation, the rest, the remembrance, and the rejoicing remain.
  9. Jesus calls Moses' words "the commandment of God" and "the word of God" (Mark 7:9–13), including a command not on the tables (Exodus 21:17).
  10. The two greatest commandments are in Moses' writing (Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18; Matthew 22:36–40), and Paul enforced Moses' writing in a Gentile church (1 Corinthians 5:1; Leviticus 18:8).
  11. Paul kept and taught the feasts after the cross (1 Corinthians 5:8; Acts 20:16; Acts 27:9) and testified that he believed all that was written in the law (Acts 24:14).
  12. The feasts continue into the kingdom (Zechariah 14:16–19; Isaiah 66:23; Luke 22:16).

In One Sentence

The priesthood changed and the sacrifices were fulfilled, because Hebrews says so in its own words; everything else God commanded through Moses still stands, because Jesus called it the commandment of God and no apostle ever said otherwise.
