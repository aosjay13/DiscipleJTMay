A Scriptural Study

The Book of Jasher

What scripture names, and what is sold under that name today

✦

Before We Begin

Of the three books covered in this series, Jasher is the strangest case, because the Bible itself names it. Twice, scripture points a reader to an outside record and says, in effect, you can find more detail there. That has led many people to assume that if they could just get their hands on a copy of the real Book of Jasher, they would be holding a lost piece of scripture.

The truth is more complicated, and in some ways more important to understand than the questions raised by Enoch or Jubilees. The book scripture names is genuinely lost. It has not survived. What circulates today under the title Book of Jasher is not that book at all, and in one widely shared version, it is not even an honest attempt at reconstruction. It is a documented forgery.

This study works through the two references scripture actually makes, what kind of writing the real book likely was, why being named in scripture is not the same as being scripture, and then turns to the harder task of sorting out what is actually being sold as the Book of Jasher today and why none of it should be confused with the ancient, lost original.

Part One

What the Bible Actually Says

Start with the only two places scripture actually mentions this book by name. Everything else in this study builds on these two verses, so it is worth reading them slowly.

Joshua 10:12–13 Then spake Joshua to the LORD in the day when the LORD delivered up the Amorites before the children of Israel, and he said in the sight of Israel, Sun, stand thou still upon Gibeon; and thou, Moon, in the valley of Ajalon. And the sun stood still, and the moon stayed, until the people had avenged themselves upon their enemies. Is not this written in the book of Jasher? So the sun stood still in the midst of heaven, and hasted not to go down about a whole day.
2 Samuel 1:17–18 And David lamented with this lamentation over Saul and over Jonathan his son: (Also he bade them teach the children of Judah the use of the bow: behold, it is written in the book of Jasher.)

Notice what each verse is actually doing. Joshua points a reader toward the book of Jasher as a place where the account of the sun standing still was also recorded, much the way a modern writer might point to a newspaper archive. Second Samuel does something similar, noting that David’s lament, and the instruction to teach Judah the use of the bow, was written down in that same book. In both cases, the book of Jasher is treated as an existing, known record that the original readers could have consulted, not as a source of new revelation being delivered to them.

This is not the only place scripture does this. It names several other records the same way, and none of them are scripture either.

Numbers 21:14 Wherefore it is said in the book of the wars of the LORD, What he did in the Red sea, and in the brooks of Arnon. 1 Kings 11:41 And the rest of the acts of Solomon, and all that he did, and his wisdom, are they not written in the book of the acts of Solomon?

The book of the wars of the LORD and the book of the acts of Solomon are named the same way Jasher is, as records the original readers could go check for themselves. Both are lost today. Neither has ever been claimed as scripture by anyone. Being named by an inspired writer as a real, existing record is simply not the same thing as being inspired scripture, and Jasher fits this same pattern exactly.

Part Two

What Kind of Book Was It

The Hebrew title, Sefer haYashar, means something like the Book of the Upright or the Book of the Just. Based on how it is used in both verses, tied to a poetic account of a great victory in Joshua and to a funeral lament in Samuel, the most likely explanation is that it was a collection of Hebrew songs and poetry, composed to remember great deeds and great men. That would fit naturally alongside the book of the wars of the LORD in Numbers, another lost collection that seems to have served a similar purpose.

This matters because it shapes what kind of loss we are actually talking about. The original book of Jasher was very likely a work of national memory and poetry, not a book of prophecy or law. Nothing in either passage suggests it claimed to speak for God the way the prophets did, or that it functioned as anything other than a historical and poetic record kept by the nation, the very kind of external annal that ancient Israel, like every other ancient nation, would naturally have kept.

Hold This Clearly

Nothing in Joshua or Samuel suggests the book of Jasher ever claimed to be a word from God. It appears to have been a national record of songs and notable deeds, the ancient equivalent of a chronicle or an anthem book, cited the way you might cite a history text today. That is a real and lost piece of ancient Hebrew literature. It was never treated as scripture in its own day, and nothing in the text that names it suggests it should be.

Part Three

Naming a Source Is Not Endorsing It

It is worth pausing here because this principle runs through the whole series. The Book of Enoch involved an inspired writer confirming one true prophetic line out of a much later collection. The Book of Jubilees involved a claim to angelic revelation that scripture gives no support for. The book of Jasher is different from both, and in a sense simpler. Joshua and the writer of Samuel are not quoting Jasher as a source of doctrine or prophecy at all. They are pointing to it the way a historian points to a source, confirming that a real event happened and that a fuller account of it existed elsewhere.

1 Kings 14:19 And the rest of the acts of Jeroboam, how he warred, and how he reigned, behold, they are written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Israel.

Scripture is full of citations like this, pointing to royal chronicles, war records, and other national archives that have not survived. No one reads these citations and concludes that the lost chronicles of the kings of Israel belong in the Bible. The same reasoning applies to Jasher without any need to stretch the point. Being named as a real record that existed is simply not a claim to inspiration, and treating it as one reads far more into these two verses than they actually say.

Part Four

The Book Sold Today Is Not the Book Scripture Names

Here is where the study has to shift from a question of interpretation to a question of plain historical fact. The actual book of Jasher named in Joshua and Samuel has not survived. It is gone, the same way the book of the wars of the LORD is gone. Anything printed today under the title Book of Jasher is, by simple necessity, not that book. What matters is understanding what these later works actually are, since two very different things circulate under the same borrowed name.

Bonus

Two separate works are commonly sold or shared today as "the Book of Jasher," and they should not be confused with each other or with the ancient original.

  • The 1751 English text. Published in England by a printer named Jacob Ilive, claiming to be a translation made centuries earlier by an Anglo-Saxon monk. This claim did not hold up, and the work is understood by historians as a fabrication written to look ancient, not a genuine translation of anything. This is the version most widely shared online today, often without any mention of its actual origin.
  • The Hebrew Sefer haYashar. A real medieval Jewish work, most likely compiled sometime between the eleventh and sixteenth centuries, retelling the narrative of Genesis through Joshua with a great deal of added legend and tradition. It was translated into English in 1840. This one is not a forgery, it is honest medieval Jewish literature, but it is many centuries removed from the events of Joshua and Samuel and was never intended by its own compilers to be the ancient book those verses name.

Neither of these books is the record Joshua or the writer of Samuel pointed their readers toward. One is a documented eighteenth-century fabrication. The other is a genuine but late medieval Jewish retelling of familiar Bible narratives, written more than two thousand years after the events of Joshua and centuries after the close of the New Testament. Calling either one "the lost Book of Jasher" gives it a claim to ancient authority that neither version has earned.

Part Five

Reading the Modern Text with Discernment

Whichever version someone encounters, the same test applies that applies to every writing outside scripture. Read it, if you read it at all, the way you would read any historical novel or legend collection, and measure every claim against what the Bible itself actually says, never the other way around.

1 Thessalonians 5:21 Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.

Both modern versions add a great deal of narrative detail to familiar Genesis and Joshua accounts, extra conversations, extra names, extra events, none of which appear anywhere in scripture. Some of it may echo genuine older Jewish tradition passed down over centuries. Much of it is simply later invention, especially in the 1751 English text, which was built to imitate an ancient style rather than to preserve one. None of it should ever be repeated in teaching as though it carries the same weight as the book of Genesis itself.

Teacher’s Note

If a class member brings up a detail from a modern "Book of Jasher" text, the most helpful response is usually to ask where that detail appears in scripture itself. If it does not appear there, treat it as tradition or legend at best, not as history confirmed by God’s word. This is a gentle way to redirect without dismissing the person’s curiosity, since the honest answer is simply that this text, in either version, was written far too late to serve as a reliable witness to events from the time of Moses and Joshua.

Part Six

Why Neither Version Belongs in the Canon

The case here is, if anything, more straightforward than it was for Enoch or Jubilees. Neither modern version even claims ancient Jewish authorship in a defensible way. The 1751 text has been examined by historians and does not hold up as a genuine ancient translation. The Hebrew Sefer haYashar openly belongs to the medieval period of Jewish literature, well over a thousand years after the close of the Old Testament and centuries after the New Testament was written and recognized by the church.

2 Timothy 3:16–17 All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.

The recognized canon closed with the writings of the apostles and prophets, received and confirmed across the whole believing church, not with a private printing centuries later. A genuine ancient record like the true book of Jasher, had it survived, would still not have been received as scripture, since neither Joshua nor Samuel presents it as a prophetic word from God. A late reconstruction or an outright forgery has even less claim than that. Whatever value either modern text might hold as literature or as a window into how later generations imagined these events, sitting alongside the word of God is not among them.

Part Seven

How to Use This Rightly

The true book of Jasher, the one Joshua and the writer of Samuel actually knew, is lost to us, and that is simply a fact of history to accept rather than a mystery to chase. What has survived under its name today is either a documented forgery or a late medieval retelling, and either one can be read with the same curiosity you might bring to any old book of legend, so long as it is never mistaken for scripture or taught as though it carries scripture’s authority.

Where scripture itself already tells the story, in the sun standing still over Gibeon or in David’s lament for Saul and Jonathan, those accounts stand complete on their own and need nothing added from an outside book to be trustworthy. That has always been sufficient, long before any modern printing ever claimed the name of Jasher for itself.

In Summary

Scripture names the book of Jasher twice, pointing to it as a real record its first readers could have consulted, most likely a collection of songs and remembrances of great deeds. Being named this way is no different from the many other lost national records scripture points to elsewhere, and it has never meant the book itself was inspired.

More importantly, the actual book named in Joshua and Samuel has not survived, and what is sold under its title today, whether the eighteenth-century English forgery or the genuine but late medieval Hebrew retelling, is not that book. Neither one should be confused with the lost original, and neither one should ever stand beside the word of God. What Joshua and Samuel themselves recorded is sufficient, and it is that account, not a borrowed title on a much later book, that we are called to trust.
