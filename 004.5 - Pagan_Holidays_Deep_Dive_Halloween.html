The Origins of Halloween

The Feast of the Dead Across the Ages

✦

Continuation of Part I

Part I named Halloween as the third of the pagan holidays and called it the one that wears its origin most openly, a festival of death, the dead, witchcraft, and divination, every one of which Scripture names an abomination. This deep dive follows that thread back to its source. Of the three great holidays, Halloween is the easiest to trace, because it has never truly hidden what it is. Its root is the Celtic feast of the dead called Samhain, and its customs were carried straight from that worship into our own streets and doorways. What follows shows where it began, how it was practiced, how the church tried to cover it over, and what the word of God says plainly about every part of it.

The Common Root

The Festival of the Dead

Across the fallen nations runs a dark and ancient impulse: to reach out to the dead. Where God commanded His people to seek the living God and to leave the dead in His hands, the nations sought instead to call up the spirits of the departed, to feed them, to appease them, and to learn the future from them. This is the soil out of which Halloween grew. Many peoples kept some festival of the dead, a season when the spirits of the departed were believed to return and had to be welcomed or warded off. These festivals did not all fall at the same time of year, but they carried the same forbidden hope, that the living could commune with the dead.

The table below shows several of these festivals of the dead among the nations. The one that gave us Halloween, its date and its shape, is the Celtic Samhain.

Land or People Festival of the Dead When
The Celts Samhain, the source of Halloween October 31 – November 1
Rome Feralia and Lemuria, to appease the spirits of the dead February and May
Egypt The cult of the dead and the realm of Osiris Through the year
China The Ghost Festival, when the dead return and are fed Seventh lunar month (late summer)
Mexico (later) The Day of the Dead, merged with the Catholic All Souls November 1 – 2
Israel (by God's command) No communing with the dead; seek the living God alone —
A note on the table. These festivals of the dead did not share a single date the way the midwinter and spring festivals clustered at the solstice and equinox. What they shared was the practice God forbade: reaching out to the dead. Samhain alone is the true ancestor of Halloween, giving it both its night and its customs. The others are shown only to make plain that the impulse behind it is found across the nations, exactly as Scripture warns.
The Root

Samhain: The Celtic Feast of the Dead

Some two thousand years ago, the Celtic peoples of Ireland, Britain, and northern France kept a festival they called Samhain, a word meaning "summer's end." It fell at the close of the harvest and the beginning of the dark, cold half of the year, and the Celts reckoned it as their new year, beginning on the first of November. The night before, the thirty-first of October, was the heart of the festival.

The Celts believed that on this night the barrier between the world of the living and the world of the dead grew thin, and that the souls of the dead were set free to roam the earth once more. With them, it was believed, came other spirits, and so the night was charged with dread. Great bonfires were kindled on the hilltops both to honor the dead and to drive off the spirits that were feared. Their priests, the Druids, presided over the rites, and the night was given to divination, for it was held that with the veil between the worlds so thin, the future could best be read and the dead best consulted. Offerings of food were set out to feed and appease the wandering souls. Every part of this festival is precisely what the word of God forbids: seeking the dead, consulting spirits, and reading the future by forbidden means.

The Practices

The Customs of the Night

The customs that fill our streets every October were not invented by toy-makers and candy-sellers. Each of them carries the meaning it was given at Samhain.

Costumes and Disguises

The Celts believed that to walk abroad on this night was to risk meeting the roaming dead and the spirits with them. So they put on disguises and masks, hoping to pass unrecognized among the spirits or to frighten them away. The custom of dressing up on Halloween is the direct descendant of this fear of the dead.

The Jack-o'-Lantern

The carved and lit lantern came from Ireland, where turnips were hollowed out, given grotesque faces, and set with a coal or candle to ward off wandering spirits, tied to the folk tale of a damned soul condemned to wander the earth with a burning coal. When the Irish came to America, they found the pumpkin larger and easier to carve, and the turnip became the pumpkin we know.

Bonfires and Divination

The hilltop fires of Samhain were believed to ward off evil and to honor the dead. The night was also given over to divination, the reading of the future by omens and signs. When Rome held these Celtic lands, the harvest goddess Pomona, whose symbol was the apple, was folded into the season, and from her worship comes the old divination game we now call bobbing for apples.

The Door to Door

Souling and Trick or Treat

The familiar custom of going from door to door has two roots, and both run back to the dead. The older root is the Celtic practice of leaving food out at night to feed and appease the wandering souls, so that they would pass by without harm. The later root grew up in the Middle Ages under the name of souling. On the days the church had set apart for the dead, the poor would go from house to house begging small round breads called soul cakes, and in exchange they promised to pray for the souls of the household's dead. To go souling was to trade a prayer for the dead for a cake.

In time the soul cake became a treat, the prayer for the dead was forgotten, and children took up the rounds wearing the old disguises, which was called guising. From this came the trick or treat of our own day. Whether by the Celtic offering to the spirits or the medieval cake for the dead, the custom was always bound to the dead, and the doorway begging that delights children today began as a transaction with the departed souls.

The Cover

The Church and All Hallows

As the influence of the church spread into the Celtic lands, it did not abolish the feast of the dead. It tried to cover it over. A day to honor all the Christian saints, which had first been kept in May, was moved in the eighth century to the first of November, the very day of Samhain. This was All Hallows' Day, since a hallow is a saint, and so the night before became All Hallows' Eve, which was shortened over time to Halloween. A few generations later, the second of November was set apart as All Souls' Day, a day to pray for the dead. Many hold that these dates were chosen to draw the people away from Samhain by giving them a Christian festival in its place.

The cover did not change the thing beneath it. The old customs of the dead, the bonfires, the costumes, the begging, and the divination, carried straight through the new names and went on as before. When the Irish came to America in great numbers in the nineteenth century, they brought Halloween with them, and from there it spread across the land. In our own day it has shed its Christian dress almost entirely and stands once more as what it always was: a celebration of death, ghosts, witchcraft, and the occult, kept as a high day by those who practice the very witchcraft Samhain was built upon.

The Word of God

What God Says of the Dead

No holiday stands more plainly condemned by Scripture than this one, for its very heart is the thing God names again and again as an abomination. He forbids divination, the reading of the future by omens. He forbids the consulting of familiar spirits and the seeking of the dead. He calls necromancy, the calling up of the dead, an abomination, and He set a penalty of death upon it under the Law. King Saul lost his life and his kingdom for the sin of seeking out a woman with a familiar spirit to call up the dead, and the Scripture records plainly that this was among the reasons the LORD slew him.

More than that, the whole belief on which the festival rests is a lie. The dead do not roam the earth on the last night of October. The Scriptures teach that the dead know not anything, and that the spirit returns to God who gave it. The living are not to seek the dead, but the living God. When the prophet was tempted to send the people to mutterers and to those with familiar spirits, his answer was a rebuke: should not a people seek unto their God? Why turn from the living God to the dead? And when the people at Ephesus turned to Christ, they did not keep their books of dark and curious arts as harmless fun. They brought them out and burned them before all men.

The Verdict

The Customs and the Verdict

When the customs of Halloween are traced to their sources, not one of them is innocent, and not one comes from the word of God. Every one was born in the worship of the dead.

Custom What It Was Where It Came From
October 31 The date of the celebration Samhain, the Celtic feast of the dead
Trick or treat Going door to door for food Offerings to the dead; medieval souling for soul cakes
Costumes Disguises and masks Hiding from or imitating the roaming spirits
The jack-o'-lantern A carved and lit lantern Irish turnip carving to ward off wandering souls
Bobbing for apples A divination game The Roman harvest goddess Pomona; Samhain divination
Bonfires Great hilltop fires Samhain fires to honor the dead and drive off spirits

The verdict needs no straining. Halloween is not a holiday with an innocent meaning that has been twisted; it is a festival of the dead that has been lightly dressed and handed to children. Where Christmas and Easter borrowed the forms of sun worship and fertility worship and set the name of Christ upon them, Halloween was never even given the name of Christ. It honors death, the dead, witchcraft, and divination openly, all of which God calls an abomination. The call of God is clear and sharp: His people are to seek the living God and not the dead, to have no fellowship with the works of darkness, and to come out from among the nations and be separate.
