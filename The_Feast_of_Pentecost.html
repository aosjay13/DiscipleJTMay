The Feast of Pentecost

The Feast of Weeks · Shavuot · The Day of Firstfruits of the Harvest

✦

Foundation

The Feast of Pentecost stands at the very center of God's redemptive calendar. It is the only one of the LORD's appointed times that has no fixed date on the month. Instead it is counted, week by week, from the resurrection of our Lord. On this one day, separated by exactly fifteen centuries, God did two of His greatest works. At Mount Sinai He wrote His Law on tables of stone. In the upper room at Jerusalem He wrote that same Law on the hearts of His people by the Holy Ghost.

As those who keep the commandments of God and hold the faith of Jesus, we study this feast to understand its harvest, its fulfillment, and the way the Father still invites us to gather before Him on the day His Spirit came down.

Introduction

The Names of the Feast and Its Place in the Calendar

Before we open the harvest and the fulfillment, we should settle what this feast is called and where it falls. Scripture gives it several names, and each one tells us something about its purpose.

The Feast of Weeks: in Hebrew this is Shavuot, which simply means "weeks." The name comes from the command to count seven full weeks before keeping the feast. We see this in Deuteronomy 16:10, "And thou shalt keep the feast of weeks unto the LORD thy God."
Pentecost: this is the Greek name used in the New Testament. It means "fiftieth," because the feast falls on the fiftieth day of the count. The Greek-speaking believers of the early church knew the day by this number.
The Feast of Harvest: Exodus 23:16 names it "the feast of harvest, the firstfruits of thy labours, which thou hast sown in the field." This was the celebration of the wheat harvest, the great ingathering of grain.
The Day of the Firstfruits: Numbers 28:26 calls it "the day of the firstfruits, when ye bring a new meat offering unto the LORD, after your weeks be out." Israel brought the first of the wheat harvest to God as an offering of thanksgiving.

A Feast That Is Counted, Not Dated

Every other appointed time in Leviticus 23 is fixed to a day of the month. Passover falls on the fourteenth day of the first month, Trumpets on the first day of the seventh month, and so on. Pentecost is different. It has no date of its own. It must be counted out, day by day, starting from the Feast of Firstfruits during the days of Unleavened Bread.

Leviticus 23:15–16 And ye shall count unto you from the morrow after the sabbath, from the day that ye brought the sheaf of the wave offering; seven sabbaths shall be complete: Even unto the morrow after the seventh sabbath shall ye number fifty days; and ye shall offer a new meat offering unto the LORD.

This counting is often called "counting the omer," after the sheaf of barley that began the count. Here is the heart of why it matters to us. The count begins at Firstfruits, which is the day Jesus rose from the dead. The whole feast is therefore measured out from the resurrection. The day the Spirit fell did not arrive by chance. It was numbered out from the empty tomb, fifty days exactly, so that the gift of the Spirit would be forever tied to the risen Christ.

One of the Three Pilgrimage Feasts

Pentecost is also one of the three feasts at which every man of Israel was required to appear before God in Jerusalem.

Deuteronomy 16:16 Three times in a year shall all thy males appear before the LORD thy God in the place which he shall choose; in the feast of unleavened bread, and in the feast of weeks, and in the feast of tabernacles: and they shall not appear before the LORD empty.

This command explains why Jerusalem was so crowded on the day the Spirit fell. Acts 2 tells us there were devout men out of every nation gathered in the city. They had not come to wait for the Holy Ghost. They had come to keep the Feast of Weeks, just as God commanded. God used the feast itself to bring the harvest of nations into one place to hear the Gospel.

Chapter I

The Old Testament Function: The Harvest Feast

In its first and plainest meaning, Pentecost was a harvest celebration. The barley harvest had begun at Firstfruits, fifty days earlier. Now the larger wheat harvest had come in. The feast was a day of joy and gratitude, when Israel brought the best of the new grain to the LORD and rejoiced in His provision.

The Two Wave Loaves

The central offering of this feast was unlike anything else in the calendar. The priest was to take fine flour from the new wheat and bake it into two loaves of bread, then wave them before the LORD.

Leviticus 23:17 Ye shall bring out of your habitations two wave loaves of two tenth deals: they shall be of fine flour; they shall be baken with leaven; they are the firstfruits unto the LORD.

Two details here are worth careful attention. First, there were two loaves, not one. Second, and most striking, they were to be baked with leaven. This is remarkable, because leaven was strictly forbidden in nearly every other offering. During Unleavened Bread, leaven was to be put out of the house entirely. Yet here, on the harvest feast, God commands that the firstfruit loaves contain leaven. We will see in Chapter III why this single detail opens the door to the meaning of Pentecost in the church.

A Feast of Generosity

The Feast of Weeks was not only about bringing grain to God. It was also about remembering the poor, the stranger, and those with no inheritance of their own. The command to keep the feast came joined to a command to leave part of the harvest for the needy.

Deuteronomy 16:10–12 And thou shalt keep the feast of weeks unto the LORD thy God with a tribute of a freewill offering of thine hand... And thou shalt rejoice before the LORD thy God, thou, and thy son, and thy daughter, and thy manservant, and thy maidservant, and the Levite that is within thy gates, and the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, that are among you... And thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman in Egypt.

The harvest was to overflow toward those who had nothing. The stranger and the widow were named directly and welcomed to the table. This generosity toward the outsider is no small thing, because the day would come when the Gentile stranger would be gathered into the harvest of God by the very Spirit poured out on this feast.

A Holy Convocation and a Day of Rest

Like the weekly Sabbath, Pentecost was a holy convocation, a called assembly, on which no ordinary work was to be done.

Leviticus 23:21 And ye shall proclaim on the selfsame day, that it may be an holy convocation unto you: ye shall do no servile work therein: it shall be a statute for ever in all your dwellings throughout your generations.

God called this a statute for ever. The day itself was set apart as holy, a time to gather, to rest from labor, and to bring the heart before the Father in worship and thanksgiving.

Chapter II

Pentecost and the Giving of the Law at Sinai

There is a long tradition, held among the Jewish people, that the Feast of Weeks marks the day God gave the Law at Mount Sinai. We should be honest that the Torah itself does not state in plain words that Shavuot is the anniversary of Sinai. It is a conclusion drawn from the timing of the journey. Still, when we follow the dates Scripture gives us, the connection is strong and worth teaching with care.

Counting From Egypt to Sinai

Israel left Egypt at Passover. Scripture then tells us when they reached the mountain of God.

Exodus 19:1 In the third month, when the children of Israel were gone forth out of the land of Egypt, the same day came they into the wilderness of Sinai.

From the time they arrived, God spoke to Moses, set bounds around the mountain, and gave the people days to prepare themselves before He descended in fire. When we count from the Passover departure through the arrival in the third month and the days of preparation, we land in the same season as the Feast of Weeks. This is why the tradition arose. Fifty days after Passover, God came down on Sinai in fire, in thunder, and in the sound of a trumpet, and He gave His people His Law.

Two Mountains, One Feast

Here is where the Pronomian heart of this feast shines clearly. On the first Pentecost at Sinai, God wrote His Law on tablets of stone with His own finger. On the Pentecost of Acts 2, God came down again, once more in fire and in a mighty sound, and this time He wrote that same Law on the hearts of His people by His Spirit. The feast did not change its meaning. It deepened it. The Law was not set aside at Acts 2. It was moved from stone to the living heart, exactly as the prophets foretold.

Jeremiah 31:33 But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the LORD, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people.
Ezekiel 36:26–27 A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you... And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them.

Notice what the Spirit is given to do. He is given to cause us to walk in God's statutes and to keep His judgments. The outpouring of the Spirit at Pentecost is not the end of obedience. It is the power for obedience. This is the entire promise of the new covenant, and it is sealed on the very feast that first delivered the Law.

The Heart of the Feast

At Sinai, the Law on stone. At Acts 2, the Law on the heart. The same God, the same fire, the same feast, the same Law. The Spirit was poured out not to free us from the commandments, but to write them within us and to give us the power to walk in them.

The Letter and the Spirit

There is also a sober parallel that has been observed in this feast for centuries. At Sinai, when the people broke the covenant with the golden calf, about three thousand men fell that day under judgment.

Exodus 32:28 And the children of Levi did according to the word of Moses: and there fell of the people that day about three thousand men.

On the Pentecost of Acts 2, when Peter preached the risen Christ, about three thousand souls were saved that day and added to the church.

Acts 2:41 Then they that gladly received his word were baptized: and the same day there were added unto them about three thousand souls.

We should hold this parallel as a meaningful echo rather than as the central argument of the text, since Scripture does not draw the line for us in so many words. Yet the apostle Paul gives us the principle that makes the echo ring true. He wrote in 2 Corinthians 3:6 that God "hath made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life." The Law received only as cold letters condemns. The same Law, written by the Spirit on a living heart, gives life.

Chapter III

The New Testament Fulfillment: The Spirit Poured Out

We now come to the great day recorded in Acts 2. The disciples were not gathered to invent a new holiday. They were keeping the Feast of Weeks, obedient to the command, waiting in Jerusalem as Jesus had told them to wait. The text is plain about the timing.

Acts 2:1–4 And when the day of Pentecost was fully come, they were all with one accord in one place. And suddenly there came a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind, and it filled all the house where they were sitting. And there appeared unto them cloven tongues like as of fire, and it sat upon each of them. And they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and began to speak with other tongues, as the Spirit gave them utterance.

The same signs that marked Sinai appear again. There was a mighty sound from heaven, as there had been thunder on the mountain. There was fire, as the mountain had burned with fire. But now the fire did not rest on a peak that no man could touch. It rested on each believer. The presence of God that once stayed at a distance now came to dwell inside His people.

The Firstfruits of the Harvest of Souls

Remember that Pentecost was the harvest feast, the day of the firstfruits of the wheat. In Acts 2 the true harvest began. The grain being gathered was no longer wheat from the field but souls from the nations. The three thousand saved that day were the firstfruits of the great ingathering that continues to this hour. The feast that celebrated grain now celebrated the gathering of people into the kingdom of God.

Paul uses this very language of the Spirit Himself.

Romans 8:23 And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.

The Holy Ghost given at Pentecost is called the firstfruits, a down payment and a foretaste of the full harvest of redemption still to come. Just as the wheat firstfruits promised a greater harvest to follow, the Spirit in us promises the full glory that is coming.

The Mystery of the Two Loaves Revealed

Now we return to the strange command we noted in Chapter I. The firstfruit offering of Pentecost was two loaves, and they were baked with leaven. This detail is no accident, and it speaks directly to what God did in the church.

  • Two loaves, two peoples: the church that the Spirit formed was made of two peoples brought into one body, the believing Jew and the believing Gentile. Paul says in Ephesians 2 that Christ has made "of twain one new man." The two loaves picture these two coming together as one firstfruit offering before God.
  • Baked with leaven: in many offerings leaven pictured sin and was forbidden. Yet the people God gathers into His church are still being sanctified. We are not yet sinless. The leaven in the loaves shows that God receives a people who are real, who are still being cleansed, and who are made acceptable not by their own perfection but by His grace. The loaves were waved and accepted, leaven and all, because they were covered by the offerings that accompanied them.

This is a beautiful picture of grace. God did not wait for a flawless harvest before He would receive His people. He gathered the firstfruits as they were, two peoples made one, still carrying the leaven of the flesh, and He accepted them in love.

The Promise of the Spirit and the Walk That Follows

This outpouring was not a one-time event for the apostles only. Peter declared it was for all who would come.

Acts 2:38–39 Then Peter said unto them, Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost. For the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the LORD our God shall call.

We receive the Holy Ghost when we are saved, for the Spirit seals every believer. Scripture also shows a further filling, a baptism in the Holy Ghost, that came upon the disciples on this day and continued in the early church. The household of Cornelius received the Spirit and spoke with tongues, which shows us the pattern is the same gift Peter promised. The same Spirit who fell at Pentecost is still given to all whom the LORD our God shall call.

Above all, we must keep the purpose of the gift in view. The Spirit was given so that the Law written on the heart would become a life actually lived. Jesus joined love for Him directly to obedience.

John 14:15–17 If ye love me, keep my commandments. And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever; Even the Spirit of truth.

The promise of the Comforter sits in the very same breath as the call to keep His commandments. The two belong together. The Spirit is the power by which the commandment becomes a delight rather than a burden.

Chapter IV

How We Keep Pentecost Now in the Faith of Jesus

We have seen the harvest, the Law at Sinai, and the Spirit poured out. The question that remains is the most practical one. If we keep the commandments of God and hold the faith of Jesus, how should we keep this feast today? We answer it the same way we answer every appointed time. We separate the day itself, which remains, from the temple sacrifices, which were fulfilled in Christ.

What Was Fulfilled and What Remains

The wave loaves, the animal offerings, and the grain and drink offerings of the temple were the ceremonial works attached to the feast. These were shadows pointing to Christ and to the gathering of His people. When Jesus offered Himself and when the Spirit was poured out, the substance arrived, and we no longer bring loaves and bullocks to an altar in Jerusalem.

Yet the day itself was called a statute for ever and a holy convocation. The appointed time was never a sacrifice. It was a meeting with God. That meeting still stands. We are not commanded to abolish the day. We are invited to keep it in spirit and in truth, freed from the old ceremonies and filled with their fulfillment.

The Pronomian Principle

We do not keep Pentecost to earn salvation, for salvation is by grace through faith alone. We do not keep it by the blood of animals, for the sacrifices were fulfilled in Christ. We keep the day because it is God's appointed time, set apart for ever, now overflowing with the meaning the Spirit poured into it.

Practical Ways to Keep the Feast

  • Gather as a holy convocation: the day was given as a called assembly. We honor it by coming together as the body of Christ to worship, to rest from ordinary labor, and to set the day apart unto the LORD.
  • Celebrate the outpouring of the Spirit: Pentecost is the birthday of the church and the day the Comforter came. We rejoice in the gift of the Holy Ghost, we seek to be filled afresh, and we welcome His gifts among us for the building up of the body.
  • Read the Word together: since this is the feast of the Law given and the Law written on the heart, it is fitting to read the Scriptures, to honor the commandments of God, and to remember that the Spirit was given to help us walk in them.
  • Give to the harvest of souls: the feast was always about the firstfruits of harvest. We keep its heart by laboring for the harvest of the lost, by evangelism, by supporting the spread of the Gospel, and by going out to gather what God is bringing in.
  • Practice generosity: the old command joined the feast to remembering the poor, the widow, the fatherless, and the stranger. We keep that spirit by giving freely to those in need and by opening the table to the outsider, as God opened His harvest to the nations.
  • Examine the heart for leaven: the loaves carried leaven, a reminder that we are still being sanctified. The feast is a time to confess what remains in us, to lean on grace, and to yield more fully to the Spirit who is conforming us to Christ.

A Day of Joy, Not Bondage

We keep this feast the way a child keeps a father's birthday, not under threat, but in love. There is no fear of falling short of a ceremony, because the ceremony is fulfilled. There is only the joy of remembering what God did, of celebrating the Spirit who lives in us, and of looking for the full harvest still to come. This is the freedom of the faith of Jesus married to the obedience of the commandments of God.

Chapter V

A Brief Word on Early Church and Rabbinic Traditions

These traditions are not the foundation of our teaching, and we hold them loosely. They are offered here only as a brief witness to how this feast was kept and understood by those who came before us. We weigh them by Scripture and keep what agrees with the Word.

Bonus · Rabbinic Traditions

Among the Jewish people the Feast of Weeks gathered several customs over the centuries, especially after the temple was destroyed and the sacrifices ceased.

  • The night of study: a tradition arose of staying awake through the night of the feast to read and study the Scriptures, in honor of the giving of the Law at Sinai. The thought behind it is that the people should be eager and ready to receive God's Word, not sleeping as Israel reportedly slept before the LORD descended on the mountain.
  • Reading the book of Ruth: it became custom to read the book of Ruth on this feast. Ruth was a harvest story, set in the days of the barley and wheat harvest, and it tells of a Gentile woman gathered into the people of God. For believers this points beautifully to the Gentiles brought into the harvest by the Spirit.
  • The Sinai theme: the rabbis tied the feast firmly to the giving of the Law, calling it the season of the giving of the Torah. As we noted in Chapter II, the Torah does not state this in plain words, yet the timing supports it, and the theme rightly honors the Law that God gave.

Bonus · Early Church Traditions

The early Christians kept Pentecost as one of the chief days of the year, second only to the celebration of the resurrection.

  • A season of rejoicing: the early church counted the fifty days from the resurrection to Pentecost as one long season of joy. Writers of the second and third centuries describe these days as a time when believers did not fast but rejoiced, remembering both the risen Lord and the coming of the Spirit.
  • The birthday of the church: the early Christians understood Acts 2 as the day the church was born in power, when the Spirit came and the Gospel went out to the nations. They saw the feast as the fulfillment of the harvest, the day God began to gather the world to Himself.
  • Sinai and the Spirit: some early teachers drew the same connection we have drawn, that the Law given at Sinai and the Spirit given at Pentecost belong to the same feast, the one preparing the way for the other. They saw the Spirit as the One who finally enables God's people to keep what the Law requires.

We receive what is true in these witnesses and lay aside the rest. Our anchor is not the tradition of men but the Word of God, which calls us to keep the commandments of God and the faith of Jesus, in the power of the Spirit poured out on this very feast.
