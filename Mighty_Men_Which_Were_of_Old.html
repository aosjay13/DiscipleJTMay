Mighty Men Which Were of Old

The Men of Renown, Nimrod, Semiramis, and the Origins of the Pagan Gods

✦

Preamble

Genesis 6:4 speaks of "mighty men which were of old, men of renown." Moses wrote as if his readers already knew the stories about these men. They did. Every ancient nation had them: the demigods of Greece, the sages and flood heroes of Babylon, the god-kings of Egypt, the giants of the North. This study follows those stories from the days before the Flood, through Nimrod and Babel, through Nineveh and Babylon, and out into the Egyptian, Greek, Roman, and Norse worlds, in the order they happened.

Along the way we meet Ninus, Semiramis, Menon, the Queen of Heaven, Tammuz, El, Ba'al, Thor, and the rest. We also answer the question that matters most: where did all these gods come from, and how does the God of Scripture stand against them? The Bible gives its own answer in Romans 1 and Deuteronomy 32, and the ancient record, read honestly, confirms it.

A lot of popular teaching on this subject mixes solid evidence with guesses. When a critic finds one of the guesses, he throws out the whole thing, including the parts that are true. This study is built so that can't happen. Every claim is labeled by the kind of source that stands behind it, and the famous claims that don't hold up are named openly in Section XX. The truth doesn't need any help from legends.

How to Read This Study: The Five Kinds of Evidence

Each major claim carries one of these labels. They are listed from strongest to weakest.

Scripture The inspired Word of God. The final authority for faith and practice (2 Timothy 3:16-17). Where Scripture speaks, the question is settled.
Artifact What the ancient peoples wrote themselves. Cuneiform tablets, inscriptions, temple ruins, and other archaeology from the time and place in question.
Classical Greek and Roman writers. Herodotus, Ctesias, Diodorus, Ovid, and others. Often valuable, but usually written centuries after the events, and sometimes pure legend.
Tradition Later Jewish, Christian, and medieval writers. Josephus, the Talmud, the church fathers, the chroniclers, and the Norse Eddas. They show how people understood things. They are witnesses, not inspired sources.
Theory Modern proposals. Scholars' identifications and reconstructions, including 19th-century writers like Alexander Hislop. These are hypotheses to test, not facts to repeat.

A note on dates: Dates for ancient records follow the conventional archaeological timeline and are marked "c." (about). Dates before 1000 BC are approximate, and Bible-believing scholars disagree about how to date the Flood and Babel. Nothing in this study depends on settling that question. Biblical events are presented in the Bible's own order.

I.

Before the Flood: The Sons of God and the Men of Renown

Every pagan mythology in this study looks back to an age when heaven and earth mixed, when divine beings walked among men and fathered heroes. Scripture tells us plainly that something like this really happened, and it tells us what it was: rebellion. The Bible's account is short, sober, and placed right before the judgment of the Flood.

Scriptural References

Genesis 6:1-2 And it came to pass, when men began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters were born unto them, That the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair; and they took them wives of all which they chose.
Genesis 6:4 There were giants in the earth in those days; and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bare children to them, the same became mighty men which were of old, men of renown.
Genesis 6:11 The earth also was corrupt before God, and the earth was filled with violence.

The Words

Three Hebrew Terms in Genesis 6:4

Scripture Nephilim ("giants")

From the root naphal, "to fall." The word means "fallen ones" or "those who fall upon others." The Greek Septuagint, translated by Jews about 250 years before Christ, rendered it gigantes, "giants," which is why the King James reads "giants." Genesis says they were there "in those days; and also after that," and Numbers 13:33 names the sons of Anak in Canaan as coming "of the giants" (the same word, Nephilim).

Scripture Gibborim ("mighty men")

Warriors, heroes, champions. Hold on to this word. It comes back in Genesis 10:8, where Nimrod "began to be a mighty one," a gibbor, in the earth.

Scripture Anshei ha-shem ("men of renown")

Literally "men of the name." These were famous men, remembered by name. The phrase shows up again in Numbers 16:2 for the 250 princes who joined Korah's rebellion, "famous in the congregation, men of renown." In Scripture, making a name for yourself apart from God is never a compliment. Watch how the word name runs through the rest of this study.

"Which were of old" (Hebrew me'olam, "from antiquity") tells us Moses is pointing at figures his readers already knew from old stories. In effect, Genesis says: the heroes the nations sing about came from here, and this is what they really were.

Who Were the "Sons of God"?

Bible believers hold two main views, and a thorough study should state both fairly.

Scripture The angelic view. Everywhere else in the Old Testament, "sons of God" (bene ha-elohim) means heavenly beings: Job 1:6, 2:1, and 38:7, where "all the sons of God shouted for joy" at creation, before man existed. Jude 6-7 and 2 Peter 2:4-5 speak of angels who "left their own habitation" and sinned, and both passages place that sin right next to the Flood. This explains why the offspring were unusual. It is also the oldest known interpretation.
Tradition Who held the angelic view. 1 Enoch 6-7, Jubilees 5, Philo, and Josephus, who wrote that "many angels of God accompanied with women, and begat sons that proved unjust," and compared them directly to "those whom the Grecians call giants" (Antiquities 1.73). Early church writers such as Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, and Tertullian held it too.
Tradition The Sethite view. The godly line of Seth intermarried with the ungodly line of Cain. This view was popularized by Julius Africanus in the 3rd century and by Augustine (City of God 15.23). Its strength is that it avoids the question of angels and human women. Its weakness is that it doesn't explain why the children became Nephilim and mighty men, and it gives "sons of God" a meaning it has nowhere else in the Old Testament.

Either way, the lesson for this study is the same. Whatever happened produced a race of famous, violent heroes, and the nations never forgot them.

The New Testament Commentary

Jude 6 And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day.
2 Peter 2:4-5 For if God spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment; And spared not the old world, but saved Noah the eighth person, a preacher of righteousness, bringing in the flood upon the world of the ungodly.

✦ Bonus: Peter's Word for "Hell"

In 2 Peter 2:4, "cast them down to hell" is one Greek verb, tartaroō: "to cast into Tartarus." In Greek mythology, Tartarus was the pit below the underworld where Zeus chained the Titans, the elder gods who had rebelled. Peter takes the pagans' own word for the prison of the rebel gods and uses it for the place where the real rebels are held. He isn't borrowing their theology. He's telling Greek readers that the truth behind their Titan stories is angels who sinned and are now in chains.

The Pagan Memories

How the Nations Remembered the Age Before the Flood

Artifact Babylon: The Antediluvian Kings

The Sumerian King List (copies from about 1900-1700 BC) opens: "After kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridu." It lists eight kings in five cities who reigned a combined 241,200 years, then says, "Then the flood swept over." The long reigns are wildly inflated, but the shape matches Genesis 5: a line of long-lived rulers, then the Flood. One of those pre-flood kings is "Dumuzi the shepherd," the figure later worshipped as Tammuz (Section IX).

Artifact Babylon: The Apkallu, Sages from the Sea

Mesopotamian texts tell of seven apkallu, semi-divine sages sent by the god Enki before the Flood to teach mankind writing, law, building, and the arts. The first, Uanna, is the "Oannes" of the Babylonian priest Berossus: a fish-man who came up out of the sea. Later texts describe sages after the Flood who were part human, and one is called only "two-thirds apkallu."

Theory Some scholars (for example Amar Annus, 2010) argue that the Watchers of 1 Enoch are a Jewish answer to the apkallu. What Babylon celebrated as heavenly wisdom given by divine sages, Enoch describes as forbidden knowledge taught by fallen angels: weapons, sorcery, enchantments, and astrology (1 Enoch 8).

Tradition The Jewish Memory: Watchers and Giants

1 Enoch (sections from the 3rd century BC or earlier) says 200 Watchers came down on Mount Hermon "in the days of Jared" and took wives, and their children were giants who devoured the earth. Jude 14-15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9, so the apostles knew the book, though it is not Scripture. The Dead Sea Scrolls include a related Book of Giants, and one of the wicked giants it names is Gilgamesh, the most famous hero of Mesopotamia. Another is Hobabish, likely Humbaba, the monster from the Gilgamesh epic. Jews before Christ looked at Babylon's greatest "man of renown" and identified him as one of the Nephilim.

Classical Greece: Titans, Giants, and Demigods

Hesiod (about 700 BC) tells of the Titans, children of Heaven and Earth, who warred against Zeus and were cast into Tartarus. In Works and Days he lists five races of men. The fourth is "a godlike race of hero-men who are called demigods" (hemitheoi), the heroes who fought at Thebes and Troy. Homer uses the same word once for the warriors at Troy. That is the Greek memory of "mighty men which were of old."

Classical Egypt: Gods, Then Demigods, Then Men

Manetho, an Egyptian priest writing in Greek around 280 BC, said Egypt was ruled first by gods, then by demigods and "spirits of the dead," and only then by mortal kings. The older Turin King List (13th century BC) also puts divine rulers before human ones.

Tradition The North: Giants Before the Gods

In the Norse sources, the giants (jötnar) came first, born from the primeval giant Ymir. The gods came later and intermarried with them. Odin's mother, Bestla, was a giantess. So was Thor's mother, Jörð, whose name means "Earth." Norse myth remembers a world where the divine and the monstrous bred together.

Weighing the Evidence

The nations agree that there was an age when divine beings mingled with humans and produced famous heroes, and that this age ended in catastrophe. The pagans remembered it with admiration. Scripture remembers it as corruption and violence that grieved God (Genesis 6:5-7). Same event, two opposite judgments. Keep that pattern in mind, because it repeats in every article that follows.

II.

The Flood Remembered by the Nations

If the Flood happened, we would expect Noah's descendants to carry the memory everywhere they went, and we would expect the memory to get bent as they turned away from God. That is exactly what we find. The Mesopotamian versions are close enough to Genesis that no serious scholar denies they are related. The question is which account is the pure one.

Scriptural References

Genesis 6:5 And GOD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually.
Genesis 8:4 And the ark rested in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, upon the mountains of Ararat.
Matthew 24:37 But as the days of Noe were, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be.

Mesopotamia

The Babylonian Flood Heroes

Artifact Ziusudra: The Sumerian Flood Story

A tablet from Nippur (about 1600 BC), often called the Eridu Genesis, tells how Ziusudra, king of Shuruppak, was warned by a god, built a great boat, and rode out a flood of seven days and seven nights. Afterward he was granted eternal life in the land of Dilmun.

Artifact Atrahasis: The Gods Who Could Not Sleep

In this Old Babylonian epic (a copy dates to about 1640 BC), the gods create humans to do their labor. Humans multiply and make so much noise that the chief god Enlil can't sleep, so he sends plague, drought, and finally a flood. The god Enki secretly warns Atrahasis through a reed wall. When the flood ends, the gods are starving, because with humanity gone, nobody was feeding them sacrifices.

Artifact Utnapishtim: Gilgamesh, Tablet XI

The best-known version was found in the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. George Smith announced its translation in London in 1872 and caused a sensation. Utnapishtim builds a boat, loads it with his family and animals, and survives a storm so terrible that "the gods cowered like dogs." The boat lands on Mount Nimush. He sends out a dove, a swallow, and a raven. When he offers sacrifice, "the gods smelled the sweet savor; the gods gathered like flies over the sacrificer." Compare Genesis 8:21, "And the LORD smelled a sweet savour."

Classical Xisuthros: Berossus the Priest

Berossus, a priest of Babylon writing in Greek around 280 BC, called the flood hero Xisuthros and made him the tenth king from the beginning. Genesis 5 makes Noah the tenth generation from Adam. Berossus said the ship's remains were still in the mountains of Armenia and that people scraped pitch from it to use as charms. Josephus quotes him (Antiquities 1.93).

Which Came First?

Critics say Genesis borrowed from Babylon because the Babylonian tablets are older copies. But the age of a surviving copy doesn't tell you which story is original. The pagan versions are full of gods who are petty, frightened, and hungry. Genesis has none of that. A pure account corrupted by polytheism is a far better explanation than a polytheist myth somehow cleaned up into Genesis. Both sides agree the stories share a common root. Scripture tells us what that root was: a real judgment on a real world (2 Peter 3:6).

The Variants

Egypt, Greece, Rome, and the North

Classical Greece: Deucalion and Pyrrha

Zeus destroys the wicked bronze race with a flood. Deucalion, son of Prometheus, builds a chest (larnax) with his wife Pyrrha, floats nine days and nights, lands on Mount Parnassus, and offers sacrifice. The couple then repopulate the earth by throwing stones over their shoulders (Apollodorus, Library 1.7.2). Prometheus was the son of the Titan Iapetus, which matters in Section III.

Classical Syria: The Animals Came in Pairs

Lucian of Samosata (2nd century AD), describing the great temple at Hierapolis in Syria, recorded the local version of Deucalion's flood. In it the animals came to Deucalion in pairs and he took them aboard, and the waters drained away through a chasm under the temple. That is closer to Genesis than any other Greek version. Lucian also reports that some said the temple had been founded by Semiramis in honor of her mother, the goddess Derceto (Section VII).

Classical Egypt: "You Greeks Are Always Children"

The Egyptians had no great flood story of their own, because for them the yearly Nile flood was a blessing. But Plato's Timaeus (22b) has an Egyptian priest at Sais tell the Greek lawgiver Solon that the Greeks are like children who remember only one deluge, when there had been many destructions by fire and water. Egypt's closest parallel is the Book of the Heavenly Cow, painted in the tomb of Seti I: mankind plots against the sun god Ra, who sends his Eye (as the lion goddess Sekhmet) to slaughter them, then relents and floods the fields with red-dyed beer so she drinks it and stops. Judgment on a rebellious mankind, then mercy. The theme survives even where the water doesn't.

Classical Rome: Ovid's Flood

Ovid's Metamorphoses (AD 8), book 1, tells the Greek story in Latin. Jupiter, angered by human wickedness after the ages of gold, silver, bronze, and iron, floods the earth. Only Deucalion and Pyrrha survive on Parnassus, because they alone were righteous and feared the gods.

Tradition The North: A Flood of Blood

In Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda (about 1220), Odin and his brothers kill the giant Ymir, and so much blood pours out that every frost giant drowns except Bergelmir, who escapes with his wife in a lúðr (the word is usually translated "boat" or "ark," though its exact meaning is debated). All later giants come from them. Snorri, a Christian, also opened his book with the real Flood of Noah (Section XIX).

Flood stories are also found in India (Manu and the fish), China, the Americas, and the Pacific. Not every one of them is a memory of Noah; some may reflect local floods. But the Near Eastern versions are clearly related to Genesis, and they are the ones this study follows.

III.

The Table of Nations: Where the Peoples and Their Gods Came From

Genesis 10 lists seventy nations descended from Noah's three sons. It is the Bible's map of the ancient world, and it tells us which peoples carried which traditions. Every culture in this study appears on it.

Scriptural References

Genesis 10:32 These are the families of the sons of Noah, after their generations, in their nations: and by these were the nations divided in the earth after the flood.
Acts 17:26 And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth.

The Three Streams

Noah's Sons and the Nations of This Study

Scripture Ham: Cush, Mizraim, Phut, and Canaan (Genesis 10:6)

Mizraim is the Hebrew name for Egypt; Egyptians still call their country Misr in Arabic. Canaan fathered the Sidonians, Hittites, Jebusites, and the other peoples of the land (10:15-19), the worshippers of El and Ba'al. Cush usually means the land south of Egypt, but Cush is also named as the father of Nimrod, who founded kingdoms in Mesopotamia. That puzzle is part of Section IV.

Scripture Shem: Elam, Asshur, Arphaxad, Lud, and Aram (Genesis 10:22)

Asshur is Assyria. In the Assyrians' own records, Ashur was the name of their oldest city, their land, and their chief god, all at once. Arphaxad leads to Eber and Abraham. Aram is the Arameans of Syria. Nineveh would be built in Shem's territory, which is why Genesis 10:11 is so striking.

Scripture Japheth: Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, Tiras (Genesis 10:2)

Javan is the Hebrew name for the Ionian Greeks; the Assyrians called them by the same name. Madai is the Medes, and Gomer is usually identified with the Cimmerians.

Theory For centuries readers have noticed that the Greeks' own ancestor figure, the Titan Iapetus (father of Prometheus and grandfather of Deucalion, the Greek Noah), has a name very close to Japheth. It's a suggestive echo, not a proof. But it fits the pattern: the Greeks, descended from Japheth, remembered a forefather of that name as a Titan.

Genesis 10:25 adds that in the days of Peleg "was the earth divided." Many interpreters connect this with the scattering at Babel in Genesis 11.

✦ Bonus: The Nations and the "Sons of God"

Deuteronomy 32:8 says that when the Most High divided the nations, "he set the bounds of the people according to the number of the children of Israel." That is the reading of the traditional Hebrew text. A Dead Sea Scrolls copy of Deuteronomy (4QDeutj) reads "according to the number of the sons of God," and the Greek Septuagint reads "angels of God." Theory Some Bible scholars read this to mean the nations were placed under spiritual powers who then became the objects of their worship, while Israel stayed the Lord's own portion (32:9). Deuteronomy 4:19 and 32:17 and the "prince of Persia" in Daniel 10:13 fit that reading. Interestingly, the Canaanite texts from Ugarit speak of the "seventy sons" of the goddess Asherah, while Genesis 10 lists seventy nations. Whatever one concludes, verse 17 of the same chapter settles what those powers are: "They sacrificed unto devils, not to God."

IV.

Nimrod, the First Mighty One After the Flood

Nimrod is named only four times in the whole Bible, yet he casts one of the longest shadows in history. Jewish and Christian writers have spent two thousand years expanding his story. To teach him accurately, start with exactly what the text says, then look at what tradition added, then look at who historians think he might have been.

Every Mention of Nimrod in Scripture

Genesis 10:8-12 And Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be a mighty one in the earth. He was a mighty hunter before the LORD: wherefore it is said, Even as Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD. And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar. Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh, and the city Rehoboth, and Calah, And Resen between Nineveh and Calah: the same is a great city.
1 Chronicles 1:10 And Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be mighty upon the earth.
Micah 5:6 And they shall waste the land of Assyria with the sword, and the land of Nimrod in the entrances thereof.

What the Text Says

Reading Genesis 10:8-12 Closely

Scripture "He began to be a mighty one"

The word is gibbor, the same word as the "mighty men" of Genesis 6:4. Nimrod is the first person after the Flood to take up the role of the pre-flood heroes. The Septuagint makes the link even sharper: "He began to be a giant upon the earth. He was a giant hunter before the Lord God." Philo of Alexandria, in the first century, called Nimrod a giant who opposed God.

Notice also the word "began" (hechel). Genesis uses it to mark new starts: men "began to multiply" before the Flood (6:1), and at Babel God says, "this they begin to do" (11:6). Nimrod's beginning is the beginning of something.

Scripture "A mighty hunter before the LORD"

The Hebrew lifnei YHWH can mean "in the sight of the LORD" or "in the face of the LORD," as in defiance. The Septuagint used enantion, which Augustine read as "against the Lord" (City of God 16.4). Josephus, the Targums, and the Talmud all read it negatively. Some modern commentators read it neutrally, as a proverb for a great hunter.

Artifact In Mesopotamia, hunting was royal propaganda. Assyrian kings covered their palace walls with scenes of themselves killing lions; the famous lion-hunt reliefs of Ashurbanipal came from Nineveh itself. A "mighty hunter" was a king who claimed to rule the wild by his own strength.

Scripture His Kingdom

Babel is Babylon. Erech is Uruk (modern Warka), one of the world's first true cities, home of Gilgamesh and of the great temple of the goddess Inanna (Section VIII). Accad is Akkad, capital of Sargon's empire, a city still not located by archaeologists. Calneh is uncertain; some read the Hebrew as "all of them" rather than a place name.

Verse 11 can be translated two ways: "Out of that land went forth Asshur" (King James), or "From that land he went forth into Assyria" (the King James margin and many modern versions). The Hebrew allows both. Micah 5:6 calls Assyria "the land of Nimrod," which supports the second reading: Nimrod himself moved north and built Nineveh, Rehoboth-Ir, Calah, and Resen.

Scripture Theory The Name

In Hebrew, "Nimrod" sounds like "we will rebel" (from marad, "to rebel"). Whether that was his actual name or a Hebrew play on a foreign name, the sound was not lost on Jewish readers. The Talmud says he was called Nimrod because "he made the whole world rebel" (Pesachim 94b). Some historians think the name echoes Ninurta, the Mesopotamian god of war and hunting. Ninurta's great temple stood at Calah, one of Nimrod's cities in Genesis 10:11. The ruins of Calah are called Nimrud to this day.

What Tradition Added

Nimrod in Jewish and Christian Writings

Tradition Josephus (about AD 94)

Josephus (Antiquities 1.113-119) says Nimrod, grandson of Ham, was bold and strong. He persuaded people that their happiness came from their own courage and not from God, and he gradually turned the government into a tyranny. He said he would build a tower too high for water to reach if God flooded the world again, and so "avenge himself on God for destroying their forefathers." In Josephus, Nimrod is the mastermind of Babel.

Tradition The Rabbis

The Talmud identifies Nimrod with Amraphel, the king of Shinar in Genesis 14:1 (Eruvin 53a). The midrash Genesis Rabbah (38:13) tells how Nimrod threw Abraham into a fiery furnace for refusing to worship fire, and God delivered him. Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer (chapter 24) says Nimrod wore the garments of skin God made for Adam, which gave him power over the animals. The Book of Jasher that circulates today, first printed in 1625, expands all of this further. It is not the lost Book of Jasher mentioned in Joshua 10:13.

These traditions are old and interesting, and they show how seriously the ancients took Nimrod's rebellion. But they aren't Scripture, they often contradict each other, and many were written more than two thousand years after the events. Teach them as "the rabbis said," never as "the Bible says."

Who Was Nimrod in History?

Theory No inscription names a king "Nimrod." Scholars have proposed several candidates. Each has strengths; none is proven.

Gilgamesh (king of Uruk, about 2700 BC): hero, hunter, builder of Uruk's walls. Uruk is Erech. Older scholars favored him.
Enmerkar (king of Uruk, before Gilgamesh): Sumerian tradition says he built Uruk, and a poem about him contains a story of the confusion of tongues (Section V). Proposed by David Rohl.
Sargon of Akkad (about 2334-2279 BC): founded Akkad (Accad), conquered all of Sumer, and extended his power north to Assyria. Argued in detail by Douglas Petrovich in the Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society (2013).
Naram-Sin (Sargon's grandson, about 2254-2218 BC): the first Mesopotamian king to be declared a god in his own lifetime. His Victory Stele shows him wearing the horned helmet of a god.
Tukulti-Ninurta I (about 1243-1207 BC): the first king to rule both Assyria and Babylon. Proposed by E. A. Speiser (1958). His name contains "Ninurta."
The god Ninurta, or Marduk: some have argued Nimrod is a god remembered as a man. This view runs against the plain sense of Genesis, which presents Nimrod as a real person with a real father.

✦ Bonus: The King Who Made Himself a God

A statue base found at Bassetki in northern Iraq records that after Naram-Sin saved his city, the citizens asked the great gods to make him "the god of their city," and they built him a temple in Akkad. From then on his name was written with the sign for a deity. Whether or not Naram-Sin was Nimrod, here in stone is the exact thing Genesis warns about: a mighty one in the earth, a man of renown, turned into a god. The ancient world did this again and again, and the gods of the nations grew out of it.

Weighing the Evidence

Anyone who tells you history has proven who Nimrod was is overstating the case. What Scripture gives us is enough: the first "mighty one" after the Flood, a hunter-king whose name became a proverb, founder of Babylon's cities and Assyria's, whose land was still called by his name in Micah's day. That is solid ground. Build on it.

V.

Babel: A Tower, a Name, and a Scattering

Genesis 11 doesn't name Nimrod, but Babel was "the beginning of his kingdom" (10:10). The account is only nine verses long, yet it explains why there are many languages and many nations, and it shows the root sin behind all the idolatry that follows.

Scriptural References

Genesis 11:3-4 And they said one to another, Go to, let us make brick, and burn them throughly. And they had brick for stone, and slime had they for morter. And they said, Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth.
Genesis 11:9 Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth: and from thence did the LORD scatter them abroad upon the face of all the earth.
Genesis 12:2 And I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee, and make thy name great.

The Three "Names"

Follow the word shem, "name," through Genesis 6 to 12:

Genesis 6:4. The Nephilim era produced "men of the name." They made names for themselves, and God sent the Flood.
Genesis 11:4. At Babel: "let us make us a name." They tried again, and God scattered them.
Genesis 11:10. The very next verse after Babel begins, "These are the generations of Shem." Noah's son's name literally means "Name."
Genesis 12:2. From that line God calls Abram and says, "I will... make thy name great." A great name is something God gives, not something man builds.

This is the heart of the whole study. Paganism is man making a name for himself, and then worshipping the names he made.

What the Ground Shows

Bricks, Bitumen, and Ziggurats

Artifact "Brick for stone, and slime for morter"

Southern Mesopotamia has almost no building stone, so its cities were built of baked brick set in bitumen (natural asphalt, the "slime" of the King James). Genesis describes this exactly, and even explains it for readers who were used to stone. That is an accurate detail from someone who knew the region.

Artifact The Ziggurats

Mesopotamian cities were crowned with stepped temple-towers called ziggurats. The temple sequence at Eridu, the city where "kingship first descended from heaven," goes back to the earliest settlement. The ziggurat of Ur-Nammu at Ur (about 2100 BC) still stands in part. The great ziggurat of Babylon was called Etemenanki, "House of the Foundation of Heaven and Earth," with a base about 91 meters square. Kings boasted of raising a ziggurat's top "to heaven," the very phrase of Genesis 11:4.

Artifact Scripture "Gate of God" or "Confusion"?

The Babylonians called their city Bab-ili, "Gate of God." Genesis 11:9 connects the name to the Hebrew balal, "to confuse." God takes their proud name, "Gate of God," and turns it into "Confusion." It's a deliberate, divine pun.

The Pagan Memories

Echoes of Babel

Artifact Enmerkar and the "Spell of Nudimmud"

A Sumerian epic, Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta (copies from about 1800 BC), contains a passage that speaks of a time when all the lands "addressed Enlil together in a single language," and of the god Enki changing "the speech in their mouths." Samuel Noah Kramer published it in 1968 under the title "The Babel of Tongues: A Sumerian Version." Theory Translators debate whether the passage describes a past confusion or a hoped-for future unity, so it should be taught as a probable echo, not a proven parallel.

Classical Abydenus and the Sibyl

A Greek writer named Abydenus, drawing on Babylonian tradition and quoted by Eusebius, said the first men, proud of their size and strength, built a lofty tower where Babylon now stands, until the gods sent winds to overthrow it and gave each people a different language. The Jewish Sibylline Oracles (book 3) tell a similar story.

Tradition The North Remembers Babel

A copy of Snorri's Prose Edda in the Codex Wormianus (about 1350) adds the Tower of Babel to its prologue: 72 master-builders, 72 languages spreading over the world, and the foremost builder named Zoroaster. This ties into an older Christian tradition that Nimrod and Zoroaster, the "inventor of magic," were connected (Section VI).

Where Organized Idolatry Began

Scripture The Bible itself treats Babylon as the fountainhead of false worship. Joshua 24:2 says Abraham's father Terah, in Ur, "served other gods." Zechariah 5:5-11 pictures Wickedness as a woman carried back to "the land of Shinar" to have a house built for her there. Jeremiah 51:7 calls Babylon a golden cup "that made all the earth drunken," and Revelation 17:5 names her "MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS AND ABOMINATIONS OF THE EARTH."

Notice that this point rests on Scripture alone. It doesn't need any of the 19th-century speculations examined in Section XX. The Bible says Babylon is the mother; the history of the gods, as we'll see, bears that out.

VI.

Ninus and the Founding of Nineveh

Here the study moves from what the Bible says to what the Greeks said, and the difference matters. Nineveh is one of the best-documented cities in the ancient world. Ninus, its supposed founder, appears in no Assyrian record at all. Understanding where Ninus came from is the key to teaching this subject without getting caught out.

The Real City

Nineveh in the Record

Artifact One of the Oldest Cities on Earth

Nineveh, across the Tigris from modern Mosul, was settled thousands of years before Assyria became an empire. Its cuneiform name was Ninua. From early times it was the city of Ishtar of Nineveh, one of the most famous forms of the goddess we'll meet in Section VIII. Around 1350 BC, the king of Mitanni sent her statue all the way to Egypt to heal the ailing Pharaoh Amenhotep III (Amarna letter EA 23). Sennacherib made Nineveh the capital of Assyria around 700 BC, and it fell in 612 BC.

Scripture Nineveh in the Bible

Scripture credits Nineveh's building to Nimrod (or to Asshur, depending on how Genesis 10:11 is read) and calls Assyria "the land of Nimrod" (Micah 5:6). Later, Nineveh is the "great city" of Jonah and the doomed city of Nahum (Section XIV).

The Legend

Ninus, King of Assyria, in Greek Writers

Classical Herodotus (about 430 BC)

The earliest mention of Ninus is a single line in Herodotus (Histories 1.7), in the family tree of the old kings of Lydia: "Agron son of Ninus, son of Belus, son of Alcaeus, son of Heracles." So in the first Greek reference, Ninus is a son of Belus and a descendant of the hero Heracles. Remember the name Belus. It turns out to be the Greek form of the Babylonian title Bel, "Lord" (Section XVI).

Classical Ctesias and Diodorus: The Full Story

The full legend of Ninus and Semiramis first appears in Ctesias of Cnidus, a Greek physician at the Persian court around 400 BC. His book is lost, but Diodorus of Sicily retold it about 60-30 BC (Library of History, book 2). In Diodorus, Ninus is the first king to make wars of conquest. Allied with a king of Arabia, he conquers Babylonia, Armenia, Media, and nearly all of Asia over seventeen years, then founds a vast city named after himself. Diodorus places that city on the Euphrates. The real Nineveh sits on the Tigris, a telling error from a writer far from the facts.

Classical Justin: Ninus and Zoroaster

The Roman historian Justin, summarizing the older writer Pompeius Trogus, says Ninus was the first to change the ancient custom of the nations and make war out of lust for empire. His last war was against Zoroaster, king of the Bactrians, "who is said to have first invented magic arts."

Ninus appears nowhere in the Assyrian King List, which begins with "seventeen kings who lived in tents," and nowhere in any cuneiform text. Historians regard him as a Greek invention, a founder-figure made up from the city's name, possibly blended with memories of real kings such as Shamshi-Adad I or Tukulti-Ninurta I. Greeks often invented a founder named after a city. The city is real; the man named for it probably isn't.

The Identifications

How Ninus Got Connected to Nimrod

Tradition The Clementine Recognitions (3rd-4th century AD)

This Christian writing, falsely attributed to Clement of Rome, is the first to say it outright. Speaking of the origin of idolatry, it says: "First among whom is named a certain king Nimrod, the magic art having been handed down to him as by a flash, whom the Greeks also called Ninus, and from whom the city of Nineveh took its name" (Recognitions 4.29). A companion work, the Clementine Homilies, connects Nimrod with Zoroaster instead.

Tradition Other Writers Disagreed

Not everyone made Nimrod and Ninus the same man. The medieval Chronicles of Jerahmeel say Nimrod begat Bel, Bel reigned in Babylon, and Bel's son Ninus conquered Assyria and built Nineveh. That makes Ninus Nimrod's grandson, which actually fits Herodotus's "Ninus son of Belus." The 5th-century historian Orosius also made Ninus a descendant of Nimrod rather than Nimrod himself.

Tradition Eusebius Puts Ninus in the Bible's Timeline

Around AD 325, Eusebius of Caesarea built a world chronicle that lined up the Bible with the Greek histories. Jerome translated it into Latin, and it shaped Christian history for over a thousand years. Its opening entry: "Ninus son of Belus was the first to reign over all Asia except for the Indies, for 52 years. In the 43rd year of the rule of this Ninus, Abraham was born." That single synchronism is why older Bible commentaries and histories treat Ninus and Semiramis as real contemporaries of Abraham.

Tradition Malalas: The First King Was Kronos

The Byzantine chronicler John Malalas (6th century) went further. He made the Titan Kronos the first king of Assyria after Babel, a man later worshipped as a god. His wife was Semiramis, "also called Rhea," and their children were Ninus, Picus Zeus, and Hera. In Malalas, Semiramis has become the mother of the Greek gods.

Weighing the Evidence

Nineveh is real, and Scripture ties it to Nimrod. Ninus is a Greek literary figure first described in detail around 400 BC. The idea that Nimrod is Ninus comes from a Christian tradition about seven hundred years after that, and other ancient writers disagreed with it. You can say, "Early Christian writers identified Nimrod with the Ninus of Greek legend." You cannot honestly say, "History records that Nimrod was also called Ninus."

VII.

Semiramis and Menones: The Legend of the Queen

Semiramis is the most famous queen of the ancient legends and the most misunderstood figure in popular teaching about Babylon. Here is her story as the ancient writers actually told it, followed by the real woman behind the name.

The Legend

The Story as Diodorus Tells It (Library of History 2.4-20)

Classical Daughter of a Fish Goddess, Raised by Doves

At Ascalon in Syria (the Philistine city of the Bible) there was a lake sacred to the goddess Derceto, who had the face of a woman and the body of a fish. Derceto bore a daughter to a Syrian youth, then in shame abandoned the baby in the rocky desert and threw herself into the lake. Doves fed the infant, first with milk and then with cheese, until shepherds found her. Simmas, keeper of the royal herds, raised her and named her Semiramis, which Diodorus says came from the Syrian word for doves. This, he explains, is why the Syrians honor doves as divine and will not eat fish.

Classical Menones, Her First Husband

When she came of age, a royal officer named Onnes (called Menones in some older books), the first man of the king's council and governor of all Syria, saw her while inspecting the herds and married her. They had two sons, Hyapates and Hydaspes. Onnes did nothing without her advice and prospered in everything.

During Ninus's siege of Bactra, Onnes sent for her. She travelled in a garment that hid whether she was a man or a woman, studied the siege, and personally led a party of soldiers up to take the citadel. Ninus was amazed, then infatuated. He offered Onnes his own daughter Sosane in exchange for Semiramis. When Onnes refused, Ninus threatened to put out his eyes. Out of fear of the king and passion for his wife, Onnes "fell into a kind of frenzy and madness" and hanged himself.

Classical Queen of Asia

Semiramis married Ninus and bore him a son, Ninyas. Ninus died soon after. She buried him within the palace and raised an enormous mound over his tomb. Then she built Babylon: miles of walls, a bridge over the Euphrates, palaces painted with hunting scenes, and a temple of Zeus (whom the Babylonians called Belus) with statues of Zeus, Hera, and Rhea on top. She carved her image on the cliff of Bagistanus (Behistun), marched through Media, Persia, Egypt, and Ethiopia, and finally invaded India, where she was defeated. Learning that Ninyas was plotting against her, she handed him the kingdom and vanished. "Some say she was turned into a dove and flew off with many birds," and so the Assyrians worshipped Semiramis as a goddess. She reigned 42 years.

The Variants

The Ancients Could Not Agree About Her

Classical A Courtesan Who Killed the King

Diodorus himself (2.20) reports a rival version: Semiramis was a beautiful courtesan whom Ninus loved. She asked to hold royal power for just five days. Once crowned, she had Ninus seized and put to death, and ruled in his place.

Classical Disguised as Her Son

Justin (book 1.2) says that after Ninus died she dressed as her young son, whom she resembled, and ruled as a man until her achievements made her secure. He adds that she was finally killed by that same son after she tried to seduce him.

Classical A Real Queen, No Miracles

Herodotus (1.184), the oldest source, says only that Semiramis ruled Babylon five generations before a later queen named Nitocris and built dikes on the plain to control the river. No fish goddess, no doves, no Ninus.

Classical Who Built the Hanging Gardens?

Some Greeks credited them to Semiramis. Diodorus (2.10) says a later Syrian king built them for a Persian concubine. Berossus, a Babylonian priest, says Nebuchadnezzar built them for his Median wife. Berossus also scolded the Greek writers for thinking Babylon was founded by Semiramis at all (quoted in Josephus, Against Apion 1.142).

Tradition Armenia: Semiramis and Ara the Beautiful

The Armenian historian Movses Khorenatsi tells how Shamiram lusted after the Armenian king Ara the Beautiful. When he refused her, she made war on him and he was killed. She then tried to bring him back to life by laying his body before the Aralez, dog-spirits who licked wounds to heal them. A goddess-queen trying to raise her dead lover is the Ishtar and Tammuz pattern (Section IX) wearing Armenian clothes.

Classical Tradition Rome and the Middle Ages

Ovid (Metamorphoses 4) sets the story of Pyramus and Thisbe in "the high city Semiramis surrounded with walls of brick," with the lovers meeting at the tomb of Ninus. Orosius (5th century) accused her of legalizing incest, and Dante placed her among the lustful in Inferno 5, the queen who "made lust lawful in her law."

The History

The Real Semiramis: Queen Shammuramat

Artifact Who She Was

Shammuramat (Sammu-ramat) was the wife of the Assyrian king Shamshi-Adad V (reigned 824-811 BC) and mother of Adad-nirari III (reigned 811-783 BC). A stone stele set up for her at the city of Ashur calls her the palace woman of Shamshi-Adad, mother of Adad-nirari, and daughter-in-law of Shalmaneser III. That was an extraordinary honor for a woman in Assyria. A boundary stele from Pazarcık in Turkey records that she crossed the Euphrates on campaign alongside her son (about 805 BC), and statues of the god Nabu at Calah were dedicated for her life and his. Many historians think she held real power, perhaps as regent, in her son's early years.

Artifact Scripture Her Place on the Timeline

Shammuramat lived around 800 BC. That puts her in the time of the prophets Elisha and Jonah (2 Kings 14:25), roughly 1,200 years after the time Eusebius assigned to Semiramis, and more than a thousand years after any reasonable date for Nimrod.

What the Greeks did was take a real and remarkable 9th-century queen, move her back to the dawn of history, marry her to an invented founder-king, and surround her with the imagery of a goddess. The legend of Semiramis is a portrait of how a "woman of renown" becomes a myth.

Semiramis and the Goddess: What Is Solid

The link between Semiramis and the Queen of Heaven is not a modern invention. The ancients built it themselves. Her mother Derceto is the Syrian goddess Atargatis, whose name blends Astarte and Anat. Doves were the sacred birds of Ishtar, Astarte, and Aphrodite. Herodotus (1.105) says the temple of "Heavenly Aphrodite" at Ascalon, the very city of Derceto's lake, was the oldest temple of that goddess anywhere. And Lucian says some believed Semiramis founded the great Syrian temple at Hierapolis for her mother Derceto. So it is fair to teach that the Semiramis legend absorbed the goddess cult of the Queen of Heaven.

What is not in any ancient source: that Semiramis was the wife of Nimrod, that she was the mother of Tammuz, or that she claimed Tammuz was Nimrod reborn. Those claims come from Alexander Hislop in 1853, and we'll test them in Section XX.

VIII.

Inanna and Ishtar: The Birth of the Queen of Heaven

We now step back in time. The legend of Semiramis was written around 400 BC, but the goddess whose imagery she absorbed is far older. She first appears in the Sumerian cities of the third millennium BC, and her first great temple stood in one of Nimrod's own cities.

Sumer and Akkad

Inanna, Lady of Heaven

Artifact Her Temple in Erech

Inanna's chief temple was the Eanna, "House of Heaven," at Uruk. Uruk is the Erech of Genesis 10:10, listed as part of "the beginning of [Nimrod's] kingdom." Some of the oldest writing ever found comes from the Eanna precinct. The Queen of Heaven's worship began, as far as the record shows, in the heartland of Nimrod's kingdom.

Artifact Who She Was

Inanna's name is usually explained as "Lady of Heaven." She was goddess of sexual love and of war, a strange pairing that stays with her in every culture. Her star was the planet Venus, the morning and evening star. Her symbols were the eight-pointed star and the lion. In one text she introduces herself: "I am Inanna, Queen of Heaven." Her Akkadian name is Ishtar, and Akkadian hymns give her the titles "Queen of Heaven" (sharrat shamê) and "Lady of Heaven" (bēlet shamê).

Artifact Sargon's Daughter, Her Poet

The first author in history known by name is Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon of Akkad (one of the Nimrod candidates) and high priestess of the moon god at Ur. Her most famous work, the Exaltation of Inanna, lifts Inanna above the other gods. The empire-builder's household was also the Queen of Heaven's chief promoter.

Artifact Her Descent to the Underworld

In the Sumerian Descent of Inanna, she goes down to the land of the dead, ruled by her sister Ereshkigal. At each of seven gates she is stripped of a garment or jewel until she stands naked, is struck dead, and is hung on a hook. The god Enki sends creatures to revive her, but the rule of the underworld is that someone must take her place. Back on earth she finds her husband, the shepherd Dumuzi, sitting on his throne in fine clothes, not mourning her at all. She hands him over to the demons. That story leads straight to Section IX.

Artifact Gilgamesh Refuses Her

In Tablet VI of the Gilgamesh epic, Ishtar proposes marriage to Gilgamesh. He refuses and lists what happened to her previous lovers, beginning: "For Dumuzi, the lover of your youth, you have ordained weeping year after year." Keep that line in mind for Ezekiel 8:14. Insulted, Ishtar sends the Bull of Heaven against him.

A Note on Her Rites

Artifact Sumerian hymns describe a "sacred marriage" in which the king united with Inanna, probably represented by a priestess, to secure the fertility of the land. Theory How widespread literal temple prostitution was in her cult is debated among scholars today, and it's best not to overstate it. Scripture What is not debated is that the Law forbade cult prostitutes of either sex in Israel (Deuteronomy 23:17-18), and that the prophets condemned sexual rites connected to the idols (1 Kings 14:24; Hosea 4:14).

God Called Abraham Out of This World

Scripture Abraham grew up in Ur, the city of the moon god Nanna (called Sin in Akkadian), whose daughter, in Sumerian tradition, was Inanna. His family moved to Haran, another great center of the moon god. Joshua 24:2 says plainly that his father "served other gods." When God said, "Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred" (Genesis 12:1), He was calling Abraham out of the religion of Babel and its Queen of Heaven. The story of Israel begins as a rescue from this system.

✦ Bonus: One Goddess, Many Names

As Inanna's worship spread, she took new names while keeping her star, her doves or lions, her love-and-war character, and her title. Ishtar in Babylon and Assyria. Astarte in Phoenicia, called Ashtoreth in the Bible. Atargatis (Derceto) in Syria. Aphrodite in Greece, arriving by way of Cyprus. Venus in Rome, who even kept Ishtar's planet. Egypt imported Astarte and Anat as "Lady of Heaven." In the North, Freyja was the goddess of both love and battle, and Friday is named for Frigg, the Germanic stand-in for Venus's day. See Appendix A for the full table, and Section XVIII for an ancient text in which the goddess herself says she is worshipped under many names.

IX.

Dumuzi and Tammuz: The Weeping for the Dying God

Ezekiel saw women weeping for Tammuz at the gate of the Lord's own house (Ezekiel 8:14). To understand what they were doing, and why every nation seemed to have a version of it, we have to go back to a shepherd-king of Sumer.

The Original

Dumuzi the Shepherd

Artifact A King Before He Was a God

The Sumerian King List names a "Dumuzi the shepherd" among the kings before the Flood (at the city of Bad-tibira), and a "Dumuzi the fisherman" as a king of Uruk after the Flood, just before Gilgamesh. However historians sort those entries out, the Mesopotamians themselves remembered Dumuzi as a man who reigned, a man of old, before he was a god. That is the Genesis 6:4 pattern once again.

Artifact Husband of the Queen of Heaven

Sumerian love poems celebrate Dumuzi's courtship of Inanna. But in the Descent of Inanna she gives him to the demons of the underworld as her substitute. Related texts say his sister Geshtinanna mourned him and agreed to take his place for half of each year, so that he goes down and comes up with the seasons. His death was mourned every year in the heat of summer, when the pastures withered.

Artifact A Month Named for Him

In Akkadian his name became Du'uzu, and the fourth month of the Babylonian year, falling in June and July, bore his name. The Jews adopted the Babylonian month names during the Exile (Nisan, Elul, and Adar appear in Nehemiah and Esther), and so the fourth month of the Jewish calendar is still called Tammuz today.

The Variants

The Dying Consort in Every Culture

Classical Canaan and Greece: Adonis

His name is simply the Semitic word adon, "lord." Loved by Aphrodite, he was killed by a wild boar. Women mourned him in the festival of the Adonia, planting "gardens of Adonis," quick-sprouting seedlings in shallow pots that withered in days. Lucian describes his rites at Byblos in Phoenicia, where the river Adonis ran red each year and was said to carry his blood.

Artifact Classical Egypt: Osiris

Osiris appears in the Pyramid Texts (about 2400 BC) as the god who died and rules the dead. The full story comes from Plutarch (On Isis and Osiris, about AD 100). Set murders his brother Osiris and sets his coffin adrift; it washes ashore at Byblos, the same city as the rites of Adonis. His wife Isis searches, mourning, finds him, and after Set cuts the body to pieces she gathers them again. Osiris becomes lord of the underworld, and Isis conceives Horus. Plutarch himself noticed the Byblos connection.

Classical Phrygia and Rome: Attis

Attis was the young consort of the great mother goddess Cybele. He died, and his death was mourned in a March festival that ended in rejoicing. Rome officially brought Cybele's cult to the city in 204 BC.

Tradition The North: Baldr

Baldr, the beautiful son of Odin and Frigg, was killed by a dart of mistletoe through Loki's trickery. The goddess of the dead agreed to release him if everything in the world would weep for him. Everything did, except one giantess (Loki in disguise), so Baldr stayed among the dead (Gylfaginning 49). The whole world weeping for a slain god is the Tammuz rite on a cosmic scale.

Answering the Skeptic: Is Jesus Just Another Tammuz?

Since James Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890), skeptics have claimed the Gospel copied a common pagan "dying and rising god." Honest scholarship doesn't support that. Theory The category itself has been heavily criticized (by J. Z. Smith) and only partly defended (by T. N. D. Mettinger, 2001). And look at what the pagan stories actually are:

  • They are seasonal and endless: Dumuzi goes down and comes up every year, like the grass. Christ died once for all (Hebrews 9:26-28) and "dieth no more" (Romans 6:9).
  • They are set in mythic time with no date, place, or witnesses. Christ "suffered under Pontius Pilate" in a known city, in a known year.
  • Their "returns" are partial: Dumuzi stays in the underworld half the year, and Osiris stays dead and rules the dead. Christ rose bodily and was seen by more than five hundred witnesses (1 Corinthians 15:3-8).

The weeping for Tammuz is a ritual of despair that repeats forever. The Gospel is news of a single victory that ended the need for weeping (Revelation 21:4).

A popular claim says Tammuz was Nimrod reincarnated and the son of Semiramis, and that the forty days of Lent began as forty days of weeping for him. No ancient source makes Tammuz the son of Semiramis or a reborn Nimrod, and the Lent link rests on very thin evidence. See Section XX.
X.

Egypt: The Gods Judged at the Exodus

Mizraim's descendants built the most enduring pagan civilization of all. Its gods were many and strange, but by the time of the Exodus Egypt had also absorbed the gods of Canaan and Mesopotamia. When God struck Egypt, He announced exactly whom He was judging.

Scriptural References

Exodus 12:12 For I will pass through the land of Egypt this night, and will smite all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, both man and beast; and against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment: I am the LORD.
Numbers 33:4 For the Egyptians buried all their firstborn, which the LORD had smitten among them: upon their gods also the LORD executed judgments.

Dating Note

The Exodus: 15th or 13th Century BC

Scripture Theory When

Taking 1 Kings 6:1 at face value, the Exodus occurred about 480 years before Solomon began the temple, placing it in the mid-1400s BC. Many scholars prefer a date in the 1200s. Either way, it falls in Egypt's New Kingdom, when foreign gods were flooding into the country.

The Gods of Egypt

What Egypt Worshipped

Artifact The Native Gods

Ra (or Re), the sun, often merged with Amun of Thebes as Amun-Ra, king of the gods. Osiris, lord of the dead; Isis, his wife; Horus, their son, the falcon god embodied in each living Pharaoh; and Set, the storm and desert god who murdered Osiris. Hathor, the cow goddess of love and music. Hapi, the god of the Nile flood. The Egyptian title "Lady of Heaven" was given to several goddesses, including Hathor and Isis.

Artifact The Imported Gods

New Kingdom Egypt welcomed the gods of Canaan. Ba'al was worshipped and identified with Set. Astarte and Anat became warrior goddesses and "Ladies of Heaven." Rameses II called himself beloved of Anat and named a daughter Bint-Anat, "Daughter of Anat." And as noted in Section VI, Ishtar of Nineveh herself was sent to Egypt to heal Amenhotep III.

The Plagues and the Gods

Scripture The Bible says the judgments fell on "all the gods of Egypt" without naming each one. Theory Teachers often pair each plague with a particular god. Some pairings are strong, and some are a stretch, so choose carefully:

Strong: the Nile turned to blood (the river was Egypt's life, deified as Hapi and tied to Osiris); the three days of darkness (Ra, the sun, was the king of the gods); the death of the firstborn (Pharaoh claimed to be the son of Ra, and his heir a god-in-waiting).
Reasonable: the frogs (Heqet, a frog-headed goddess of birth); the death of the livestock (the Apis bull and Hathor the cow).
Weak: specific god-matches for lice, flies, and boils are mostly guesswork. The point stands without them.

Later Egypt

Isis Becomes Queen of Heaven to the World

Artifact Classical Isis and Horus

In the centuries after Alexander the Great, Isis became a universal mother goddess worshipped from Egypt to Britain, with temples in Rome and titles such as "Queen of Heaven." Statues showing Isis nursing the infant Horus were common. Art historians have long discussed how that image influenced later Christian paintings of mother and child. Her last great temple, at Philae, was not closed until the 6th century AD.

Classical The Colossi of "Memnon"

Two giant seated statues of Amenhotep III stand at Thebes. The Greeks and Romans called them the Colossi of Memnon. After an earthquake in 27 BC, the northern statue made a sound at dawn, and visitors said it was Memnon greeting his mother, the Dawn. Roman tourists carved their names into its legs. That same Memnon will carry us from Egypt to Troy and on to Thor in Section XII.

XI.

Canaan: El, Asherah, Ba'al, and the Storm-God Family

Israel entered a land full of gods. For most of history we knew those gods only through the Bible's condemnations. Then, in 1928, a farmer's plow broke into a tomb near the Syrian coast, and the library of a Canaanite city came to light.

The Discovery

The Tablets of Ugarit

Artifact Ras Shamra

Excavations at Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit, began in 1929 under Claude Schaeffer. The city's tablets, written about 1400-1200 BC in an alphabetic cuneiform, include long poems about the gods of Canaan. They are the closest thing we have to the Canaanites' own Bible, and they come from the very era of Israel's entry into the land.

The Pantheon

The Gods of Canaan in Their Own Words

Artifact El

The aged father and king of the gods, called "Father of Years" and "Bull El," creator of creatures, gray-bearded, living at the source of the two rivers. At Ugarit, El is a specific god with a wife and children. But el is also the ordinary Semitic word for "god," used across the whole region. That double meaning matters a great deal in Section XIII.

Artifact Asherah (Athirat)

El's consort, "Lady Athirat of the Sea," mother of the gods, called the mother of seventy sons. In the Bible, asherah is both the goddess and her wooden cult pole or image. The King James usually translates it "grove." When Jezebel fed "the prophets of the groves four hundred" (1 Kings 18:19), those were prophets of Asherah.

Artifact Ba'al Hadad

Ba'al means "lord," "master," or "husband." It is a title. The god's personal name was Hadad, the storm god. He is called "Rider of the Clouds," and a famous stele shows him striding forward with a club raised and a lightning-spear in hand. He lived on Mount Zaphon, the sacred mountain of the north, where he built a palace. He brought the rain on which every farmer depended.

Artifact The Ba'al Cycle

Ba'al fights Yam (Sea) and defeats him with two magic clubs. He boasts of crushing Lotan, the twisting serpent with seven heads. Then Mot (Death) swallows him, and the rains stop. His violent sister Anat searches for him, kills Mot, and Ba'al returns to life, bringing back the rain. Here is another dying and returning figure, tied to the seasons like Dumuzi.

When the Bible Uses Ba'al's Language

Artifact Scripture The Ugaritic text about Lotan calls him "the fleeing serpent... the twisting serpent, the mighty one with seven heads." Now read Isaiah 27:1: "In that day the LORD with his sore and great and strong sword shall punish leviathan the piercing serpent, even leviathan that crooked serpent." The Hebrew words match the Ugaritic words. Psalm 74:14 speaks of God breaking "the heads of leviathan." Psalm 68:4 exalts Him "that rideth upon the heavens," and Isaiah 19:1 says "the LORD rideth upon a swift cloud."

This is not the Bible borrowing Canaanite religion. It is the Bible taking the titles and victories the Canaanites gave to Ba'al and declaring that they belong to the Lord alone. Isaiah 14:13 even has the king of Babylon boasting, "I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north," using the language of Ba'al's mountain, Zaphon. Scripture answers every false claim by name.

Scriptural References

Isaiah 27:1 In that day the LORD with his sore and great and strong sword shall punish leviathan the piercing serpent, even leviathan that crooked serpent; and he shall slay the dragon that is in the sea.
Psalm 29:3 The voice of the LORD is upon the waters: the God of glory thundereth: the LORD is upon many waters.

The Variants

The Storm-God Family Across the Nations

Artifact Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Egypt

Adad was Hadad's name in Babylon and Assyria. The Hurrians called their storm god Teshub, the Hittites Tarhunz. In Egypt, as we saw, Ba'al was identified with Set.

Classical Greece: Zeus on Ba'al's Mountain

Zeus is the cloud-gatherer who hurls the thunderbolt. He fought the monster Typhon, a serpent-limbed giant, and according to Apollodorus (Library 1.6.3) the battle took place at Mount Casius in Syria. Mount Casius is Mount Zaphon, Ba'al's mountain. The Greeks worshipped "Zeus Casius" on the same peak where the Canaanites worshipped "Ba'al Zaphon." The Hurrian-Hittite myths of the storm god's rise to power are so close to Hesiod's account of Zeus that scholars agree the Greek version came from the Near East.

Classical Rome: Jupiter

Jupiter (from an ancient word meaning "Sky-Father") took over Zeus's thunderbolt and eagle. In 167 BC the Greek king Antiochus IV renamed the temple in Jerusalem for "Olympian Zeus" (2 Maccabees 6:2), the act Daniel foresaw as the abomination that makes desolate (Daniel 11:31).

Tradition The North: Thor

Thor (Þórr, "thunder") rides a chariot pulled by goats, swings the hammer Mjölnir against the giants, and fights the great sea serpent Jörmungandr, which encircles the world. The medieval chronicler Adam of Bremen (about 1075) wrote that at the temple of Uppsala, Thor "presides over the air, which governs thunder and lightning, winds and rains, fair weather and crops." That is almost word for word the job description of Ba'al.

A careful teacher should add this: Thor comes from the Indo-European family of gods (alongside the Slavic Perun and the Hindu Indra), while Ba'al comes from the Semitic family. They are not the same person under two names. They are the same office. The storm god who slays the sea serpent and sends the rain appears in both families, and in each case Scripture says the throne he claims belongs to the Lord.
XII.

Troy and Memnon: How Thor Got a Trojan Father

The name "Menon" shows up in two different places in these legends. The first is Menones (Onnes), Semiramis's first husband (Section VII). The second is Memnon, also spelled Mennon, the king who fought at Troy. This second Menon is the thread that ties Assyria, Egypt, Greece, Rome, and the Norse gods together, and every link in the chain is in a text you can open.

The Chain

Memnon from Assyria to Iceland

Classical Greece: The Son of the Dawn

In Greek epic, Memnon was king of the Ethiopians and son of Eos (the Dawn) and Tithonus, a prince of Troy and brother of King Priam. Homer mentions him in the Odyssey (4.188; 11.522) and Hesiod in the Theogony. In the lost epic Aethiopis, he comes to rescue Troy after Hector's death, kills Nestor's son Antilochus, and is slain by Achilles. His mother Dawn carries his body away. The traditional date of the Trojan War is around 1200 BC.

Classical Assyria: Sent by the Heir of Ninus

Ctesias, as preserved by Diodorus (2.22), put Memnon inside the Ninus and Semiramis saga. When Teutamus was king of Asia, about twenty generations after Ninyas son of Semiramis, the Greeks attacked Troy. Priam, a vassal of the Assyrian king, asked for help, and Teutamus sent ten thousand Ethiopians, ten thousand men of Susa, and two hundred chariots under the command of Memnon son of Tithonus. Diodorus adds that Memnon built the palace at Susa called the Memnonium.

Classical Egypt: The Singing Statue

As we saw in Section X, the Greeks gave Memnon's name to the colossal statues of Amenhotep III at Thebes, and the "singing" northern statue was said to be Memnon greeting his mother at dawn. The Ethiopians of Africa and the Persians of Susa both claimed him.

Classical Rome: Birds From His Ashes

Ovid (Metamorphoses 13) tells how Aurora (Dawn) begged Jupiter to honor her son. From the smoke of his funeral pyre rose a flock of birds, the Memnonides, which fought each year at his tomb. Her tears for him fall every morning as dew. Virgil (Aeneid 1.489) pictures "the arms of black Memnon" painted on the temple of Juno at Carthage.

Tradition The North: Thor, Son of Mennon

Around 1220, the Icelandic Christian scholar Snorri Sturluson opened his Prose Edda with a prologue explaining where the Norse gods came from. Troy, he says, was the greatest city at the center of the world, with twelve kingdoms under the High King Priam. "One king among them was called Múnón or Mennón; and he was wedded to the daughter of the High King Priam, her who was called Tróán; they had a child named Trór, whom we call Thor." Thor was raised in Thrace, killed his foster-father at twelve, conquered giants, berserks, and a dragon, and married a prophetess named Sibil, "whom we call Sif." Many generations later came Vóden, "whom we call Odin," who led his people north. The Æsir, Snorri says, were "the men of Asia."

What Snorri Was Doing

Iceland had become Christian around the year 1000. Snorri was preserving the old stories for poets while making clear they were not true religion. His method is called euhemerism, after the Greek writer Euhemerus (about 300 BC), who taught that the gods were famous kings and heroes of long ago whom later generations worshipped. Snorri's prologue begins with the creation, Adam and Eve, Noah's Flood, and mankind forgetting God. Then it explains the Norse gods as great men of Troy who were honored as gods.

That is the Genesis 6:4 and Genesis 10:8 pattern, told by a medieval Christian: men of renown, remembered as gods. Snorri's family tree is a learned theory, not a historical record. But his theory lines up with the Bible's own explanation of paganism far better than the modern idea that the gods are just nature personified.

✦ Bonus: Snorri's Other Trick

The main section of Snorri's book, where the myths are told, is called Gylfaginning, "The Deluding of Gylfi." In it a Swedish king visits the Æsir, and they tell him the stories of the gods, and at the end the whole hall vanishes. Snorri frames the entire Norse mythology as a deception performed on a pagan king. It's hard to imagine a clearer Christian verdict on the old gods.

XIII.

Yahweh Against the Ba'als: From the Judges to Elijah

From the day Israel entered Canaan, the pull of Ba'al and Ashtoreth never let up. The history of Israel in the land is largely the history of that struggle, and it reached its climax on Mount Carmel.

The Long Struggle

Israel and the Gods of the Land

Scripture In the Wilderness and Under the Judges

At Peor, "Israel joined himself unto Baalpeor" (Numbers 25:3). Under the judges, "they forsook the LORD, and served Baal and Ashtaroth" (Judges 2:13). Gideon's first act was to tear down his own father's altar to Ba'al and cut down the grove beside it, earning the name Jerubbaal, "Let Ba'al contend" (Judges 6:25-32).

Scripture Solomon

"For Solomon went after Ashtoreth the goddess of the Zidonians, and after Milcom the abomination of the Ammonites" (1 Kings 11:5). The Queen of Heaven, under her Phoenician name, was given a high place outside Jerusalem by Israel's wisest king. It stood until Josiah tore it down more than three centuries later (2 Kings 23:13).

Scripture Ahab and Jezebel

Ahab married Jezebel, daughter of Ethbaal, king of the Zidonians, built a temple for Ba'al in Samaria, and made an Asherah (1 Kings 16:31-33). Ethbaal's own name means "with Ba'al." In Tyre, Ba'al was worshipped as Melqart, "king of the city," whom the Greeks identified with Heracles. Herodotus visited his temple there (Histories 2.44).

Mount Carmel: A Contest on Ba'al's Own Ground

Every detail of 1 Kings 17-18 strikes at Ba'al's claims. Ba'al was the god of rain, so Elijah announced three and a half years with no rain or dew "but according to my word" (17:1). Ba'al was the lightning god, so the contest was: "the God that answereth by fire, let him be God" (18:24). The prophets of Ba'al cried out from morning until noon and cut themselves. Elijah mocked them: "peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked" (18:27). The Ugaritic poems really do describe Ba'al as dead and gone for a season while Mot holds him. Then the fire of the Lord fell, and the people cried, "The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God" (18:39). Only after that did the rain come.

Scriptural References

1 Kings 18:21 How long halt ye between two opinions? if the LORD be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him.
Hosea 2:8 For she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil, and multiplied her silver and gold, which they prepared for Baal.
Hosea 2:16 And it shall be at that day, saith the LORD, that thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali.
Hosea 2:16 is worth pausing on. Ba'al could simply mean "husband" or "master," so an Israelite could call the Lord "my Ba'al" and mean nothing pagan by it. God refused even the title. He will not share a name with an idol (Isaiah 42:8).

The Hard Question

Is Yahweh Just Another Canaanite El?

Theory The Skeptic's Claim

A common academic theory says Yahweh began as a separate warrior or storm god from the south (pointing to Judges 5:4, Deuteronomy 33:2, and Habakkuk 3:3, "God came from Teman"), who was later merged with El, the Canaanite high god. Some add that Deuteronomy 32:8-9, in the Dead Sea Scrolls reading, shows the high god Elyon handing Israel to Yahweh as one of his sons.

Artifact "Yahweh and His Asherah"

At Kuntillet Ajrud in the Sinai (about 800 BC), jars were found inscribed with blessings "by Yahweh of Samaria and his asherah" and "by Yahweh of Teman and his asherah." A tomb inscription at Khirbet el-Qom near Hebron says something similar. Critics point to these and say Israel's God originally had a wife.

The Biblical Answer

El is a word before it is a name. El is the common Semitic word for "God," the way "God" is in English. Scripture uses it for the true God with qualifiers: El Elyon (Most High), El Shaddai (Almighty), El Olam (Everlasting). Sharing a word is not sharing a deity. Paul quoted a Greek poem written about Zeus and applied it to the true God (Acts 17:28) without making Zeus into the Lord.
Abraham identified El Elyon as Yahweh. "I have lift up mine hand unto the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth" (Genesis 14:22). The Hebrew is "Yahweh, El Elyon." There is one God, not a father god and a junior god.
The name is revelation, not evolution. "I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, by the name of God Almighty, but by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them" (Exodus 6:3; see 3:14-15). The same God made Himself known more fully.
Deuteronomy 32 can't be read the skeptic's way. The same chapter says, "the LORD alone did lead him, and there was no strange god with him" (v. 12), calls the other gods "devils" (v. 17), and ends, "I, even I, am he, and there is no god with me" (v. 39).
"God came from Teman" describes Sinai. Habakkuk 3 and Deuteronomy 33 are poems about the Lord appearing at Sinai, which lies to the south. They describe where He revealed Himself, not where He was invented.
The inscriptions prove the Bible's history. Kuntillet Ajrud shows exactly what the Bible says Israel was doing. Manasseh set an Asherah image in the temple itself (2 Kings 21:7, where the King James reads "a graven image of the grove"). Josiah "brought out the grove from the house of the LORD" and burned it (2 Kings 23:6). The prophets fought this mixing for centuries. The jars record apostasy; they don't record Israel's true faith.

Scriptural References

Deuteronomy 32:17 They sacrificed unto devils, not to God; to gods whom they knew not, to new gods that came newly up, whom your fathers feared not.
Deuteronomy 32:39 See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god with me: I kill, and I make alive; I wound, and I heal: neither is there any that can deliver out of my hand.
XIV.

Nineveh: The Real Assyria

The Greek legends gave Nineveh a founder named Ninus and a queen named Semiramis. The spade has given us something better: the actual kings of Assyria, in their own words, carved in stone and pressed into clay. When their records are laid beside the Bible, the two fit together so closely that the Assyrian inscriptions have become one of the strongest external witnesses to the history of Israel and Judah.

The Land of Nimrod

Ashur, the City and the God

Scripture Two Ways to Read Genesis 10:11

The King James reads, "Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh, and the city Rehoboth, and Calah" (Genesis 10:11). Asshur here is taken as the son of Shem (10:22). The Hebrew can also be read, "Out of that land he went forth into Assyria," meaning Nimrod carried his kingdom north, and the King James margin itself gives that reading. Micah 5:6 supports it by calling Assyria "the land of Nimrod." Either way, Scripture ties the rise of Assyria to the men who came out of Shinar.

Artifact A God Named After His City

Assyria took its name from the city of Ashur on the Tigris, and the city's god was also called Ashur. He became the national god, and every Assyrian king called himself Ashur's appointed ruler. His symbol was a winged disk. Nineveh itself was far older than the empire. Its great temple belonged to Ishtar of Nineveh, and kings of Akkad and later Shamshi-Adad I (about 1808-1776 BC) restored it. This is the same Ishtar of Nineveh whose statue was sent to Egypt to heal Amenhotep III, as we saw in Section VI.

Artifact Kings Who Hunted "Before Ninurta"

Assyrian kings boasted about hunting almost as much as about war. Tiglath-pileser I (1114-1076 BC) listed the lions and wild bulls he killed on foot and from his chariot. Ashurnasirpal II wrote that "Ninurta and Nergal, who love my priesthood," gave him the beasts of the field. Compare that with Genesis 10:9: "a mighty hunter before the LORD." Assyrian royal hunting was a religious act done before their gods. Tukulti-Ninurta I (1243-1207 BC), whose name means "my trust is in Ninurta," even conquered Babylon and carried off Marduk's statue. As Section IV showed, E. A. Speiser proposed him as the historical memory behind Nimrod.

The Empire Rises

Calah, Ahab, and Jehu

Artifact Calah Rebuilt (about 879 BC)

Ashurnasirpal II (883-859 BC) rebuilt Calah, the city named in Genesis 10:11-12, and made it his capital. At its center he built a temple to Ninurta beside a ziggurat. A stone monument records the ten-day feast he held to open the city, with 69,574 guests. Much later, local Arabs called the ruins "Nimrud" after Nimrod, and that is still the site's name. The name is medieval tradition, but the city is exactly where Genesis puts it.

Artifact Scripture Ahab at Qarqar and Jehu on the Black Obelisk

Shalmaneser III (859-824 BC) fought a coalition of western kings at Qarqar in 853 BC. His Kurkh Monolith names "Ahab the Israelite" as bringing 2,000 chariots and 10,000 soldiers, which shows Ahab as a major military power, just as 1 Kings portrays him. Twelve years later, in 841 BC, Shalmaneser's Black Obelisk, found at Nimrud by Austen Henry Layard in 1846, shows "Jehu son of Omri" (or his envoy) bowing to the ground with tribute. It is the only known picture of an Israelite king. Jehu is the same man who wiped out Ba'al worship in Samaria (2 Kings 10:18-28).

Artifact The Real Semiramis

Shalmaneser's son Shamshi-Adad V (824-811 BC) was married to Shammuramat. After his death she held unusual power during the early reign of her son Adad-nirari III (811-783 BC). As Section VII explained, she is the historical woman behind the legend, and she lived more than a thousand years after the Semiramis of Ctesias was supposed to have ruled.

The Great City Repents

Jonah in Nineveh

Scripture A Historical Prophet

Jonah son of Amittai was a real prophet of the northern kingdom who foretold the victories of Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14:25), who reigned about 793-753 BC. God sent him to Nineveh with an eight-word sermon in English: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (Jonah 3:4). "So the people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth" (3:5). The book closes with God's compassion for a city of "more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand" (4:11). The Lord Jesus treated the event as history: "The men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation... because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here" (Matthew 12:41).

Artifact Theory A Frightened Empire

The Assyrian list of year-names records that in 763 BC, in the year of an official named Bur-Sagale, "in the month Simanu, an eclipse of the sun." Astronomers date it to June 15, 763 BC, and it anchors the whole of Assyrian chronology. The same list records revolts and plague in the years around it. Some scholars suggest that an eclipse, plague, and unrest would have made Nineveh unusually ready to hear a prophet of doom. That link is a theory, but the timing falls within the reign of Jeroboam II.

The Rod of God's Anger

Assyria Against Israel and Judah

Scripture Artifact Pul Is Tiglath-pileser III

"Pul the king of Assyria came against the land: and Menahem gave Pul a thousand talents of silver" (2 Kings 15:19). For a long time readers wondered who Pul was. Babylonian king lists call Tiglath-pileser III (745-727 BC) by his Babylonian throne name, Pulu. 1 Chronicles 5:26 had already put the two names side by side. His annals list tribute from "Menahem of Samaria" and from Ahaz of Judah.

Scripture Artifact Samaria Falls (722 BC) and the Nations Bring Their Gods

Samaria fell after a three-year siege (2 Kings 17:5-6). Sargon II claimed to have carried away about 27,000 people. For centuries Sargon was known only from Isaiah 20:1, and critics doubted he existed, until Paul-Émile Botta uncovered his palace at Khorsabad in 1843. The Assyrians then resettled Samaria with foreigners, and each group brought its own gods: "the men of Babylon made Succothbenoth, and the men of Cuth made Nergal, and the men of Hamath made Ashima, and the Avites made Nibhaz and Tartak, and the Sepharvites burnt their children in fire to Adrammelech and Anammelech" (2 Kings 17:30-31). The result was a mixed religion: "They feared the LORD, and served their own gods" (17:33). Ezra 4:10 later names "the great and noble Asnapper," usually identified with Ashurbanipal, as one who settled more foreigners there.

Artifact Scripture Sennacherib Against Hezekiah (701 BC)

Sennacherib (705-681 BC) made Nineveh his capital and built a vast "Palace Without Rival." Its walls were lined with reliefs of his siege of Lachish in Judah, now in the British Museum. His own prism records, in his own boasting words, that he took 46 walled cities of Judah and shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." He lists the tribute, including 30 talents of gold, the same figure as 2 Kings 18:14. What the prism does not claim is just as important. He never says he took Jerusalem. The Bible tells why: "the angel of the LORD went out, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians an hundred fourscore and five thousand" (2 Kings 19:35). Classical Herodotus (2.141) heard a garbled Egyptian version in which field mice gnawed through the bowstrings and shield straps of Sennacherib's army overnight.

Scripture Artifact Murdered in the House of His God (681 BC)

"As he was worshipping in the house of Nisroch his god, that Adrammelech and Sharezer his sons smote him with the sword: and they escaped into the land of Armenia. And Esarhaddon his son reigned in his stead" (2 Kings 19:37). The Babylonian Chronicle confirms that Sennacherib was killed by his son in a rebellion, and Esarhaddon's own inscriptions describe his brothers' revolt. In 1980 the Assyriologist Simo Parpola identified the murderer from an Assyrian letter as Arda-Mulissu, a name that fits the Bible's Adrammelech. The god Nisroch has not been identified with certainty; Nusku and Ninurta are the usual suggestions. Esarhaddon's own records list "Manasseh king of Judah" among his vassals, which agrees with 2 Chronicles 33:11, where Manasseh is carried off in fetters by the captains of the king of Assyria.

Artifact Ashurbanipal and His Library

Ashurbanipal (668-about 631 BC) was the last great king of Assyria. He collected tens of thousands of clay tablets at Nineveh, including the Gilgamesh Epic, the Flood tablet, and the Babylonian creation story. These were dug up by Layard and Hormuzd Rassam between 1849 and 1853, and George Smith read the Flood account among them in 1872. His palace walls show him killing lions from his chariot and on foot, the mighty hunter to the very end. In 663 BC his army sacked Thebes, the greatest city of Egypt.

The Burden of Nineveh

Nahum and the Fall of 612 BC

Scripture Nahum's Prophecy

Nahum wrote after the fall of Thebes, which he mentions ("Art thou better than populous No?" 3:8), and before Nineveh fell. He warned, "no more of thy name be sown: out of the house of thy gods will I cut off the graven image and the molten image: I will make thy grave; for thou art vile" (1:14). He said, "The gates of the rivers shall be opened, and the palace shall be dissolved" (2:6). He called Nineveh "the wellfavoured harlot, the mistress of witchcrafts, that selleth nations through her whoredoms" (3:4). Zephaniah added that Nineveh was "the rejoicing city that dwelt carelessly, that said in her heart, I am, and there is none beside me" (Zephaniah 2:15).

Artifact The Babylonian Chronicle

A Babylonian chronicle tablet in the British Museum (published in 1923) records how the Babylonians under Nabopolassar and the Medes under Cyaxares besieged Nineveh and took it in the summer of 612 BC. A remnant of the Assyrian army held out at Haran until 609 BC. That is why Pharaoh Necho was marching north in 609 when King Josiah met him at Megiddo and was killed (2 Kings 23:29). The chronicles show Egypt was going to help Assyria, and the Hebrew preposition in that verse can mean "to" the king of Assyria as easily as "against" him.

Classical The River Became Her Enemy

Diodorus (2.26-27), following Ctesias, tells a legend of the last Assyrian king, Sardanapalus. An oracle said Nineveh would never fall until the river became its enemy. In the third year of the siege, the river flooded and broke down a long stretch of the wall, and the king burned himself in his palace with his wives and treasure. The details are legendary, and scholars think the tale blends the name Ashurbanipal with the fiery death of his brother Shamash-shum-ukin at Babylon in 648 BC. Still, the flooding river echoes Nahum 2:6 in a striking way.

✦ Bonus: The City That Was Forgotten

Nahum said Nineveh's name would not be sown again, and for over two thousand years it very nearly vanished. When the Greek soldier Xenophon marched past its ruins in 401 BC, only two centuries after its fall, he described the great mounds but did not know whose city it had been (Anabasis 3.4). In the second century AD, the satirist Lucian had Hermes say that Nineveh was so completely gone that no one could even tell where it had stood. Skeptics in the early 1800s wondered whether the Bible had exaggerated the place. Then Botta dug at Khorsabad in 1843 and Layard at Nimrud in 1845 and at Nineveh itself in 1847. The palaces, the reliefs, and the library came out of the ground. The city that once said "I am, and there is none beside me" had to be dug up to be believed.

Theory Nineveh's patron goddess was Ishtar of Nineveh, goddess of love, war, and divination. Some commentators suggest that Nahum's image of Nineveh as a seductive harlot and "mistress of witchcrafts" takes deliberate aim at the city's goddess. The text doesn't name her, so this is a reasonable reading rather than a certainty. It does fit the pattern we see in Isaiah 47 and Revelation 17 and 18, where a great city is pictured as a queen and a harlot.

2 Kings 17:33 They feared the LORD, and served their own gods, after the manner of the nations whom they carried away from thence.
Nahum 1:14 And the LORD hath given a commandment concerning thee, that no more of thy name be sown: out of the house of thy gods will I cut off the graven image and the molten image: I will make thy grave; for thou art vile.
Zephaniah 2:15 This is the rejoicing city that dwelt carelessly, that said in her heart, I am, and there is none beside me: how is she become a desolation, a place for beasts to lie down in!
XV.

The Queen of Heaven and Tammuz in Jerusalem

Everything traced so far came home to Jerusalem in the last century before the exile. The goddess of Uruk and Nineveh, the weeping for the dying shepherd, and the worship of the sun and stars were practiced in the streets of the holy city and inside the courts of the temple. The prophets name these things plainly, and this is where the Bible's own phrase "the queen of heaven" appears.

697-642 BC

Manasseh Brings the Gods Into the Temple

Scripture The Worst King of Judah

Manasseh, a vassal of Assyria, "built up again the high places which Hezekiah his father had destroyed; and he reared up altars for Baal, and made a grove, as did Ahab king of Israel; and worshipped all the host of heaven, and served them" (2 Kings 21:3). He built altars to the stars in both courts of the temple, burned his son in the fire, practiced sorcery and spiritism, "and he set a graven image of the grove that he had made in the house" (21:4-7). The Chronicler records that he later repented in captivity and removed "the idol out of the house of the LORD" (2 Chronicles 33:12-15). But the people he had taught did not turn back with him.

622 BC

Josiah Cleans House

Scripture What Josiah Found

When the book of the law was found in the eighteenth year of Josiah (2 Kings 22:3-8), the king began a reform. The list of what he removed shows how far things had gone. He brought out of the temple "all the vessels that were made for Baal, and for the grove, and for all the host of heaven" (23:4). He removed the priests who "burned incense unto Baal, to the sun, and to the moon, and to the planets, and to all the host of heaven" (23:5); the word for planets likely means the constellations of the zodiac. He broke down the houses of the cult prostitutes "that were by the house of the LORD, where the women wove hangings for the grove" (23:7). He took away "the horses that the kings of Judah had given to the sun" and "burned the chariots of the sun with fire" (23:11). He defiled the high places "which Solomon the king of Israel had builded for Ashtoreth the abomination of the Zidonians" (23:13), which had stood for more than three hundred years.

Artifact Theory What the Ground Shows

Archaeologists have found hundreds of small clay "pillar figurines" of a woman in Judah, mostly from the 700s and 600s BC. Many scholars connect them with Asherah or another goddess, though their exact meaning is debated. In Jerusalem, clay horse figurines were found, some with a disk on the head, which many interpret as sun symbols in light of 2 Kings 23:11. An older cult stand from Taanach (900s BC) shows an animal, probably a horse, beneath a winged sun disk. These objects don't prove every detail, but they fit the picture Kings gives.

About 609-586 BC

Cakes for the Queen of Heaven

Scripture A Family Religion

Josiah's reform didn't last. Under his sons, Jeremiah stood in the temple gate and described what he saw: "The children gather wood, and the fathers kindle the fire, and the women knead their dough, to make cakes to the queen of heaven, and to pour out drink offerings unto other gods, that they may provoke me to anger" (Jeremiah 7:18). The whole family was involved.

Scripture The Argument in Egypt

After Jerusalem fell, a group of Jews fled to Egypt and settled at Migdol, Tahpanhes, Noph, and "the country of Pathros," which is Upper Egypt (Jeremiah 44:1). When Jeremiah rebuked them, they answered him directly: "we will certainly... burn incense unto the queen of heaven, and... pour out drink offerings unto her, as we have done, we, and our fathers, our kings, and our princes... for then had we plenty of victuals, and were well, and saw no evil. But since we left off to burn incense to the queen of heaven... we have wanted all things, and have been consumed by the sword and by the famine" (44:17-18). The women added, "did we make her cakes to worship her... without our men?" (44:19).

Notice their reasoning. "Since we left off" most likely points to Josiah's reform. They were arguing that Judah's disasters came from abandoning the goddess, not from abandoning the Lord. Jeremiah gave God's answer: "Because ye have burned incense, and because ye have sinned against the LORD... therefore this evil is happened unto you, as at this day" (44:23).

Artifact The Cakes Were Ishtar's Cakes

The Hebrew word for these cakes, kawwanim, is not a normal Hebrew word for bread. Scholars have matched it with the Akkadian kamanu, a sweet cake offered to Ishtar in Mesopotamian rituals. The phrase "to worship her" in 44:19 can also be read "to make an image of her," which suggests cakes shaped like the goddess or stamped with her image. Clay baking molds from the palace kitchen at Mari (1700s BC) include some shaped like a woman. The cake vocabulary points straight back to Mesopotamia.

Artifact Theory Who Was She?

Scripture gives her a title, not a personal name. The title fits several goddesses. In the Amarna letters, the king of Mitanni calls Ishtar "mistress of heaven." Egyptian inscriptions from the 1300s and 1200s BC call Astarte "lady of heaven." Inanna's own name means "Lady of Heaven." The strongest current view (Susan Ackerman, 1992) is that the Queen of Heaven in Jeremiah was a blend of Mesopotamian Ishtar and Canaanite Astarte, the Ashtoreth of Solomon's high place. That matches everything the Bible says: a sky goddess, honored with cakes, incense, and drink offerings, and worshipped especially by women.

Artifact She Followed Them to Egypt

An Aramaic letter found at Hermopolis in Egypt, written around 500 BC, sends greetings to a temple of the Queen of Heaven at Syene (modern Aswan), in the very region Jeremiah calls Pathros. On the island of Elephantine, right across the river, a Jewish military colony of the 400s BC had its own temple to "Yahu." Its papyri mention offerings for other deities alongside him, and one text speaks of "Anat-Yahu," pairing a goddess with the Lord's name. A century after Jeremiah, the descendants of the people he warned were still mixing the worship of the Lord with a goddess.

592 BC

Ezekiel's Tour of the Temple

Scripture Four Abominations, Each Worse Than the Last

In the sixth year of the exile (Ezekiel 8:1), the prophet was carried in vision from Babylon to Jerusalem and shown four scenes:

The image of jealousy. At the north gate stood "the seat of the image of jealousy, which provoketh to jealousy" (8:3, 5).
The seventy elders. Behind a hidden wall, seventy elders of Israel offered incense before "every form of creeping things, and abominable beasts, and all the idols of the house of Israel, pourtrayed upon the wall." Their excuse was, "The LORD seeth us not; the LORD hath forsaken the earth" (8:10-12).
The weeping women. "Then he brought me to the door of the gate of the LORD'S house which was toward the north; and, behold, there sat women weeping for Tammuz" (8:14). This is the only place the name Tammuz appears in Scripture. It is the same Dumuzi mourned in Mesopotamia for over a thousand years, as traced in Section IX.
The sun worshippers. Between the porch and the altar, about twenty-five men stood "with their backs toward the temple of the LORD, and their faces toward the east; and they worshipped the sun toward the east" (8:16). They literally turned their backs on God to face the sunrise.

Scripture Theory The Image of Jealousy

The Hebrew word Ezekiel uses for this "image," semel, appears only five times in the Old Testament. Two of those are in 2 Chronicles 33:7 and 15, describing the idol Manasseh set in the temple, which Kings calls the image of the grove, meaning Asherah. Many scholars conclude that the image of jealousy was an Asherah image, set back up after Manasseh's removal of it. The word link is solid; the identification is a strong inference.

Scripture The Glory Departs

Ezekiel then watched the glory of the Lord leave the temple in stages: from the cherub to the threshold (9:3), from the threshold to the east gate (10:18-19), and finally "from the midst of the city" to "the mountain which is on the east side of the city" (11:23). Six years later, in 586 BC, Nebuchadnezzar's army burned the temple (2 Kings 25:8-9). Yet Ezekiel also saw the glory return: "the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east... And the glory of the LORD came into the house" (43:2-4).

The same direction runs through Ezekiel 8 and 11. The sun worshippers faced east with their backs to God's house, and the glory of God left toward the east. They wanted the sunrise more than the Lord, and He gave them what they chose. Paul describes the same exchange in Romans 1:25: they "worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator."

XVI.

Babylon the Great: Bel, Nebo, and Nebuchadnezzar

The story began at Babel, and it returns there. When Nineveh fell, Babylon became the capital of the world. Its god Marduk, called Bel, stood at the head of the gods, and its king Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt the city on a scale that amazed the Greeks. This is the Babylon of Daniel, Isaiah, and Jeremiah, and it is the Babylon whose name the book of Revelation takes up at the end of the Bible.

The Gods of Babylon

Marduk Who Is Bel, and Nabu Who Is Nebo

Artifact Marduk Rises With Hammurabi

Marduk began as the local god of the city of Babylon. When Hammurabi (1792-1750 BC) made Babylon the center of an empire, Marduk rose with it. The prologue to Hammurabi's law code says the great gods gave Marduk rule over all mankind. Over the centuries he was called simply Bel, "Lord," the same word as the Canaanite Ba'al. Scripture uses both names: "Bel is confounded, Merodach is broken in pieces" (Jeremiah 50:2). His name appears in the names of Babylonian kings the Bible mentions, such as Merodach-baladan (Isaiah 39:1) and Evil-merodach (2 Kings 25:27). His sacred animal was the mushhushshu, a scaly dragon with a serpent's head.

Artifact Nabu, Scribe of the Gods

Nabu was Marduk's son, the god of writing and wisdom, whose temple stood in the neighboring city of Borsippa. The ruins of Borsippa's ziggurat are called Birs Nimrud today, one more place named for Nimrod by later tradition. Nabu's name is built into the names of Babylonian kings: Nabopolassar, Nebuchadnezzar (Nabu-kudurri-usur, "O Nabu, protect my heir"), and Nabonidus. Isaiah mocked both gods together: "Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth, their idols were upon the beasts, and upon the cattle" (Isaiah 46:1). The gods Babylon carried in its processions would soon be carried off as baggage.

Scripture Theory The Renaming of Daniel and His Friends

When Daniel and his friends were taken to Babylon in 605 BC, their names were changed (Daniel 1:7). Every one of their Hebrew names honored the true God: Daniel ("God is my judge"), Hananiah ("the LORD is gracious"), Mishael ("who is what God is?"), and Azariah ("the LORD has helped"). The new names replaced God with Babylon's gods. Nebuchadnezzar himself says Daniel was named Belteshazzar "according to the name of my god" (4:8), which points to Bel. Abed-nego is generally understood as "servant of Nabu," with the god's name slightly altered. The meanings of Shadrach and Meshach are less certain. Babylon tried to rename God's people, but Daniel "purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself" (1:8).

The Babylonian Creation Story

Enuma Elish: Marduk Slays the Sea

Artifact The Story

Enuma Elish ("When on high") tells how the gods were born from the mingling of fresh water (Apsu) and salt water (Tiamat). When Tiamat raged against the younger gods, Marduk agreed to fight her if they would make him king. He killed her, split her body in two like a dried fish, and made the sky from one half and the earth from the other. Man was made from the blood of a rebel god to do the gods' work. The poem ends with the gods proclaiming Marduk's fifty names. It was recited at Babylon's New Year festival. Copies came from Ashurbanipal's library, and George Smith published it in 1876 as The Chaldean Account of Genesis. Its date is debated, and many scholars place its composition around 1100 BC. In Assyria, scribes simply replaced Marduk's name with Ashur's.

Scripture Genesis Answers Babylon

Genesis 1:2 speaks of "the deep" (tehom), a word related to Tiamat. But in Genesis the deep is not a goddess, there is no battle, and nothing has to be killed. God speaks, and it is done. The sun and moon, which Babylon worshipped, are not even named in Genesis 1. They are called "the greater light" and "the lesser light" (1:16), lamps God hung in the sky. Man is not made from a dead god's blood to be a slave, but "in the image of God" (1:27) and blessed. Whether or not Moses had Babylon in view, Genesis 1 reads like a flat denial of everything Enuma Elish claims.

605-562 BC

Nebuchadnezzar's Babylon

Artifact Scripture The King the Bible Knows

Nebuchadnezzar II defeated Egypt at Carchemish in 605 BC (Jeremiah 46:2) and took the first captives from Jerusalem that same year (Daniel 1:1). The Babylonian Chronicle records that in 597 BC he "seized the city" of Judah on the second day of the month Adar, captured its king, and appointed a king of his own choice, exactly as 2 Kings 24:10-17 says. Ration tablets found in Babylon list oil for "Jehoiachin, king of Judah," and his five sons, matching 2 Kings 25:27-30. In 586 BC his army burned the temple.

Artifact The Ishtar Gate

Nebuchadnezzar rebuilt Babylon with double walls, the great temple of Marduk (Esagila), and the tower Etemenanki. The northern entrance was the Ishtar Gate, faced with blue glazed brick and decorated with rows of Marduk's dragons and the bulls of the storm god Adad. The Processional Way leading to it was lined with lions, the animal of Ishtar. German excavators under Robert Koldewey uncovered it between 1899 and 1917, and it now stands reconstructed in Berlin. Millions of Nebuchadnezzar's bricks are stamped with his name and titles.

Scripture "Is Not This Great Babylon?"

Walking on his palace roof, the king said, "Is not this great Babylon, that I have built for the house of the kingdom by the might of my power, and for the honour of my majesty?" (Daniel 4:30). That is the spirit of Babel again: "let us build us a city... and let us make us a name" (Genesis 11:4). God humbled him until he learned "that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men" (4:32). His last recorded words in Scripture are a confession: "Now I Nebuchadnezzar praise and extol and honour the King of heaven... and those that walk in pride he is able to abase" (4:37).

The Prophets Against Babylon

The Lady of Kingdoms and the Morning Star

Scripture A City That Spoke Like a Goddess

Isaiah 47 pictures Babylon as a proud queen. She was called "The lady of kingdoms" (47:5). She said, "I shall be a lady for ever" (47:7), and "I am, and none else beside me; I shall not sit as a widow, neither shall I know the loss of children" (47:8). Those words "I am, and none else" are God's own words from two chapters earlier: "I am the LORD, and there is none else" (45:5-6). Babylon took God's name for herself. Revelation 18:7 repeats the same boast: "I sit a queen, and am no widow, and shall see no sorrow."

Scripture Theory Lucifer, Son of the Morning

Isaiah 14 is a taunt "against the king of Babylon" (14:4). In verse 12 he is called "Lucifer, son of the morning." The Hebrew is helel ben shachar, "shining one, son of the dawn," which is the morning star, the planet Venus. "Lucifer" is the Latin name for the morning star, which the King James took from the Latin Bible. The king's boast goes beyond anything an earthly ruler could do: "I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God: I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north... I will be like the most High" (14:13-14). The "sides of the north" is Zaphon, the mountain of Ba'al (Section XI). In Babylon, Venus was Ishtar's star. Many interpreters, from the early church onward, have seen Satan himself behind this king. Whatever view one takes, the Bible's answer is clear: the true morning star is not Ishtar's planet or a proud king. Jesus says, "I am the root and the offspring of David, and the bright and morning star" (Revelation 22:16).

Scripture Bel Will Give Back What He Swallowed

"I will punish Bel in Babylon, and I will bring forth out of his mouth that which he hath swallowed up: and the nations shall not flow together any more unto him: yea, the wall of Babylon shall fall" (Jeremiah 51:44). Babel had tried to keep the nations gathered in one place. Here God promises that the nations will stop flowing to Bel.

539 BC

The Night Babylon Fell

Artifact Scripture Belshazzar and Nabonidus

Babylon's last king, Nabonidus, spent years away in Arabia and favored the moon god Sin of Haran. The Nabonidus Chronicle notes that in those years the New Year festival was not held and "Bel did not come out" in procession. His son Belshazzar ruled in Babylon in his place. For a long time critics said Belshazzar never existed, since Greek historians didn't name him. Then in 1854 cylinders of Nabonidus found at Ur were read, and they name Belshazzar as his eldest son. That also explains why Belshazzar could only offer Daniel the rank of "third ruler in the kingdom" (Daniel 5:16, 29). He himself was second.

Classical Scripture The River and the Feast

Herodotus (1.191) says Cyrus diverted the Euphrates, and his men walked into the city along the riverbed while the Babylonians were celebrating a festival. Xenophon tells a similar story. Daniel 5 describes Belshazzar's feast that same night, with the temple vessels from Jerusalem used to praise "the gods of gold, and of silver" (5:4). "In that night was Belshazzar the king of the Chaldeans slain" (5:30). Jeremiah had foretold both details: "I will dry up her sea" (51:36), and "In their heat I will make their feasts, and I will make them drunken... and sleep a perpetual sleep" (51:39).

Artifact Scripture Called by Name

The Cyrus Cylinder (found in 1879) claims that Marduk searched for a righteous king, called Cyrus by name, and sent him to take Babylon. Isaiah had written it the true way around, long before: "Thus saith the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus... I have even called thee by thy name: I have surnamed thee, though thou hast not known me. I am the LORD, and there is none else" (Isaiah 45:1, 4-5). Marduk's priests gave their god the credit. Scripture had already named the God who actually did it.

✦ Bonus: How "Belus, Father of Ninus" Was Born

When Herodotus wrote about Babylon around 430 BC, he described "the sanctuary of Zeus Belus" (1.181-183), with its tower of eight stages and a golden statue of the god. To a Greek, the chief god of any city was "Zeus," and the local name for him was Bel, so he wrote "Zeus Belus." In the same book (1.7), Herodotus mentions an early king list that began with "Ninus son of Belus."

Put those together and the legend comes apart. "Belus" is simply Bel, the title of Marduk, "the Lord." "Ninus" is the city of Nineveh turned into a man. The famous genealogy "Ninus son of Belus" amounts to "Nineveh, child of the Lord Bel." Ctesias and Diodorus built a family and a history on that, Christian chroniclers matched it to Nimrod, and Isidore of Seville (around AD 630) taught that idolatry began when Ninus had a golden statue made of his father Belus and worshipped it. That story was repeated through the Middle Ages. It is a tidy picture of how a god's title became a legendary king and then a supposed historical person.

Babylon's end came slowly. Alexander the Great died there in 323 BC, planning to rebuild it. The Greek city of Seleucia drew its people away, and by the time of Christ the geographer Strabo (16.1.5) quoted a line that "the great city is a great desert." In the 1980s Saddam Hussein rebuilt part of Nebuchadnezzar's palace and had the new bricks stamped with his own name, as Nebuchadnezzar had done. Some men still want a name at Babel.

XVII.

How the Legends Were Written

Most of what people "know" about Ninus, Semiramis, and Menones does not come from Assyria at all. It comes from a handful of Greek and Latin writers who lived centuries after the events and who never read cuneiform. Knowing who wrote what, and when, is the key to sorting fact from legend. It is also the best defense against both the skeptic who dismisses everything and the enthusiast who believes everything.

The Writers Behind the Legends

From Herodotus to Philo of Byblos

Classical Herodotus (about 430 BC)

The "Father of History" is the first Greek to mention Semiramis (1.184) and "Ninus son of Belus" (1.7). He mentions her only briefly, as a queen who built dikes on the Euphrates. He was an honest collector of stories who often told his readers when he doubted what he was told.

Classical Ctesias of Cnidus (about 400 BC)

Ctesias was a Greek physician at the Persian court of Artaxerxes II. He wrote a Persian History claiming to use royal records. He is the source of nearly the entire Ninus and Semiramis romance: the conquest of Asia, Menones hanging himself, the building of Babylon, the war with India, and the dove. He gave Assyria thirty generations of kings from Ninus to Sardanapalus, none of which match the real Assyrian king lists. Even in the ancient world he had a poor reputation. Aristotle called him untrustworthy (History of Animals 8.28), and Plutarch complained that he filled his books with fables. His work survives only in pieces quoted by others.

Classical Berossus (about 280 BC)

Berossus was a priest of Bel-Marduk at Babylon who wrote a history of Babylon in Greek, using the cuneiform records he could actually read. He is the source of the ten kings before the flood and the flood hero Xisuthros. Josephus preserves his complaint that the Greeks were wrong to credit Semiramis with building Babylon (Against Apion 1.142). In other words, the one ancient writer with access to Babylon's own records rejected the Semiramis legend.

Classical Manetho (about 280 BC)

An Egyptian priest who wrote the history of Egypt in Greek. His division of Egyptian history into thirty dynasties is still used today.

Classical Diodorus Siculus (about 60-30 BC)

A Greek historian from Sicily whose Library of History gathered earlier writers into one large work. His second book follows Ctesias almost entirely, and it is the main place the full legend of Ninus, Semiramis, and Menones survives. Diodorus is a collector, not an eyewitness, and he says so.

Classical Pompeius Trogus and Justin

Trogus, writing under Augustus, began his world history with Ninus, "the first" to wage wars of conquest. His work survives only in a later summary by Justin. This is the version where Semiramis disguises herself as her son.

Classical Philo of Byblos (about AD 100)

Philo claimed to translate the writings of an ancient Phoenician priest named Sanchuniathon. His work survives in quotations by Eusebius (Preparation for the Gospel 1.10). He treats the gods as ancient men and matches Phoenician gods with Greek ones: El is Kronos, and the Lord of Heaven is Zeus. Scholars once dismissed him as an inventor, but after the Ugarit tablets were found, many of his names (El, Elioun, Dagon, Mot) turned out to be genuine Canaanite deities. He even says Dagon was the god of grain, which fits the Semitic word dagan, "grain."

Two Habits That Shaped Everything

Matching Gods and Making Men Into Gods

Classical "Their God Is Our God": Interpretatio Graeca

Greeks traveling abroad assumed that foreign gods were their own gods under other names. Herodotus says the Assyrians call Aphrodite Mylitta and the Arabians call her Alilat (1.131). He says Amun is Zeus, Isis is Demeter, Osiris is Dionysus, and Horus is Apollo (2.42, 2.59, 2.144). Tacitus later calls this "the Roman interpretation" (Germania 43). This is why the same goddess seems to appear in every country. The ancients themselves matched them up, and the matching was based on similar roles, not on a common history.

Classical Euhemerus and the Deified Kings

Around 300 BC, Euhemerus of Messene wrote a travel tale claiming to have found a golden pillar on a distant island recording that Zeus, Kronos, and the others were once human kings who were worshipped after death. The Roman poet Ennius translated him into Latin. The idea is called euhemerism, and early Christian writers used it gladly. Lactantius (Divine Institutes 1.11) cites Euhemerus and Ennius to argue that Jupiter was only a mortal king.

Tradition The Christian Chroniclers

Once Christians began writing world histories, they needed to fit the Greek king lists alongside Genesis. Eusebius and Jerome placed Ninus in the time of Abraham. The Clementine Recognitions identified Ninus with Nimrod. Malalas made Kronos the first king of Assyria. Isidore of Seville and Peter Comestor taught that idolatry began with Ninus's statue of Belus. Snorri Sturluson made Thor the grandson of Priam of Troy. All of these are euhemerism, the attempt to explain the gods as deified men. They show real insight into how idolatry works, but they are not records from the time. They are later scholars connecting dots.

Tradition The Wisdom of Solomon

The same idea appears in the apocryphal Wisdom of Solomon, written by a Jewish author in Egypt before Christ: "For a father afflicted with untimely mourning, when he hath made an image of his child soon taken away, now honoured him as a god, which was then a dead man" (14:15). It goes on to describe how the images of distant kings were honored until "the multitude... took him now for a god, which a little before was but honoured as a man" (14:20). It is not Scripture, but it is an old and thoughtful Jewish explanation that fits Romans 1.

What Scripture Says About Where the Gods Came From

The Bible gives a fuller answer than any of these writers. It names three roots of idolatry, and all three are visible in this study.

Man glorifying man. "They... changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man" (Romans 1:23). The mighty men who sought a name (Genesis 6:4, 11:4) and the kings who called themselves divine are this root. Euhemerus saw this part of the truth.
Man worshipping creation. "Lest thou lift up thine eyes unto heaven, and when thou seest the sun, and the moon, and the stars, even all the host of heaven, shouldest be driven to worship them" (Deuteronomy 4:19). Ishtar as Venus, Shamash as the sun, and Ba'al as the storm are this root. Paul says they "worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator" (Romans 1:25).
Demons receiving the worship. "The things which the Gentiles sacrifice, they sacrifice to devils, and not to God" (1 Corinthians 10:20; see Deuteronomy 32:17). The idol itself is nothing (1 Corinthians 8:4), but behind it are real spiritual powers who are glad to receive what belongs to God. This is why Scripture treats idolatry as spiritual adultery and not merely as a mistake.

All three can be true at once. A real king could be remembered, linked to a star, and then used by fallen spirits as a mask. That is why no single theory, whether "they were only men," "they were only nature," or "they were only demons," explains all the evidence. Scripture holds all three together.

Romans 1:22-23 Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools, and changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man, and to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things.
1 Corinthians 8:5-6 For though there be that are called gods, whether in heaven or in earth, (as there be gods many, and lords many,) but to us there is but one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we in him; and one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things, and we by him.
1 Corinthians 10:20 But I say, that the things which the Gentiles sacrifice, they sacrifice to devils, and not to God: and I would not that ye should have fellowship with devils.
XVIII.

Rome: Latin Names, and Babylon Moves West

Rome conquered the world that Assyria and Babylon had ruled, and it gathered that world's gods into its own temples. By the time of the apostles, the Queen of Heaven, the dying consort, and the storm god were all being worshipped in Rome under Latin names, and emperors were being declared gods. That is why the New Testament could take the old name Babylon and apply it to the city on seven hills.

The Roman Pantheon

Greek Gods With Latin Names

Classical The Borrowed Family

Rome had its own old gods, but it matched them with the Greek gods so completely that the two families became one. Jupiter is Zeus, Juno is Hera, Venus is Aphrodite, Mars is Ares, Mercury is Hermes, Saturn is Kronos, Diana is Artemis, Minerva is Athena, and Pluto is Hades. The name Jupiter comes from an old form meaning "sky father," and Zeus has the same root, which is why linguists see the two as one inherited god rather than a later borrowing.

Classical Juno Regina, the Queen

The great temple on the Capitoline Hill, dedicated in 509 BC, honored three gods: Jupiter Best and Greatest, Juno Regina ("Juno the Queen"), and Minerva. In 396 BC, when Rome besieged the city of Veii, the Roman general formally invited Veii's Juno to abandon her city and come to Rome, and she was given a temple on the Aventine (Livy 5.21-22). Rome was already a city that collected queens of heaven.

Classical Venus the Mother and the Trojan Ancestors

The Romans claimed descent from Troy. Virgil's Aeneid tells how Aeneas, son of Venus, escaped burning Troy and came to Italy, and Julius Caesar's family claimed Aeneas's son Iulus as its ancestor. In 46 BC Caesar dedicated a temple to Venus Genetrix, "Venus the Mother." This is the same Trojan-origin idea Snorri used for the Norse gods (Section XII). The Franks, and later the Britons through Geoffrey of Monmouth's Brutus (about 1136), made the same claim. Troy became the favorite royal family tree of Europe.

Classical Saturn and the Golden Age

Saturn was matched with Kronos, and Philo of Byblos had already matched Kronos with El. Virgil says Saturn fled from Jupiter and hid in Latium, where he ruled a golden age of peace (Aeneid 8.319-325). His festival, the Saturnalia, began on December 17. It was a time of feasting, gift giving, and masters serving their slaves. His name survives in Saturday.

The Eastern Goddesses Come to Rome

Cybele, Isis, and Caelestis

Classical The Great Mother (204 BC)

During the war with Hannibal, Rome's sacred books advised bringing the Great Mother of the gods from Asia Minor. In 204 BC her sacred black stone was brought from Pessinus and installed in Rome, and she received a temple on the Palatine (Livy 29.10-14). Her consort was Attis, the young shepherd who died and was mourned, one more version of the Tammuz pattern (Section IX). Her priests castrated themselves in her service.

Classical Isis in Rome

The Senate tried several times to ban the worship of Isis in the first century BC. In AD 19, after a scandal in her temple, the emperor Tiberius crucified her priests and threw her statue into the Tiber (Josephus, Antiquities 18.65-80). It didn't work. Later emperors built her new temples, and she became one of the most popular deities in the empire, often shown nursing the infant Horus.

Classical "Queen of Heaven" in Latin

In the novel Metamorphoses (also called The Golden Ass) by Apuleius, written around AD 160, the hero prays to the goddess as Regina caeli, "Queen of heaven," not sure whether to call her Ceres, heavenly Venus, Diana, or Proserpina (11.2). The goddess appears and answers that the Phrygians call her the Mother of the gods, the Athenians Minerva, the Cyprians Venus, the Cretans Diana, the Sicilians Proserpina, the Eleusinians Ceres, and others Juno, Bellona, Hecate, and Rhamnusia. But the Egyptians, she says, call her by her true name, Queen Isis (11.5). Here is a pagan writer saying in plain words that the goddesses of many nations are one goddess with many names.

Classical Tradition Caelestis, the Heavenly One

At Carthage, the Phoenician goddess Tanit was called Caelestis, "the Heavenly One," and matched with Juno. Her cult spread to Rome. Augustine, who grew up in North Africa, remembered watching obscene shows performed in honor of "the virgin Caelestis" when he was young (City of God 2.4).

✦ Bonus: The Title Comes Back

Around AD 375, Epiphanius of Salamis described a group of women in Arabia, later called Collyridians, who decorated a chair or cart, set a small loaf before it on a certain day of the year, and offered it in the name of Mary (Panarion 79). Epiphanius condemned the practice and connected it with the cakes for the queen of heaven in Jeremiah. His rule was that Mary should be honored but not worshipped, since worship belongs to the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit alone. The title Regina caeli was later used of Mary in medieval Latin hymns. In Scripture, the only one ever called "queen of heaven" is the goddess of Jeremiah 7 and 44, and God condemned her worship.

Gods in Human Form

Emperors, Apostles, and a King Eaten by Worms

Artifact Classical The Divine Emperors

After Julius Caesar was murdered, a comet appeared, and in 42 BC the Senate officially declared him a god, Divus Julius. His adopted son Augustus put "son of the god" (divi filius) on his coins. In 29 BC the province of Asia built a temple to Rome and Augustus at Pergamum. Theory When Jesus tells the church at Pergamos that it dwells "where Satan's seat is" (Revelation 2:13), interpreters point to the emperor cult, the great altar of Zeus, or the temple of the healing god Asclepius, all of which stood there.

Scripture Classical "The Gods Are Come Down"

At Lystra, after Paul healed a lame man, the crowd shouted, "The gods are come down to us in the likeness of men. And they called Barnabas, Jupiter; and Paul, Mercurius, because he was the chief speaker" (Acts 14:11-12). The priest of Jupiter brought oxen and garlands to sacrifice to them. Ovid had told a legend that Jupiter and Mercury once visited neighboring Phrygia disguised as travelers, and only an old couple named Baucis and Philemon took them in (Metamorphoses 8). The crowd was not going to miss the gods a second time. Paul and Barnabas tore their clothes and cried, "We also are men of like passions with you, and preach unto you that ye should turn from these vanities unto the living God" (14:15). They refused the very honor Naram-Sin and the emperors reached for.

Scripture Classical Herod Accepts It

Herod Agrippa I did the opposite. When the crowd at Caesarea shouted, "It is the voice of a god, and not of a man," he accepted it, "and immediately the angel of the Lord smote him, because he gave not God the glory: and he was eaten of worms, and gave up the ghost" (Acts 12:22-23). Josephus tells the same story independently. He says Herod wore a robe woven of silver that glittered in the morning sun, his flatterers called him a god, he did not rebuke them, and he was seized with violent pains and died five days later (Antiquities 19.343-350).

The Name Moves West

Rome as Babylon

Scripture Tradition "The Church That Is at Babylon"

Peter closes his first letter, "The church that is at Babylon, elected together with you, saluteth you" (1 Peter 5:13). Early church writers quoted by Eusebius understood this as a coded name for Rome, where Peter was writing (Church History 2.15). Jewish writers after AD 70 also called Rome "Babylon," because Rome, like Babylon, had destroyed the temple.

Scripture Artifact Mystery, Babylon the Great

John saw a woman with a name on her forehead: "MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT, THE MOTHER OF HARLOTS AND ABOMINATIONS OF THE EARTH" (Revelation 17:5). He is told, "The seven heads are seven mountains, on which the woman sitteth" (17:9), and "the woman which thou sawest is that great city, which reigneth over the kings of the earth" (17:18). In John's day only one city fit that description. A bronze coin of the emperor Vespasian from AD 71 shows the goddess Roma seated on seven hills. The woman's boast, "I sit a queen, and am no widow" (18:7), is the boast of Babylon in Isaiah 47:7-8. How this prophecy works out at the end of the age is where interpreters differ. What John's first readers would have recognized is not in doubt.

The line runs straight through Scripture. Babel sought a name (Genesis 11:4). Nineveh said, "I am, and there is none beside me" (Zephaniah 2:15). Babylon said, "I shall be a lady for ever" (Isaiah 47:7). Rome said, "I sit a queen" (Revelation 18:7). The city changes, but the voice is the same. And the answer is the same: "Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins" (Revelation 18:4).

XIX.

The North: Snorri's Gods and Our Weekdays

The Norse gods seem a world away from Babylon, but they are closer than most people realize. Every English speaker names three weekdays after them, and those names were chosen by matching them with Roman gods, who had been matched with Greek gods, who had been matched with Babylonian planet gods. Before tracing that chain, it helps to know where our knowledge of the Norse gods actually comes from.

The Sources

Written Down by Christians, Long After

Classical Tacitus (AD 98)

The earliest description of Germanic religion comes from the Roman historian Tacitus. In his Germania (9), he says the Germans worship Mercury above all, and on certain days offer him human sacrifices. They appease Hercules and Mars with animal offerings, and part of the Suebi tribe also sacrifices to Isis. Tacitus was using Roman names for Germanic gods. Scholars agree that his "Mercury" is Woden (Odin), and his "Hercules" is most likely Thor. Isis worship among the Germans shows how far the Queen of Heaven had traveled even then.

Tradition Adam of Bremen (about 1075)

As Section XI showed, Adam of Bremen described the temple at Uppsala in Sweden with statues of Thor, Woden, and Fricco (Frey). He says Thor ruled the air, thunder, rain, and crops, and that he seemed to resemble Jupiter. Every nine years, nine males of every kind of creature, including men, were sacrificed and hung in the sacred grove.

Tradition The Eddas and Sagas (1200s)

Iceland became Christian around AD 1000. More than two hundred years later, Snorri Sturluson, a Christian scholar and politician, wrote the Prose Edda (about 1220) as a handbook for poets. Its prologue is where Thor is made the grandson of Priam of Troy (Section XII). In his Ynglinga saga, Snorri says Odin was a chieftain from Asaland, east of the river Don, whose chief city was Asgard, and that he led his people north into Sweden. The poems of the Poetic Edda are older in origin, but they survive mainly in one manuscript, the Codex Regius, written about 1270. The Danish historian Saxo Grammaticus (about 1200) also treats Odin as a man and a sorcerer. So what we call "Norse mythology" was written down by Christians looking back at the old religion, and it has to be read with that in mind.

The Stories

Giants, a World Serpent, and a Dying God

Tradition Made From a Giant's Body

Snorri says the first being was the giant Ymir. Odin and his brothers killed him, and so much blood flowed that it drowned all the frost giants except Bergelmir and his wife, who escaped in a boat (Section II). The gods made the earth from Ymir's flesh, the sea from his blood, and the sky from his skull. This is strikingly close to Marduk making heaven and earth from Tiamat's body in Enuma Elish (Section XVI). In both stories the world is made from the corpse of a slain enemy.

Tradition Theory Gods and Giants Intermarry

In Norse tradition the gods are partly giant themselves. Odin's mother, Bestla, was the daughter of a giant. Frey married the giantess Gerd, and Njord married the giantess Skadi. Some readers see a distant echo here of the sons of God and the daughters of men in Genesis 6. That comparison is only a suggestion, and nothing in the Norse sources ties it to Genesis. It is still interesting that a people at the far edge of the world told stories of gods mating with giants and of a great race of giants destroyed by a flood.

Tradition Thor and the World Serpent

Thor's great enemy is Jörmungandr, the serpent that lies in the sea around the world. In the poem Hymiskviða, Thor goes fishing with an ox head for bait and hooks the serpent. At Ragnarök, the end of the world, they kill each other: Thor strikes it dead, walks nine steps, and falls dead from its venom. This is the same "storm god against the sea serpent" pattern found across the ancient world: Ba'al against Yam and Lotan, Marduk against Tiamat, Zeus against Typhon, and Ra against Apophis.

Scripture The Lord and Leviathan

Scripture uses the same imagery, but changes it completely. "Thou brakest the heads of leviathan in pieces" (Psalm 74:14). "In that day the LORD with his sore and great and strong sword shall punish leviathan the piercing serpent" (Isaiah 27:1). But in the Bible, Leviathan is a creature God made: "there is that leviathan, whom thou hast made to play therein" (Psalm 104:26). God never struggles with him, never needs an alliance, and never dies. Revelation reveals who the serpent really is: "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan" (Revelation 12:9), and it ends with him bound and cast into the lake of fire (20:2, 10). Thor dies killing his serpent. Christ died and rose again, and by His death He defeated "him that had the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14).

Tradition Theory Baldr

As Section IX described, Baldr, the beautiful son of Odin and Frigg, was killed by a mistletoe dart through Loki's trickery. The whole world wept to bring him back, but one giantess refused, so he stayed in the realm of Hel until after Ragnarök. The weeping recalls Tammuz and Adonis. Because Snorri was a Christian writing in the 1200s, many scholars also suspect that parts of Baldr's story were colored by the story of Christ. Either way, the Norse story ends with a dead god who cannot save anyone.

✦ Bonus: A Mold for Crosses and Hammers

At Trendgården in Denmark, archaeologists found a soapstone mold from the 900s AD that could cast both Christian crosses and Thor's hammer pendants at the same time. The smith who owned it was happy to sell to either customer. In the century when Scandinavia was turning to Christ, Thor's hammer became a sign people wore in answer to the cross. It is a small picture of what Elijah called halting "between two opinions" (1 Kings 18:21).

From Babylon to the Calendar

How the Weekdays Got Their Names

Artifact Step One: Babylon's Planet Gods

Babylonian astronomers watched seven moving lights: the sun, the moon, and five visible planets. Each was tied to a god. Jupiter was the star of Marduk, Venus the star of Ishtar, Mercury the star of Nabu, Mars the star of Nergal, and Saturn was often linked with Ninurta. The sun was Shamash and the moon was Sin. Saturn's Babylonian name was Kayyamanu, "the steady one," because it moves so slowly. That word appears in the Bible: "ye have borne the tabernacle of your Moloch and Chiun your images, the star of your god" (Amos 5:26). The Greek translation rendered Chiun as Rhaiphan, which Stephen quotes as "the star of your god Remphan" (Acts 7:43). Stephen applies the verse to Israel in the wilderness, so a Mesopotamian planet god was among the idols God held against His people.

Classical Step Two: The Greeks Rename the Planets

Greek astronomers took over the Babylonian planets along with their divine pairings and simply swapped in Greek gods: the star of Marduk became the star of Zeus, Ishtar's became Aphrodite's, Nabu's became Hermes', Nergal's became Ares', and Ninurta's became Kronos'. A work from Plato's school, the Epinomis (987), admits that the Greeks learned the planets from the "barbarians" of Syria and Egypt.

Classical Step Three: The Roman Planetary Week

In the Roman period, astrologers assigned each hour of the day to a planet, and each day took its name from the planet ruling its first hour. That produced the order still used today: the day of Saturn, the Sun, the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, and Venus. The historian Cassius Dio explained the method and said it had spread everywhere by his time (37.18-19). A wall painting at Pompeii shows the seven day-gods in order. In Latin the days were dies Saturni, Solis, Lunae, Martis, Mercurii, Iovis, Veneris, which is why French still has mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi.

Artifact Step Four: The Germanic Tribes Swap Again

When the Germanic peoples adopted the Roman week, they did exactly what Tacitus had done in reverse. They replaced the Roman gods with their own: Mars became Tiw, giving Tuesday. Mercury became Woden, giving Wednesday. Jupiter the thunderer became Thunor, or Thor, giving Thursday (German Donnerstag, "thunder's day"). Venus became Frigg (some say Freyja), giving Friday. They never found a match for Saturn, so Saturday kept his Roman name.

How Scripture Names the Days

The Bible never names a day after a god or a planet. In Genesis 1 the days are simply numbered: "the first day," "the second day," and so on. Only the seventh is given a name: "God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it" (Genesis 2:3), and it is called the Sabbath (Exodus 20:8-11). The New Testament keeps the same pattern: the resurrection took place on "the first day of the week" (Matthew 28:1). Hebrew still numbers the days today. Modern Greek does too, and calls Friday Paraskevi, "Preparation," the same word used in Mark 15:42.

Christians noticed the pagan names early. Around AD 574, Bishop Martin of Braga in Portugal objected to naming days after Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn, and urged believers to use numbers instead. That is why Portuguese still calls Monday segunda-feira, "second day." In AD 321, the emperor Constantine made the "venerable day of the Sun" a legal day of rest across the empire (Code of Justinian 3.12.2), which shows how deeply the planetary week had become part of Roman life.

God told Israel, "make no mention of the name of other gods, neither let it be heard out of thy mouth" (Exodus 23:13). Believers have differed on whether using the common weekday names breaks that command, since most people who say "Thursday" have never heard of Thor. That is a matter for each conscience before the Lord (Romans 14:5). What is not in question is where the names came from, and that the Scripture's own week points to the Creator and His Sabbath, not to the planets.

XX.

Hislop's Two Babylons: What Holds, What Doesn't

Anyone who teaches on Nimrod and Semiramis will meet Alexander Hislop's The Two Babylons. Many of the most repeated claims about Nimrod, Semiramis, and Tammuz come from this one book, not from the ancient sources. To teach with no arguments left standing, we have to separate what Hislop got right from what he got wrong. The truth about Babylon is strong enough that it doesn't need any help from bad evidence.

The Book and Its Author

A Scottish Pastor and a Big Idea

Theory The Thesis

Alexander Hislop (1807-1865) was a minister of the Free Church of Scotland. He published a pamphlet in 1853 and expanded it into a book in 1858. His argument was that Nimrod, his wife Semiramis, and their son Tammuz founded a mystery religion at Babel, and that this religion spread to every nation under different names and finally entered the church in Rome. He tied Christmas, Easter, Lent, the Madonna and Child, and many other things back to that one family.

Theory The Problem of Timing

Hislop wrote at the very moment cuneiform was first being deciphered. The Sumerian language was almost unknown. The King List, the Descent of Inanna, the Dumuzi poems, the Ugarit tablets, and the inscriptions of Shammuramat had not yet been found or read. He built his case from Greek and Latin writers, church fathers, and 19th-century speculative mythologists, and he relied heavily on words that sound alike in different languages. He simply didn't have the evidence we have today.

Theory A Disciple Changes His Mind

In 1966 Ralph Woodrow published Babylon Mystery Religion, which spread Hislop's ideas to a new generation and sold very widely. When Woodrow later went back to check Hislop's sources, he found that many claims had no support, so he withdrew his own book. In 1997 he published The Babylon Connection? explaining why. It is rare for an author to do that, and it is to his credit.

The Lesson

Hislop's instinct was not wrong. Pagan ideas did enter Christian practice, and the early church itself said so. Epiphanius condemned cakes offered to Mary in the 300s, and Martin of Braga objected to the pagan weekday names in the 500s. But his method was wrong. He took real facts, mixed them with legends from many centuries, joined them with look-alike words, and presented the result as proven history.

That matters for a teacher. When a skeptic finds that "Easter comes from Ishtar" is false, he often throws out the rest, including the parts that are true and important. The Queen of Heaven, Tammuz, Ba'al, and Babylon's cup need no exaggeration. Scripture says, "Every word of God is pure... Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar" (Proverbs 30:5-6). The same principle applies to evidence: we shouldn't add to it either. "Prove all things; hold fast that which is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21).

XXI.

Conclusion: The Name Above Every Name

One word has run through this entire study, from before the Flood to the fall of Babylon the Great. That word is name. The whole history of the pagan gods is the history of men and powers reaching for a name that doesn't belong to them.

The Men Who Wanted a Name

Before the Flood. The giants were "mighty men which were of old, men of renown," literally "men of the name" (Genesis 6:4).
At Babel. "Let us build us a city and a tower... and let us make us a name" (Genesis 11:4).
In Akkad. Naram-Sin wrote the sign for "god" before his own name, and his people asked to make him the god of their city.
In legend. Semiramis was said to have flown away as a dove and been worshipped. Ninus became the city of Nineveh in human form. Memnon's name was carried north until Snorri made him Thor's father.
In Canaan. The storm god was called Ba'al, "Lord," a title God refused to share (Hosea 2:16).
In Babylon. "Is not this great Babylon, that I have built... for the honour of my majesty?" (Daniel 4:30). The city said, "I am, and none else beside me" (Isaiah 47:8).
In Rome. The Senate declared Caesar a god, and Herod accepted the shout, "the voice of a god, and not of a man" (Acts 12:22).

The One Who Gives a Name

Right after Babel, God called Abraham out of Ur and said, "I will make thy name great" (Genesis 12:2). What Babel tried to seize, God gave freely to a man who trusted Him. God has His own name, and He does not share it: "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14), and "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images" (Isaiah 42:8).

Every god in this study had a name, and every one of those names is now a museum label, a line in a textbook, or a day of the week. "For all the gods of the nations are idols: but the LORD made the heavens" (Psalm 96:5). Ishtar's temples are ruins. Tammuz is not mourned. Bel bowed down. Thor has no worshippers at Uppsala. Babylon is a desert.

And yet one Man did what the mighty men could not. He did not grasp at equality with God, but "made himself of no reputation," took the form of a servant, and died on a cross. "Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Philippians 2:7-10). The dying gods of the pagans only came and went with the seasons, and no one was ever saved by it. Jesus Christ died for sinners and rose on the third day, "declared to be the Son of God with power... by the resurrection from the dead" (Romans 1:4).

Babel's curse will be undone. At Pentecost, men "out of every nation under heaven" heard the gospel in their own tongues (Acts 2:5-11). God promised, "then will I turn to the people a pure language, that they may all call upon the name of the LORD, to serve him with one consent" (Zephaniah 3:9). The last word goes to Him: "Babylon the great is fallen, is fallen" (Revelation 18:2), and the Rider on the white horse "hath on his vesture and on his thigh a name written, KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS" (Revelation 19:16). "And the LORD shall be king over all the earth: in that day shall there be one LORD, and his name one" (Zechariah 14:9).

Two lines connect this chart with Section XII. Memnon's mother was Eos, the Dawn, whose son Eosphoros was the morning star. And the Anglo-Saxon Eostre, whom Bede named, has a name built from the same old word for "dawn" as Eos. So the dawn goddess shows up at both ends of this study: as the mother of the Memnon whom Snorri made Thor's father, and in the English name of the season Scripture calls the Passover.
