1.The Story of the Passover

Before we look at a single dish on the plate, we need the story that the plate was made to remember. It runs in three movements. It begins on a dark night in Egypt, it returns on a borrowed upstairs room the night before the cross, and it finds its end at Golgotha, where the true Lamb of God was slain. Hold all three together and the whole table opens up.

The First Passover in Egypt

Israel had been slaves in Egypt for generations, crushed under hard bondage, crying out under the whip. God heard them, and He raised up Moses to bring them out. Plague after plague fell on Egypt, yet Pharaoh would not let the people go. So God prepared one final judgment, and with it He gave His people a way to be spared.

On the tenth day of the first month, every household was to take a lamb without blemish and keep it until the fourteenth day. At evening they were to kill it, then take its blood and strike it on the two side posts and the upper post of the door. That blood was the sign. When the judgment passed through the land that night, death would pass over every house that was covered by the blood of the lamb.

Exodus 12:13

"And the blood shall be to you for a token upon the houses where ye are: and when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and the plague shall not be upon you to destroy you, when I smite the land of Egypt."

Inside, they ate the lamb roasted with fire, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, dressed and ready to leave at a moment's notice. That night the firstborn of Egypt died, but Israel was spared by the blood, and Pharaoh finally drove them out. God commanded that this night be kept every year as a memorial, so that no generation would forget how the LORD redeemed them with an outstretched arm.

The Pattern to Watch

A lamb without blemish. Its blood applied as a sign. Death passing over all who are covered by it. Hold that pattern in your mind, because it does not stay in Egypt. It is a shadow, and the One who casts it is still to come.

The Passover We Call the Last Supper

Fifteen hundred years later, on the night before He was crucified, Jesus sat down with His disciples to keep this very feast. What we have come to call the Last Supper was a Passover meal. He had longed for it.

Luke 22:15

"And he said unto them, With desire I have desired to eat this passover with you before I suffer."

In the middle of that ancient meal, Jesus did something new. He took the unleavened bread, gave thanks, broke it, and gave it to them, saying it was His body given for them. Then He took the cup after supper and called it the new covenant in His blood, shed for the remission of sins. The lamb of Egypt had always pointed somewhere. Now, at His own table, the Lord quietly revealed that it had been pointing to Him all along.

Luke 22:19-20

"And he took bread, and gave thanks, and brake it, and gave unto them, saying, This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me. Likewise also the cup after supper, saying, This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you."

This is the meal we keep as the Communion, and it was born here, inside the Passover, given by the Lord Himself.

The Cross and the Lamb of God

The very next day, Jesus was led out and crucified. Long before, when John saw Him coming, he had already named who He was.

John 1:29

"Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world."

Everything that happened at the cross answered the first Passover point for point. As a lamb without blemish was required, He was without sin. As the lamb's blood spared Israel from death, His blood covers us and death passes over the soul that is under it. And just as the law of the Passover lamb said that not one of its bones could be broken, the soldiers came to break the legs of the crucified men, but finding Jesus already dead, they did not break His.

John 19:36

"For these things were done, that the scripture should be fulfilled, A bone of him shall not be broken."

This is why Paul could say it so plainly: Christ our Passover is sacrificed for us. The lamb in Egypt was the picture. The Lord Jesus, slain at Passover, is the substance. The shadow had finally met the body that cast it.

Why the Story Comes First

The plate we are about to study is a memorial of this story. Once you see that the whole thing was always pointing to Christ, you can look at every item on the table and ask the right question: does this remember the Lamb who has come, or does it cling to a sacrifice still treated as missing? That single question will guide the rest of this teaching.

2.The Feast That Remains

The Passover is not a Jewish holiday that the church left behind. It is a feast of the LORD, given by His own command, and He never called it back. When the children of Israel came out of Egypt, God told them to keep this night every year, and He attached no expiration date to it.

Exodus 12:14

"And this day shall be unto you for a memorial; and ye shall keep it a feast to the LORD throughout your generations; ye shall keep it a feast by an ordinance for ever."

Leviticus 23:5

"In the fourteenth day of the first month at even is the LORD's passover."

Notice the language. It is the LORD's passover, one of the LORD's feasts, and it is to be kept for ever. The believer who walks in the whole counsel of God does not ask whether he is allowed to keep Passover. He asks how to keep it now that the true Lamb has come and the great work is finished.

That is the question this teaching answers. The plate that sits at the center of the Passover meal grew up over many centuries, and not every piece of it stands on the same ground. Some of it rests on the written commandment of God. Some of it rests on the traditions of men who were trying to remember a sacrifice they could no longer offer. Our task is to keep what God commanded, to weigh the traditions with honesty, and to let the finished work of Christ fill the whole table with meaning.

3.Two Foundations: Commandment and Tradition

Before we touch a single item, we have to separate two things that are often blended together. The first foundation is the written commandment of God. The second is the tradition of the elders. Both can teach us, but they do not carry the same weight, and the believer must never let the second one push out the first.

Scripture itself names only a few things for the Passover table. The lamb, roasted with fire. Unleavened bread. Bitter herbs. That is the commanded triad.

Exodus 12:8

"And they shall eat the flesh in that night, roast with fire, and unleavened bread; and with bitter herbs they shall eat it."

Everything else on the modern plate, the egg, the sweet paste, the spring vegetable, the second bitter herb, the salt water, the four cups, comes from later Jewish tradition. Much of it was shaped after the Temple was destroyed in 70 AD, when the people could no longer offer the lamb and reached for symbols to fill the empty space where the sacrifice used to be.

This matters because the Lord Himself drew a sharp line between God's commandment and man's tradition, and He warned against letting the one swallow the other.

Mark 7:8-9

"For laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men... Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition."

The Rule for the Whole Table

Keep what God commanded as commanded. Use the traditions of men only as far as they help us teach the truth, never as a law that binds the conscience, and never in a way that contradicts Scripture. A tradition that points to Christ is a good servant. A tradition that buries the commandment is a bad master.

Teacher's Note

This is the single most important point in the lesson, so do not rush it. Most people have never been told that the Seder plate is mostly rabbinic tradition built around the missing sacrifice. Once the class sees that clearly, every element afterward becomes easy to weigh. Spend the time here.

4.The Center of the Plate Has Changed

Here is the heart of the whole matter. Rabbinic Judaism rebuilt the Seder around an absence. The Temple was gone, the lamb could no longer be offered, so the plate became a careful memorial of a sacrifice that was missing. Every roasted symbol on it whispers the same sorrow: we cannot do what God commanded, so we remember it instead.

For the believer in Christ, the center of the plate is not an absence. It is a Presence. The sacrifice is not missing. It has been offered once, perfectly, for ever.

John 1:29

"Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world."

1 Corinthians 5:7

"Purge out therefore the old leaven, that ye may be a new lump, as ye are unleavened. For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us."

Hebrews 10:12

"But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God."

This is where Pronomian teaching parts company with both error on either side. We do not throw away the feast, as if the law of God were abolished. The feast stands. But neither do we keep slaughtering lambs or mourn an empty Temple as though Messiah had never come. The animal sacrifice, the part of the law tied to the altar and the priesthood, found its fulfillment in Christ. The lamb was a shadow. The body that cast the shadow is the Lord Jesus Himself.

Colossians 2:16-17

"Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days: which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ."

The Pronomian Reading

The feast days are a shadow whose substance belongs to Christ. A shadow that has met its substance is not discarded; it is finally understood. So we keep the feast that God commanded, and we keep it seeing Christ in every part of it. The ceremonial lamb is fulfilled. The appointed time is still kept.

With that settled, we can walk the plate one piece at a time. For each item we will ask three questions. What does Scripture actually command? What is tradition? And what does it become now that the Lamb has come?

5.The Shankbone

Zeroa · Roasted Bone

Scripture: points to the lamb  ·  The bone itself: tradition  ·  Now: fulfilled in Christ

What Scripture Commands

The commandment was a roasted lamb, not a bare bone. The lamb was the very heart of the night. Its blood went on the doorposts, and the LORD passed over every house that was covered by it.

Exodus 12:7, 13

"And they shall take of the blood, and strike it on the two side posts and on the upper door post... And when I see the blood, I will pass over you."

What Tradition Made of It

After the Temple fell, no lamb could be offered, because the law forbade the sacrifice anywhere but the place God chose. So the sages set a single roasted bone on the plate to stand in for the lamb that was missing. They even forbade lifting it or eating it, lest it look like a sacrifice offered in the wrong place. The bone is a monument to a sacrifice they could no longer make.

What It Becomes in Christ

This is the most striking change of all. The Jewish plate keeps a silent, uneaten bone for a Lamb that has not come. We hold the opposite truth. The Lamb has come, and not one of His bones was broken, exactly as the Passover law required of the lamb.

Exodus 12:46

"...neither shall ye break a bone thereof."

John 19:36

"For these things were done, that the scripture should be fulfilled, A bone of him shall not be broken."

Teaching Point

The unbroken bone of the Passover lamb was a prophecy. On the cross, when the soldiers broke the legs of the two beside Him but found Jesus already dead, the law of the lamb was kept to the letter. The believer does not need a bone to remember an absent sacrifice. He looks to a risen Lord whose unbroken body proves He is the true Passover.

Teacher's Note

A believer keeping Passover may still place a roasted bone on the table purely as a visual teaching aid for the lamb of Exodus 12, and then point straight to John 19:36. That is honest. What we never do is treat the bone as a required substitute for an offering we are sad we cannot make, because we are not sad. Our sacrifice is finished and our Lamb is alive.

6.The Egg

Beitzah · Roasted Egg

Scripture: not commanded  ·  The egg: tradition of mourning  ·  Now: our mourning has turned to joy

What Scripture Commands

Nothing. There is no egg anywhere in the Passover commandment. The egg stands for a second Temple offering, the festival peace offering, that was eaten alongside the lamb on the feast.

What Tradition Made of It

In Jewish custom the egg is a food of mourners, round and unbroken like the cycle of grief. It was placed on the plate to mourn the destroyed Temple and the offerings that could no longer be brought. So the egg, like the bone, is built around loss.

What It Becomes in Christ

The believer is not in mourning over a lost altar. The veil of the Temple was torn from top to bottom the moment Christ died, and a new and living way was opened to God. We do not grieve the end of the old sacrifices; we rejoice that the perfect sacrifice replaced them.

Hebrews 9:26

"...but now once in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself."

Teaching Point

The egg is the clearest example of a tradition built on sorrow that simply does not fit the believer's table. It is not commanded, and the grief it expresses has been answered by the cross. A believer may leave it off the plate entirely with a clear conscience. If anyone keeps it, it should only be as a marker that the old offerings have given way to a better one, never as mourning.

7.The Bitter Herbs

Maror · Bitter Herbs

Scripture: commanded  ·  Keep it  ·  Now: the bitterness of bondage to sin

What Scripture Commands

The bitter herbs are one of the three things God actually named for the meal, right alongside the lamb and the unleavened bread. This one is not optional and not merely tradition. It is part of the written command, repeated even for those who kept the second Passover.

Numbers 9:11

"...they shall eat it with unleavened bread and bitter herbs."

What It Means

The herbs carry the taste of slavery. The lives of Israel were made bitter with hard bondage in Egypt, and the sharp herb puts that bitterness on the tongue so the memory is felt, not just spoken. Scripture binds the bitterness to the redemption on purpose. You cannot taste the sweetness of being set free until you have tasted what you were freed from.

What It Becomes in Christ

Egypt is a picture of our slavery to sin. The bitter herb reminds the believer of the cruelty of the bondage Christ brought us out of. We were not in light bondage. We were dead in trespasses and could not free ourselves. The sharpness of the herb is the honest taste of what sin costs.

Teaching Point

Keep the bitter herbs without hesitation. They are commanded, and they preach a true gospel: that we remember the misery of sin so that the deliverance of Christ tastes as sweet as it truly is. A faith that never tastes the bitterness of what it was saved from grows shallow.

8.The Second Bitter Herb

Chazeret · Second Bitter Herb

Scripture: the herb is commanded  ·  A second one: tradition  ·  Now: the slow trap of sin

What Scripture Commands

Scripture commands bitter herbs. The custom of placing two separate bitter herbs on the plate comes from a rabbinic reading of the plural word in the Hebrew text, taking the plural to require at least two kinds. That is a tradition drawn from grammar, not a separate command.

Teacher's Note

Do not lean your teaching on the Hebrew grammar argument, since it is a rabbinic inference rather than a plain command, and a sharp listener could push back on it. The stronger ground is simply this: God commanded bitter herbs, and a believer is free to use one or more without binding anyone's conscience to a number.

What It Can Teach

The second herb is usually romaine lettuce, which is sweet and tender when it first sprouts and turns hard and bitter as it grows. That picture preaches well. Israel entered Egypt in comfort and honor, and the sweetness slowly hardened into slavery. Sin works the same way. It rarely begins with chains. It begins with ease and welcome, and only later shows its bitter root.

Teaching Point

Use the second herb if it helps you teach how sin entices before it enslaves. Just do not present a second herb as a command from God. It is a tradition with a useful lesson, and that is exactly how the believer should hold it.

9.The Sweet Paste

Charoset · Sweet Paste

Scripture: not commanded  ·  Tradition  ·  Now: sweet hope inside hard labor

What Scripture Commands

The sweet paste is not commanded anywhere in the Torah. The word for it does not appear there. It is entirely a tradition, though a thoughtful and beautiful one.

What It Pictures

The paste is thick and earthy, made to look like the mortar and clay the Israelites used to make bricks under the whip. So the very food that tastes sweet is shaped like the mortar of their suffering. It holds two truths together: the labor was hard, yet God kept His people alive and gave them hope in the middle of it.

What It Can Teach

For the believer the sweet paste is a fitting picture of grace at work even in our former bondage. While we were still slaves to sin, God was already preserving us and preparing to deliver us. The sweetness in the mortar is the mercy of God reaching into the hardest place. There is no command to eat it, but there is a real lesson in it.

Teacher's Note

The research traditions attach some vivid stories to this paste, including legends about the women of Israel and hidden word meanings. Those are interesting as Jewish lore, but do not preach them as Scripture. Teach the paste simply as a man-made symbol of grace and hope inside hard bondage, and keep the weight of the lesson on the written word.

10.The Spring Vegetable

Karpas · Spring Vegetable

Scripture: not commanded  ·  Tradition  ·  Now: tears of bondage, hope of new life

What Scripture Commands

The spring vegetable, dipped in salt water, is not a Passover command. It is a tradition that grew up to open the meal, partly to stir the children to ask questions about the night.

What It Pictures

The green vegetable speaks of new life and spring, since Passover falls in the month of the firstfruits of the year. The salt water it is dipped in speaks of the tears that Israel wept under slavery. So the first taste of the meal joins fresh life with the memory of weeping.

What It Can Teach

This is an easy and true picture for the believer. We came up out of the salt water of our own tears and bondage into new life. The green of spring is the resurrection life that Christ gives. There is no command here, but the symbol carries a clean gospel shape: out of weeping into new life.

Teacher's Note

Some traditions tie hidden number codes and old word histories to this vegetable. Those are clever but contested, and they are not where the strength of the lesson lies. Keep it plain: green for new life, salt water for the tears of slavery, and Christ as the One who brings us through.

11.The Unleavened Bread

Matzah · Unleavened Bread

Scripture: commanded  ·  Keep it  ·  Now: the body of Christ, sincerity and truth

What Scripture Commands

The unleavened bread is one of the three commanded elements, and the whole feast that follows is named for it. Israel ate it because they left Egypt in haste, with no time for the bread to rise. It is the bread of affliction.

Deuteronomy 16:3

"Thou shalt eat no leavened bread with it; seven days shalt thou eat unleavened bread therewith, even the bread of affliction; for thou camest forth out of the land of Egypt in haste."

What It Becomes in Christ

Leaven in Scripture is a picture of sin, which puffs up and spreads quietly through the whole lump. Bread without leaven is therefore a picture of a life and a Savior without sin. When Paul tells the church to keep the feast, he says to keep it with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.

1 Corinthians 5:8

"Therefore let us keep the feast, not with old leaven, neither with the leaven of malice and wickedness; but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth."

More than this, the Lord took the unleavened bread at His own Passover, broke it, and gave it as His body. The sinless bread is the sinless Christ, given for us.

Luke 22:19

"And he took bread, and gave thanks, and brake it, and gave unto them, saying, This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me."

Teaching Point

The unleavened bread is striped from baking and pierced with small holes so it can cook through. Long before the rabbis ever made it that way, the prophets said the Messiah would be pierced, and that by His stripes we would be healed. The believer holds that bread and sees the Lord who was wounded for us, the true Bread of Life who came down from heaven.

This Is the Communion

This broken bread, taken together with the cup that follows, is the Communion the Lord gave us. He gave it at the Passover, and at Passover is when we keep it, one time a year. The fuller meaning of the cup and the Communion is taken up in the next section.

12.The Cup

The Cup of Redemption

Scripture: wine at the meal  ·  Four cups: tradition  ·  Now: the blood of the new covenant

What Scripture Commands and What Tradition Added

Wine belonged to the festival meal, but the practice of four set cups, each with its own name, is a later tradition built on the four promises of redemption in Exodus 6:6-7. The promises are God's word; the count of four cups is the tradition that frames them.

What It Becomes in Christ

At the Passover, after supper, the Lord took the cup and made it the sign of His own blood poured out. The cup that tradition called the cup of redemption became, in His hands, the cup of the new covenant in His blood.

Luke 22:20

"...This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you."

1 Corinthians 11:26

"For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do shew the Lord's death till he come."

Teaching Point

This is the great bridge between the old feast and the believer's table. The bread and the cup of the Lord's Supper did not appear from nowhere. The Lord lifted them out of the Passover and filled them with Himself. When we take communion, we are keeping the deepest part of the Passover in the way He commanded, remembering His death until He comes.

This Is the Communion

The bread and the cup together are the Communion, the Lord's Supper. The Lord gave them at the Passover, and that is the time we keep them. We take the Communion one time a year, at Passover, in the appointed season the Lord Himself chose for it. This is not a separate rite invented later; it is the very heart of the Passover, kept exactly where He placed it.

13.Setting the Plate Today

So how does a believer who walks in the commandments of God actually keep this feast now? Here is a plain summary that holds the commandment and the tradition in their right places.

Keep What God Commanded

Keep the appointed time of Passover, for it is the LORD's feast for ever. On the table, keep the unleavened bread and the bitter herbs, since both are commanded. Above all, keep the bread and the cup as the Lord gave them, remembering His body and His blood. These are the parts that rest on the written word, and they are the center of the believer's table.

Handle the Lamb Rightly

Do not slaughter a Passover lamb. The lamb was a shadow, and Christ our Passover has already been sacrificed for us. If a roasted bone is placed on the table, let it serve only to teach the lamb of Exodus 12 and to point straight to the Lamb of God whose bones were not broken. Never treat it as a substitute offering for a sacrifice we wish we could make, because the perfect sacrifice is finished.

Weigh the Traditions Honestly

The sweet paste and the spring vegetable may be kept as teaching pictures, since they preach grace and new life, as long as no one is told they are commands. The second bitter herb may be used to show how sin entices before it enslaves. The egg, built on mourning a lost Temple, fits the believer's table least of all, and may be left off without any loss, since our grief over the old sacrifices was answered at the cross.

The Whole Lesson in One Sentence

We keep the feast God commanded, we let Christ be the center where the lamb once stood, and we hold every man-made tradition loosely, using only what points to Him and binding no one's conscience to the rest.

Teacher's Note

If the class asks, "are we required to hold a full Seder?", answer carefully. We are commanded to keep the feast of Passover and Unleavened Bread, and the Lord commanded the bread and the cup. The elaborate plate with all six items is a tradition, not a command. A believer may use a fuller table as a rich teaching tool, or keep a simple table with the commanded elements and the Lord's Supper. Either way, the conscience stays free and Christ stays central.

Keep the Feast

The Passover plate, in the hands of those still waiting, is a careful memorial of an absence. In the hands of the believer it becomes a table of fulfillment. The lamb has come. The blood has been shed. The bread of affliction is the sinless body given for us, and the cup of redemption is the new covenant in His blood.

So we do not abandon the feast, and we do not return to its shadows as though the substance had never arrived. We keep it, and we keep it looking full in the face of the Lord Jesus, who desired with great desire to eat that Passover with His own before He suffered.

1 Corinthians 5:7-8

"For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us: therefore let us keep the feast... with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth."

14.Discipleship Questions

Answer in your own words from the Scriptures we have studied.

Does the command to keep the Passover have an end date in Scripture? Quote the phrase from Exodus 12:14 that answers this.
What is the difference between the commandment of God and the tradition of men, and which warning did the Lord give about confusing them?
Why does the rabbinic plate keep a bone for the lamb instead of a lamb? How is the believer's situation the opposite of theirs?
How was the law of the Passover lamb in Exodus 12:46 fulfilled at the cross? Give the verse from John.
Which items on the plate are commanded in Scripture, and which are traditions of men?
Why does the egg fit the believer's table least of all? What truth answers the mourning behind it?
What does leaven picture in Scripture, and how does that make the unleavened bread a picture of Christ?
How did the bread and the cup of the Lord's Supper come out of the Passover meal?
