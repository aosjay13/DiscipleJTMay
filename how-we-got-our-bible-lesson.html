How We Got Our Bible

The Story of Scripture's Formation and Transmission

✦

Preamble

Some believers hesitate to look closely at the history behind their Bible, worried that studying how it was written down, copied, and translated might shake their confidence in it. The opposite is true. When you actually trace the story, from Moses writing on a scroll to the leather-bound Bible on your nightstand, what stands out is not fragility but faithfulness. God did not simply hand down a finished book from the sky. He worked through real people, real languages, and real history, and he preserved his word through all of it.

Paul told Timothy that all scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching, correction, and training in righteousness, so that the man or woman of God would be fully equipped (2 Timothy 3:16-17). Isaiah put it even more simply: grass withers and flowers fade, but the word of our God stands forever (Isaiah 40:8). This lesson walks through how that word came to us, the languages it was first written in, the ancient copies that preserved it, and the long road from those first scrolls to the Bible we hold today.

I.

Three Languages, One Story

The Bible was not written in one language. It came to us in three, and each one tells us something about the time and the people God was speaking through.

Hebrew

Nearly the entire Old Testament was written in Hebrew, the language of Israel. Hebrew is built on three-letter root words that branch out into families of related meaning, which is part of why Hebrew poetry and wordplay run so deep through the Psalms and the Prophets.

Aramaic

A handful of Old Testament passages shift into Aramaic, the common language of the ancient Near East during the exile and the Persian period that followed it. Long sections of Daniel (2:4 through 7:28) and Ezra (4:8 through 6:18, and 7:12 through 26) are in Aramaic, along with a single verse in Jeremiah (10:11) and two words tucked into Genesis (31:47). By the time of Jesus, Aramaic had become the everyday spoken language in Judea, which is why a few Aramaic phrases show up on Jesus's own lips in the Gospels.

Koine Greek

The entire New Testament was written in Koine Greek, the common, everyday Greek spoken across the Mediterranean world after Alexander the Great's conquests spread Greek language and culture from Egypt to Persia. This was not the polished literary Greek of the philosophers. It was the trade language ordinary people used, which meant the gospel could travel to nearly anyone in the Roman world without needing translation first.

II.

The Old Testament Takes Shape

The Hebrew Bible did not arrive all at once. It grew over roughly a thousand years, and the Jewish tradition organized it into three parts that Jesus himself referred to by name when he told his disciples that everything written about him in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms had to be fulfilled (Luke 24:44).

Part One

The Torah, or Law

Genesis through Deuteronomy. Jewish and Christian tradition has long held that Moses wrote the Torah, placing its composition around the fourteenth or thirteenth century BC, during the exodus and wilderness years. This is the foundation the rest of scripture builds on.

Modern scholars debate how much of the Torah's final form came together later, through editing and compiling of older material. Believers hold a range of views on this, but the traditional position, that Moses is the author under God's direction, remains the historic and confessional understanding.

Part Two

The Prophets, or Nevi'im

This section splits into the "former prophets," the historical narrative books of Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings, and the "latter prophets," the great preaching books from Isaiah through Malachi (with Daniel placed elsewhere, in the Writings, in the Hebrew ordering). These books were written across several centuries as God raised up prophets to call Israel back to covenant faithfulness, warn of coming judgment, and promise future restoration.

Part Three

The Writings, or Ketuvim

Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah, and Chronicles. This is the most varied section, holding poetry, wisdom literature, and history, much of it associated with David, Solomon, and the post-exilic community that rebuilt Jerusalem.

When was the Old Testament canon settled?

Most conservative scholars place the recognition of the Hebrew scriptures as complete not long after the last prophet, Malachi, finished writing in roughly the fifth century BC. The books were already functioning as authoritative scripture in Jewish worship and life well before that point. You may run across the claim that a rabbinic council at Jamnia, near the end of the first century AD, is what "closed" the Old Testament canon. Most historians today consider that theory overstated. The gathering at Jamnia appears to have been a discussion among rabbis, not a canon-setting council, and the books it discussed were already widely regarded as scripture long before the rabbis met. By the time of Jesus, the Hebrew scriptures he quoted from were already a settled, recognized body of writing.

III.

Ancient Texts That Preserved the Word

Once the Old Testament books were written, someone had to keep copying them, by hand, for well over a thousand years, before the printing press existed. Three sources let us check how well that copying held up.

The Masoretic Text

Jewish scholars called the Masoretes worked between roughly 500 and 1000 AD to standardize and safeguard the Hebrew text. Ancient Hebrew was written with consonants only, so the Masoretes added a system of vowel points and marginal notes to lock in the correct pronunciation and guard against copying errors. Their finished work, the Masoretic Text, became the standard Hebrew Bible still used by scholars and translators today.

The Dead Sea Scrolls

In 1947, a shepherd discovered ancient scrolls hidden in caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea. These scrolls, dated from roughly 250 BC to 70 AD, included copies of almost every Old Testament book, some more than a thousand years older than any Hebrew manuscript scholars had access to before. When researchers compared the Dead Sea Scrolls to the much later Masoretic Text, the two matched with remarkable consistency. A thousand years of careful, hand-copied transmission had preserved the text with striking accuracy.

The Septuagint

As Jewish communities spread across the Greek-speaking world, many no longer read Hebrew fluently, so Jewish scholars in Alexandria, Egypt began translating the Hebrew scriptures into Greek starting around 250 BC. This translation became known as the Septuagint, from the Latin word for seventy, tied to a tradition that seventy or seventy-two scholars produced it. The Septuagint became the Bible most widely used in the Greek-speaking world of the first century, and the New Testament writers frequently quote the Old Testament from it.

IV.

The New Testament Takes Shape

Unlike the Old Testament's thousand-year span, the New Testament came together in the span of a single lifetime, roughly 45 to 100 AD, written by the apostles and their close companions while eyewitnesses to Jesus's life and resurrection were still alive to confirm or correct what was written.

Paul's letters are generally the earliest New Testament writings, with some dated as early as 48 or 49 AD. The Gospels drew on eyewitness testimony and, in some cases, earlier written sources, and were composed over the following decades. John's writings are usually placed latest, toward the end of the first century.

How the early church recognized which books were scripture

The early church did not invent authority for these books. It recognized authority that was already present, using a consistent set of questions:

  • Apostolic origin: was it written by an apostle, or by someone working under an apostle's direct authority?
  • Orthodoxy: did it agree with the apostolic teaching already received?
  • Catholicity: was it widely received and used across the churches, not just in one isolated community?
  • Inspiration: did it bear the unmistakable mark of God's authority, recognized by the church as it read and prayed through it?
V.

The Canon Is Recognized

The New Testament's 27 books were already being read, copied, and treated as authoritative scripture in churches long before any council made a formal pronouncement. What follows is the story of how that recognition became explicit.

Around 140 AD

Marcion's Challenge

A teacher named Marcion in Rome rejected the entire Old Testament and accepted only a trimmed-down version of Luke and ten of Paul's letters, cutting out anything that sounded too Jewish. The church rejected Marcion as a heretic, but his shortened, distorted canon pushed the wider church to begin stating clearly which books it actually recognized.

Around 170 AD

The Muratorian Fragment

This surviving list, one of the earliest of its kind, names most of the books in our New Testament as already recognized and in use, showing that broad agreement existed decades before any formal council met.

Third Century

Origen and Eusebius Sort the Evidence

Origen, and later the historian Eusebius, both sorted early Christian writings into categories: books universally accepted, books disputed by some but accepted by many, and books rejected as spurious. Their careful sorting shows a church working through the question with real discernment rather than simply accepting everything in circulation.

367 AD

Athanasius's Festal Letter

Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote an Easter letter to the churches under his care listing exactly the 27 books that make up our New Testament today, the first time in history that precise list appears in writing. He was not creating a new canon. He was confirming, in a single document, what was already the settled conviction of the wider church.

393 and 397 AD

The Councils of Hippo and Carthage

Regional church councils in North Africa formally affirmed the same 27-book New Testament already in common use. These councils did not vote a canon into existence. They ratified a list the church had already been living by for generations.

VI.

The Manuscripts That Survived

No original manuscript, what scholars call an "autograph," survives for any book of the Bible. What survives are thousands of hand-copied manuscripts, and the New Testament is by far the best-attested document from the ancient world, with far more surviving copies, and far earlier ones, than any other ancient text.

Early fragments and papyri

The earliest known New Testament fragment, a small scrap of John's Gospel known as Papyrus 52, dates to the first half of the second century, remarkably close to John's own lifetime, and was found in Egypt, far from where John wrote. The Chester Beatty Papyri and the Bodmer Papyri, both from the 200s AD, preserve substantial portions of the Gospels, Acts, and Paul's letters.

The great codices

By the fourth and fifth centuries, complete handwritten Bibles bound as books, called codices, begin to survive. Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus, both from the 300s AD, and Codex Alexandrinus, from the 400s AD, contain most or all of the Old and New Testaments and remain foundational sources for scholars establishing the most reliable biblical text today.

✦ Bonus

For centuries, scribes copied scripture entirely by hand, one letter at a time, which naturally introduced small variations between copies, mostly spelling, word order, or an accidentally repeated line. Textual criticism is the careful scholarly discipline of comparing thousands of surviving manuscripts to reconstruct the original wording with as much precision as possible. The overwhelming majority of these variations affect nothing of doctrinal weight. No point of Christian belief rests on a disputed reading.

VII.

From Scroll to Printed Page

Once the canon was settled, the next challenge was getting the Bible into the hands, and the language, of ordinary believers.

382 to 405 AD

The Latin Vulgate

Jerome, working largely in Bethlehem, produced a fresh Latin translation directly from the Hebrew Old Testament and Greek New Testament, rather than translating Latin from the Greek Septuagint as earlier Latin versions had done. His Vulgate became the standard Bible of the Western church for the next thousand years.

1380s

John Wycliffe's English Bible

Wycliffe and his followers produced the first complete English translation of the Bible, hand-copied and translated from the Latin Vulgate, at a time when the church discouraged putting scripture directly into the hands of ordinary people in their own language.

1526

William Tyndale's New Testament

Tyndale translated the New Testament directly from Greek into clear, plain English, and had it printed, making it the first English New Testament produced on a printing press. His work was considered dangerous enough that he was arrested and executed in 1536. A huge amount of his wording, "let there be light," "the powers that be," and more, survives essentially unchanged in English Bibles to this day.

1535 to 1560

Coverdale and the Geneva Bible

Miles Coverdale produced the first complete printed English Bible in 1535, building on Tyndale's work. The Geneva Bible followed in 1560, produced by English Protestants living in exile in Geneva, and became the household Bible of its era, filled with study notes and small enough for ordinary families to own and read.

1611

The King James Version

King James I commissioned a new English translation, and roughly 47 scholars, working in committees, produced what became the King James Version. It leaned heavily on Tyndale's earlier work and became the most widely used and influential English Bible for the next three and a half centuries.

VIII.

Into English, Into Our Hands

Two major manuscript discoveries reshaped biblical scholarship in the modern era. Codex Sinaiticus, unknown to the wider world for centuries, was rediscovered at a monastery in the Sinai desert in 1844. Then, in 1947, the Dead Sea Scrolls surfaced, giving scholars access to Old Testament manuscripts a thousand years older than anything previously available. Both discoveries pushed scholars to refine their Greek and Hebrew source texts, producing more precise critical editions than translators had ever worked from before.

That refined scholarship fed a wave of new English translations from the 1800s onward: the American Standard Version, the Revised Standard Version, the New American Standard Bible, the New International Version, the English Standard Version, and many others. These translations differ mainly in translation philosophy. Some aim for formal equivalence, staying as close as possible to the exact word order and phrasing of the original languages. Others aim for dynamic equivalence, prioritizing how a thought would naturally be expressed in modern English, even if that means departing from a strict word-for-word rendering.

✦ Bonus: What About the Apocrypha?

You may hear about additional books, called the Apocrypha or the deuterocanonical books, that appear in some Bibles but not others. These books circulated in the Greek-speaking Jewish world and were included in many copies of the Septuagint, which is part of why the Catholic and Orthodox traditions include them in their Old Testaments today. The Catholic Church formally affirmed their canonical status at the Council of Trent in 1546.

Jerome himself, translating the Vulgate centuries earlier, distinguished these books from the Hebrew canon, treating them as useful for instruction but not as scripture on the same level as the books the Jewish community had always recognized. The Reformers followed Jerome's distinction rather than the later medieval practice, which is why Protestant Bibles hold to the same 66 books recognized in the Hebrew canon and the earliest New Testament lists. The whole of that 66-book Bible, Genesis to Revelation, stands as complete and sufficient, the final authority for faith and practice (2 Timothy 3:16-17), and nothing outside it carries equal weight.

The Point of All This

What should stand out, walking back through all of this, is not how fragile the Bible's history looks, but how sturdy it is. Different languages, different centuries, different continents, and yet the message that reached us matches what was written down in the first place, confirmed again and again by manuscript after manuscript. Jesus said heaven and earth would pass away before his words would (Matthew 24:35). The history behind your Bible is one long record of that promise being kept.
