The Calendar of the Nations

The Names We Speak Every Day · Tried by Scripture and History

✦

Continuation of Part I

Part I of this study established that the popular holidays of the world, Christmas, Easter, Halloween, Valentine's Day, and New Year's Day, all draw their customs from the worship of false gods whose roots run back to Babel and the kingdom of Nimrod. But the reach of the Babylonian system extends further than the holidays we observe. It reaches into the very language by which we mark every single day of the week and every month of the year. God commanded His people to make no mention of the names of other gods. Yet in the names of our days and months, the gods of Rome, of the Norse, and of Babylon are spoken from our mouths without a second thought, from the moment we write the date on a page to the moment we plan the week ahead. This study traces those names to their source and sets them alongside God's own ordering of time, which He established from the first day of creation.

Foundation

The Governing Command

In Exodus 23:13, God goes beyond forbidding the worship of other gods. He commands that their very names not be heard from the mouths of His people. It is a sobering word to set beside the calendar we live by every day. When we speak the name Sunday, we name the sun, which the Romans worshipped as Sol Invictus, the unconquered sun. When we say Monday, we name the moon goddess. When we say Thursday, we name Thor, the Norse god of thunder. When we say January, we name Janus, the two-faced Roman god. When we say March, we name Mars, the Roman god of war. When we say June, we name Juno, who was called the queen of the gods and, like Ashtoreth and Ishtar before her, the queen of heaven. These names were not given to the days and months by accident. They were deliberately set there by the ruling powers of the pagan world so that no ordinary day of life could pass without the gods of the nations being invoked in ordinary speech.

God did not leave His own people without a pattern for time. From the very first chapter of Genesis He numbered the days of the week. He established the Sabbath and He appointed His own calendar of months and feasts. The contrast between what the nations did with time and what God commanded for His people is the subject of this study.

Article II

The Days of the Week

The seven-day week is not a human invention. God established the seven-day cycle at creation, and the seventh-day Sabbath is His own mark upon it. Ancient Rome originally used an eight-day market cycle. It was the spread of the biblical creation pattern, carried through Jewish influence into the wider world, that brought the seven-day week to Rome. The pagan world received the structure of the week from God and then named its days after their gods.

The Romans assigned each day to one of the seven visible heavenly bodies, each of which bore the name of a god: the Sun, the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn. When the Germanic peoples adopted the Roman week, they kept the Sun and Moon but replaced the four planetary gods with their own equivalent Norse deities. That blend of Roman and Norse gives us the English names we speak every day. God numbered the days and named only one of them. The nations replaced His numbering with the names of their gods.

Observations on the Days

The First and Second Days bear the names of the sun and moon, which were objects of worship across the ancient world. God created the sun and moon on the fourth day and gave them no names at all, calling them simply "the greater light" and "the lesser light," so that Israel would not be tempted to venerate what the nations worshipped.

Wednesday carries the name of Odin, the chief god of the Norse pantheon, described as the lord of wisdom, war, death, and magic. To his worshippers he was the all-father, the ruler of all the gods. His name sits at the middle of every week.

Saturday bears the name of Saturn, whose December festival, the Saturnalia, was the very celebration from which Christmas drew its season of feasting, gift-giving, and license. The day the nations gave to Saturn is the same day God set apart as the Sabbath, the sign of the covenant between Himself and His people.

Friday honors the goddess of love, the Norse Frigg or Freya, the exact counterpart of Roman Venus and ultimately the same fertility goddess whose worship ran through Ashtoreth and Ishtar. The day before the Sabbath carries her name in every English-speaking country on earth.

Article III

The Months of the Year

The original Roman calendar had only ten months and began in March, named for Mars, the god of war. The months of January and February were added afterward, and when Julius Caesar reformed the calendar in 46 BC, the civil new year was moved to the first of January to honor Janus, the two-faced god of beginnings. This is the reason the last four months of our year, September, October, November, and December, literally mean the seventh, eighth, ninth, and tenth months. Their names were correct when March was the first month, and they have been wrong by two every year since Rome moved the new year to honor a pagan god.

Of the twelve months, six carry the names of gods or goddesses of the Roman pantheon, two honor deified emperors who were worshipped as gods, and four retain their original numbering from the old Roman calendar. Not one of the twelve months is named after the God of Israel.

Observations on the Months

Juno in June is the Roman name for the very goddess who was worshipped as "queen of heaven," the same title the prophet Jeremiah rebukes Israel for giving to Ashtoreth, the same title that belongs to Ishtar of Babylon and Astarte of Greece. The month most associated with weddings in Western culture is named for the pagan queen of heaven.

July and August are monuments to emperor worship. These two men, Julius Caesar and Augustus, were formally declared gods by the Roman Senate and worshipped throughout the empire. Two months of every year carry the names of deified men as a permanent record of the world's willingness to put the creature in the place of the Creator.

September through December testify silently to the fact that God's spring new year was overwritten by a pagan one. Their names are off by two months, and they have been off by two months ever since Julius Caesar moved the new year to January to honor Janus. Every time someone hears that October means "the eighth month" but is the tenth, the calendar itself is confessing that something was displaced.

March bearing the name of Mars and being the original first month of the Roman year is worth noting alongside God's instruction. God set the beginning of the year in spring, in the month of Abib. Rome also began in spring, but in the name of the god of war. When Caesar moved it to January, even that pagan starting point was abandoned for an earlier one devoted to Janus.

Article IV

When Does the Year Begin?

God answered this question plainly when He brought His people out of Egypt. In the month of Abib, the month of the Passover, in the spring of the year, God told Moses and Aaron: "This month shall be unto you the beginning of months; it shall be the first month of the year to you." He did not leave the start of the year to human reckoning or the movement of the winter solstice. He tied the beginning of His year to His greatest act of redemption: the Passover.

The Roman calendar moved the new year to January to honor Janus, a god of doorways and transitions. Before Caesar's reform, even Rome began the year in March, named for the god of war. The world has therefore cycled through several pagan starting points for the year, none of which have anything to do with God's redemptive calendar. The result is a year that begins in the dead of winter, under the name of a false god, in a month that carries no biblical significance, in a season attached to the Saturnalia.

God's year is a redemptive year. It begins with the Passover, moves through the Feast of Weeks, and closes with the Feast of Tabernacles in the seventh month. It is structured entirely around His appointed times and His acts in history. The years themselves run in cycles of seven, with the seventh year being a Sabbatical year of rest for the land, and the fiftieth year being the Jubilee, the year of liberty. God's calendar is a proclamation of His sovereignty over time itself.

Article V

God's Pattern for Time

From the first chapter of Genesis, God established a pattern for marking time that is entirely unlike the calendar of the nations. He numbered the days of the week. He did not name them after celestial bodies He created, and He did not give them to the worship of any god. The days are simply the first day, the second day, the third day, and so on, and the seventh day alone receives a name: the Sabbath. The rest of the week exists in plain numbered order. God's calendar is not built on the personalities of gods; it is built on the work and rest of the Creator Himself.

God's months follow the same principle. They are numbered months from a spring beginning, tied to His agricultural cycles and His appointed times. Where Scripture gives names to the months, those names carry descriptive meaning: Abib means "fresh grain" or "the month of the green ears," pointing to the barley harvest of Passover. Zif means "brightness." Ethanim means "flowing streams," pointing to the autumn season of the seventh month. Bul means "produce" or "rain." These are not the names of gods. They are the language of the land and the seasons that God made, describing what He does in each part of the year.

Article VI

Walking by God's Calendar

The weight of what these two studies have uncovered cannot be overstated. The Babylonian system of false worship did not confine itself to the temples. It embedded itself in the names of the days we live by, the months we plan by, and the holidays we gather around. Every week the names of Odin, Thor, Freya, and Saturn pass through our lips. Every year the names of Janus, Mars, Maia, and Juno mark our months. Every calendar on our wall is a monument to the gods the nations chose instead of the God of Israel.

A practical word is needed here. To refuse to speak the name Wednesday or January entirely would require withdrawing from ordinary life in ways that go beyond what Scripture commands. The call is not to refuse the vocabulary of the world at the cost of all daily function. The call is to understand, to guard the heart, and above all to walk by God's calendar rather than the world's. God's people know that time belongs to Him. They know when the Sabbath falls, they know when the Passover comes, and they know when the Feast of Tabernacles calls them to gather. They measure their year by His appointed times and not by the festivals of the nations.

God has promised that the day is coming when He will take the names of Baalim out of His people's mouths entirely, and they shall no more be remembered. That promise is the direction believers are to be moving in now, even as they live in a world that has written those names into every day of every week. In the meantime, we hold God's pattern before us: numbered days, a Sabbath of rest, a year that begins with redemption, and a calendar of holy convocations that proclaims the story of salvation from the Passover to the Feast of Tabernacles. For the full account of God's appointed times and how the assembly is to observe them, see the companion documents Moedim in the Church and the article on the Moedim in the Bylaws of Faith and Practice.
