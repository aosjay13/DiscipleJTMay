A Scriptural Study · The Trumpets of God

The Shofar and the Silver Trumpets

The horns God commanded, the calls they sounded, and the trumpet yet to be blown

✦

Introduction

There is no instrument in all of Scripture that carries more weight than the horn. It sounded on Sinai before God spoke the Ten Commandments. It brought down the walls of Jericho. It crowned kings, freed slaves, gathered armies, warned cities, and opened the year. It is the only instrument the Bible commands men to make, commands men to blow, and then places in the hand of God Himself at the end of the age. When the Lord comes for His people, the Scripture does not say He comes with a harp or a psaltery. He comes with the trump of God.

This teaching covers both instruments God appointed: the ram's horn, commonly called the shofar, and the two beaten silver trumpets of Numbers chapter ten. We will look at how they were made, who was commanded to blow them, every occasion Scripture names, the difference between the two sounds the Law itself distinguishes, the traditional calls that developed later in Israel, and how a believer today can actually put a horn to his lips and sound it. We will be careful the whole way through to mark the line between what God commanded and what men added.

The Instruments

I. Two Instruments, One English Word

The King James translators used the word trumpet for two very different instruments, and a great deal of confusion comes from missing that. One is a natural animal horn, curved, hollowed out, unshaped by any craftsman except that the tip is opened. The other is a manufactured metal instrument, straight, beaten out of solid silver by command of God, and belonging to the tabernacle service.

The King James does give us clues where the words differ. When you read cornet, or ram's horn, or trumpet of the jubile, you are almost always looking at the animal horn. When you read of trumpets in the hands of the priests at the altar, at the beginnings of months, over the burnt offerings, you are looking at the silver instruments. Both are called a trumpet in English. Both were blown by the breath of a man. But they had different makers, different keepers, and to some extent different purposes.

The Horn

A natural ram's horn, hollowed and opened. Made by God, not by a craftsman. Blown by priests, judges, watchmen, soldiers, and common men alike.
  • Sinai, Exodus 19
  • Jericho, Joshua 6
  • Gideon's three hundred, Judges 7
  • The jubile, Leviticus 25
  • The crowning of kings
  • The watchman on the wall

The Silver Trumpets

Two straight trumpets beaten from one piece of silver at the direct command of God. Blown by the sons of Aaron, the priests.
  • Calling the assembly
  • Signalling the camps to journey
  • The alarm of war
  • The new moons and the feasts
  • Over the burnt offerings
  • Temple worship under David
Teacher's Note

The two instruments overlap in Scripture and are sometimes sounded together, as at the bringing up of the ark in First Chronicles fifteen, where both the cornet and the trumpets are named in the same verse. Do not build a hard wall between them. The point of the distinction is accuracy in reading, not a new set of rules.

Numbers 10:1–2

II. The Silver Trumpets: Their Making and Their Keepers

God did not leave the design of these instruments to preference. He specified the number, the metal, and the method of manufacture.

Numbers 10:1–2 And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying, Make thee two trumpets of silver; of a whole piece shalt thou make them: that thou mayest use them for the calling of the assembly, and for the journeying of the camps.

Three details matter. First, there were two of them, not one and not a dozen. That number is deliberate, because as we will see, one trumpet and two trumpets meant two entirely different commands. Second, they were silver, not gold and not bronze. Third, they were of a whole piece, meaning beaten out of a single mass of metal rather than assembled from soldered parts. The same word for beaten work is used of the golden lampstand and of the cherubim on the mercy seat. God attaches value to things hammered whole from one piece.

The keepers were named just as plainly.

Numbers 10:8 And the sons of Aaron, the priests, shall blow with the trumpets; and they shall be to you for an ordinance for ever throughout your generations.

This is a priestly instrument. It was not handed to whoever wanted to sound it. When Israel went out to battle against Midian, Phinehas the son of Eleazar the priest went with the holy instruments and the trumpets in his hand. When Abijah stood against Jeroboam, he pointed to the priests with sounding trumpets as the proof that God Himself was Judah's captain. The trumpet in the priest's hand was a declaration that this action, this journey, this war, this offering, was being brought before God.

2 Chronicles 13:12 And, behold, God himself is with us for our captain, and his priests with sounding trumpets to cry alarm against you. O children of Israel, fight ye not against the LORD God of your fathers; for ye shall not prosper.

Under Solomon the two became a hundred and twenty, and the sound of them was joined to the singers at the dedication of the temple. That is the moment the glory came down.

2 Chronicles 5:12–13 Also the Levites which were the singers, all of them of Asaph, of Heman, of Jeduthun, with their sons and their brethren, being arrayed in white linen, having cymbals and psalteries and harps, stood at the east end of the altar, and with them an hundred and twenty priests sounding with trumpets: It came even to pass, as the trumpeters and singers were as one, to make one sound to be heard in praising and thanking the LORD; and when they lifted up their voice with the trumpets and cymbals and instruments of musick, and praised the LORD, saying, For he is good; for his mercy endureth for ever: that then the house was filled with a cloud, even the house of the LORD.

What They Looked Like

Scripture does not describe the shape of the silver trumpets, but two sources outside the Bible give us a picture that agrees with each other. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the first century, described them as slightly less than a cubit in length, a narrow tube a little thicker than a flute, with a mouthpiece wide enough to take the breath and a flared bell at the end. And the Arch of Titus, carved in Rome after the destruction of Jerusalem in the year seventy, shows the spoils of the temple being carried in triumph, including the lampstand and a pair of long straight trumpets. Both point to the same thing: a straight, slender, flared horn, nothing like the curved animal horn.

Teacher's Note

Some teachers connect the silver of the trumpets to the atonement money of Exodus thirty, where every man numbered gave a half shekel of silver as a ransom for his soul, and that money was appointed for the service of the tabernacle. The connection is worth mentioning as a picture, since silver in the sanctuary consistently carries the theme of redemption. But the text of Numbers ten does not say the trumpets were made from that particular silver. Present it as a possible picture and never as a proven fact, and never lean the weight of a doctrine on it.

The Signal Code

III. The Calls of Numbers Ten

Here is the heart of the matter, and it is the passage that most people never read carefully. God gave Israel a signal code. A camp of well over a million people, spread out around the tabernacle by tribe, had no radios and no runners fast enough. The trumpets were the command channel. And the code had distinct calls that meant distinct things.

Numbers 10:3–7 And when they shall blow with them, all the assembly shall assemble themselves to thee at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation. And if they blow but with one trumpet, then the princes, which are heads of the thousands of Israel, shall gather themselves unto thee. When ye blow an alarm, then the camps that lie on the east parts shall go forward. When ye blow an alarm the second time, then the camps that lie on the south side shall take their journey: they shall blow an alarm for their journeys. But when the congregation is to be gathered together, ye shall blow, but ye shall not sound an alarm.

Read verse seven again, because it is the key that unlocks the whole subject. Ye shall blow, but ye shall not sound an alarm. Scripture itself draws a line between two kinds of sound. There is the plain blast, which gathers and assembles. And there is the alarm, which moves, warns, and goes to war. They are not the same sound and they must not be confused, because if they are confused, the camp does the wrong thing.

The Two Sounds of the Law

The plain blast gathers. It calls the people to stand still before God at the door of the tabernacle. It is the sound of convocation, of assembly, of come and hear.

The alarm moves. It breaks camp, it sends the tribes forward, it goes out to war, it warns the city. In the Hebrew this is the word teruah, a broken, urgent, repeating sound rather than one long tone.

Then note the second variable, which is how many trumpets are blown. Two trumpets sounding together calls the whole assembly. One trumpet alone calls only the princes, the heads of thousands. That is a leaders' meeting, and the ordinary Israelite hearing a single horn knew it was not for him. This is the earliest command structure in Scripture, and it was run entirely on breath and brass.

The last two are worth stating in full, because they carry a promise attached to the sound.

Numbers 10:9–10 And if ye go to war in your land against the enemy that oppresseth you, then ye shall blow an alarm with the trumpets; and ye shall be remembered before the LORD your God, and ye shall be saved from your enemies. Also in the day of your gladness, and in your solemn days, and in the beginnings of your months, ye shall blow with the trumpets over your burnt offerings, and over the sacrifices of your peace offerings; that they may be to you for a memorial before your God: I am the LORD your God.

Notice the phrase that appears in both verses: a memorial before your God, and ye shall be remembered. The trumpet was never magic. It did not move God because of the vibration in the air. It was a covenant reminder, an appointed sign that said to heaven and to the camp alike, these are the people who belong to the LORD, and they are calling on His name. God had bound Himself to answer that sound.

Exodus 19–20

IV. The Horn at Sinai

Before Israel ever made a trumpet, they heard one. And the one they heard was not blown by any man.

Exodus 19:16 And it came to pass on the third day in the morning, that there were thunders and lightnings, and a thick cloud upon the mount, and the voice of the trumpet exceeding loud; so that all the people that was in the camp trembled. Exodus 19:19 And when the voice of the trumpet sounded long, and waxed louder and louder, Moses spake, and God answered him by a voice. Exodus 20:18 And all the people saw the thunderings, and the lightnings, and the noise of the trumpet, and the mountain smoking: and when the people saw it, they removed, and stood afar off.

This is the first trumpet in the Bible and it belongs to God. No man is standing on that mountain with a horn. The sound came out of the cloud, it grew louder rather than fading as a man's breath would fade, and it announced the giving of the Law. Every trumpet after this one is an echo of that one. When a priest lifted a horn at the tabernacle, he was reproducing in a small way the sound that shook the mountain when God spoke the Ten Commandments.

And there was a call at Sinai too. God gave Israel a signal for when they could approach.

Exodus 19:13 There shall not an hand touch it, but he shall surely be stoned, or shot through; whether it be beast or man, it shall not live: when the trumpet soundeth long, they shall come up to the mount.

The long sounding of the horn was the signal to come near. Hold that thought, because it comes back at the end of this study in a way that ought to make a believer's heart leap.

Hebrews 12:18–19 For ye are not come unto the mount that might be touched, and that burned with fire, nor unto blackness, and darkness, and tempest, And the sound of a trumpet, and the voice of words; which voice they that heard intreated that the word should not be spoken to them any more.

Leviticus 25

V. The Trumpet of the Jubile

Once every fifty years, the horn was blown for a purpose that no other sound in the ancient world carried. It set men free and gave them back their land.

Leviticus 25:9–10 Then shalt thou cause the trumpet of the jubile to sound on the tenth day of the seventh month, in the day of atonement shall ye make the trumpet sound throughout all your land. And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof: it shall be a jubile unto you; and ye shall return every man unto his possession, and ye shall return every man unto his family.

Mark the timing carefully. The jubile trumpet did not sound on the first day of the year. It sounded on the Day of Atonement, after the blood had been carried in. Liberty was proclaimed on the far side of atonement, never before it. The order is the gospel in miniature. Blood first, then freedom. A man was not released because the horn was loud. He was released because atonement had been made, and the horn announced what the blood had accomplished.

Every debt was cancelled at that sound. Every servant sold for poverty went home. Every field that had passed out of a family's hands returned. A man could be far from his inheritance, working another man's ground, with no legal means of getting home, and then hear one long note carried across the hills and know that it was over.

The Pattern to Preach

The jubile horn did not create liberty. It proclaimed it. The freedom was already secured by the atonement that day, and the trumpet simply carried the news to every man in the land so he could get up and walk into what was already his. That is exactly what preaching is. We do not make men free. Christ made them free at the cross, and we are the horn that carries the sound to the far end of the field.

The Battlefield

VI. The Trumpet in War

The alarm of Numbers ten was not a symbol. Israel actually fought that way, and Scripture records the results with no apology.

Jericho

Seven priests, seven horns, seven days, and on the seventh day seven circuits. Then one long blast and a shout.

Joshua 6:4–5 And seven priests shall bear before the ark seven trumpets of rams' horns: and the seventh day ye shall compass the city seven times, and the priests shall blow with the trumpets. And it shall come to pass, that when they make a long blast with the ram's horn, and when ye hear the sound of the trumpet, all the people shall shout with a great shout; and the wall of the city shall fall down flat, and the people shall ascend up every man straight before him. Joshua 6:20 So the people shouted when the priests blew with the trumpets: and it came to pass, when the people heard the sound of the trumpet, and the people shouted with a great shout, that the wall fell down flat, so that the people went up into the city, every man straight before him, and they took the city.

For six days the horns sounded and nothing happened. That is the part people skip. Obedience to the sound was required long before any result was visible. There is a lesson there for anyone who has been walking in obedience without seeing a wall move yet.

Gideon

Judges 7:20 And the three companies blew the trumpets, and brake the pitchers, and held the lamps in their left hands, and the trumpets in their right hands to blow withal: and they cried, The sword of the LORD, and of Gideon.

Three hundred men, and not a sword drawn among them at the start. Each man had a horn in one hand and a broken pitcher with a light in it in the other. The Midianite host heard three hundred horns and assumed three hundred companies, because in that army each trumpet represented a unit. The sound itself routed them. God won that battle with light and noise and obedience.

The Gathering Call

The horn also raised the army in the first place. Ehud blew a trumpet in the mountain of Ephraim and Israel came down after him. Saul blew a trumpet throughout all the land. Nehemiah, rebuilding the wall under threat, kept a trumpeter at his side.

Nehemiah 4:18–20 For the builders, every one had his sword girded by his side, and so builded. And he that sounded the trumpet was by me. And I said unto the nobles, and to the rulers, and to the rest of the people, The work is great and large, and we are separated upon the wall, one far from another. In what place therefore ye hear the sound of the trumpet, resort ye thither unto us: our God shall fight for us.

That is a picture of a scattered people who are still one people. Separated on the wall, one far from another, but bound together by a sound they all knew and all agreed to answer.

Ezekiel 33

VII. The Trumpet of the Watchman

Of every use of the trumpet in Scripture, this is the one that lands hardest on a teacher or a preacher, because God attaches blood to it.

Ezekiel 33:3–6 If when he seeth the sword come upon the land, he blow the trumpet, and warn the people; Then whosoever heareth the sound of the trumpet, and taketh not warning; if the sword come, and take him away, his blood shall be upon his own head. He heard the sound of the trumpet, and took not warning; his blood shall be upon him. But he that taketh warning shall deliver his soul. But if the watchman see the sword come, and blow not the trumpet, and the people be not warned; if the sword come, and take any person from among them, he is taken away in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at the watchman's hand.

The watchman is not responsible for the response. He is responsible for the sound. If he blows and men ignore it, their blood is on their own heads. If he sees the sword and stays silent to keep the peace, God requires their blood at his hand. There is no third option where the watchman quietly decides the warning would upset people.

This is why the prophets constantly speak of the trumpet. Joel called for it at the coming of the day of the LORD. Amos asked whether a city could hear a trumpet and not be afraid. Hosea was told to set the trumpet to his mouth because the people had transgressed the covenant and trespassed against the Law. Isaiah was told to make his own voice into one.

Joel 2:1 Blow ye the trumpet in Zion, and sound an alarm in my holy mountain: let all the inhabitants of the land tremble: for the day of the LORD cometh, for it is nigh at hand. Amos 3:6 Shall a trumpet be blown in the city, and the people not be afraid? shall there be evil in a city, and the LORD hath not done it? Hosea 8:1 Set the trumpet to thy mouth. He shall come as an eagle against the house of the LORD, because they have transgressed my covenant, and trespassed against my law. Isaiah 58:1 Cry aloud, spare not, lift up thy voice like a trumpet, and shew my people their transgression, and the house of Jacob their sins.

There is one more use in this family, and it is the call to repentance. The same horn that sounds the alarm also calls a solemn assembly and a fast.

Joel 2:15 Blow the trumpet in Zion, sanctify a fast, call a solemn assembly.

The Coronation

VIII. The Trumpet of the King

In Israel a king was not made by a crown alone. He was anointed with oil, and then a horn sounded, and the people answered with a cry. That was the moment his reign was published.

1 Kings 1:34 And let Zadok the priest and Nathan the prophet anoint him there king over Israel: and blow ye with the trumpet, and say, God save king Solomon. 1 Kings 1:39 And Zadok the priest took an horn of oil out of the tabernacle, and anointed Solomon. And they blew the trumpet; and all the people said, God save king Solomon. 2 Kings 9:13 Then they hasted, and took every man his garment, and put it under him on the top of the stairs, and blew with trumpets, saying, Jehu is king.

Even a usurper knew the pattern. Absalom arranged for the sound of the trumpet to be the signal that men should say, Absalom reigneth in Hebron. The counterfeit borrows the form of the real thing, which is why the sound alone proves nothing. The question is always whether the man the horn announces was anointed by God or anointed himself.

And this is the meaning that runs straight into the end of the age. When the last trumpet sounds in Revelation, the announcement is a coronation.

Revelation 11:15 And the seventh angel sounded; and there were great voices in heaven, saying, The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ; and he shall reign for ever and ever. Psalm 47:5 God is gone up with a shout, the LORD with the sound of a trumpet.

The Assembly

IX. The Trumpet in Worship

The horn was not only for war and warning. It was an instrument of praise, and the Psalms command it plainly.

Psalm 150:3 Praise him with the sound of the trumpet: praise him with the psaltery and harp. Psalm 98:6 With trumpets and sound of cornet make a joyful noise before the LORD, the King. Psalm 89:15 Blessed is the people that know the joyful sound: they shall walk, O LORD, in the light of thy countenance.

That phrase in the eighty-ninth Psalm, the people that know the joyful sound, is worth sitting with. There is a blessing pronounced on people who recognize the sound. Not people who make it, and not people who admire it. People who know it, who hear it and understand what it is calling them to do.

The great moments of Israel's worship were marked by it. When David brought up the ark, when Ezra laid the temple foundation, when Asa's generation swore covenant with God, when Hezekiah restored the Passover, when Nehemiah dedicated the wall, the horns sounded.

2 Samuel 6:15 So David and all the house of Israel brought up the ark of the LORD with shouting, and with the sound of the trumpet. 2 Chronicles 15:14 And they sware unto the LORD with a loud voice, and with shouting, and with trumpets, and with cornets. Ezra 3:10 And when the builders laid the foundation of the temple of the LORD, they set the priests in their apparel with trumpets, and the Levites the sons of Asaph with cymbals, to praise the LORD, after the ordinance of David king of Israel.

Leviticus 23 & Numbers 29

X. The Memorial of Blowing of Trumpets

Of the seven appointed times of the LORD, one is defined entirely by this sound. On the first day of the seventh month, God commanded a holy convocation whose whole content is the blowing of horns.

Leviticus 23:24–25 Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, In the seventh month, in the first day of the month, shall ye have a sabbath, a memorial of blowing of trumpets, an holy convocation. Ye shall do no servile work therein: but ye shall offer an offering made by fire unto the LORD. Numbers 29:1 And in the seventh month, on the first day of the month, ye shall have an holy convocation; ye shall do no servile work: it is a day of blowing the trumpets unto you.

God gives no explanation for this feast. Passover has its lamb and its exodus. Unleavened Bread has its purging of leaven. Tabernacles has its booths and its wilderness memory. Trumpets has a sound and a silence about its meaning. It is called a memorial, which means it is meant to make somebody remember something, but the text does not spell out what. What we can say with certainty is that it opens the seventh month, it stands ten days before the Day of Atonement, and it functions as a summons.

Psalm 81:3 Blow up the trumpet in the new moon, in the time appointed, on our solemn feast day.

Every new moon had its blast, according to Numbers ten. But this new moon had a feast attached to it, and it began the most solemn stretch of the year. The horn sounded, and every man in Israel had ten days before he stood at the Day of Atonement. That is a call to get right, and it comes with time built into it to actually do so.

Teacher's Note

There is a long tradition, both Jewish and Christian, of tying the Feast of Trumpets to the resurrection and the gathering of the saints. The parallels are real and worth teaching: a trumpet, an assembly, a summons before the throne. But this must never be turned into a calendar for the Lord's return. Christ said plainly that of that day and hour knoweth no man, and every attempt to fix a year on this feast has failed and has embarrassed the gospel. Teach the pattern. Refuse the date. If a hearer walks away with a year circled on a calendar, the teaching has done harm.

The Calls

XII. The Calls: What God Named and What Men Added

This is where care is required, because two things get mixed together and taught as if they were the same. Let us take them one at a time and keep them separate.

What Scripture Names

The Law distinguishes exactly two kinds of sound, and it does so in a single verse: ye shall blow, but ye shall not sound an alarm. That is the whole biblical vocabulary of calls.

SoundCharacterPurpose
The BlastOne sustained, unbroken toneGathering, assembling, convocation, announcing
The AlarmBroken, urgent, repeating notesMovement, war, warning, breaking camp
The Long BlastA blast sustained and heldApproach at Sinai, the fall of Jericho, the jubile

The third line above is not a separate command so much as a description of how a blast was sometimes held. The Scripture speaks of the trumpet sounding long at Sinai, and of a long blast with the ram's horn at Jericho. That is a plain blast, extended.

What Later Tradition Added

In the centuries after the temple, the synagogue developed a fuller set of named calls, largely because the rabbis were uncertain what the alarm of the Law actually sounded like. Rather than choose one, they preserved several and blew them all. These names are Hebrew words describing the sounds, not names of God, and there is no harm in using them so long as we know where they came from.

CallHow It Is BlownCharacter
TekiahOne long, steady, unbroken note, roughly three seconds, ending cleanlyThe plain blast. Announcement and assembly.
ShevarimThree medium broken blasts, each about a second, together equal to one tekiahA sighing or groaning sound.
TeruahNine or more very short staccato notes in rapid successionA sobbing or alarm sound. Urgency.
Shevarim–TeruahThe two joined together in a single breathBoth sounds of mourning in one call.
Tekiah GedolahThe great blast, one long note held as long as the breath will hold itThe closing call. The final sound.

The customary order in which they are sounded is a set of three groupings, each one opened and closed by a plain blast, with the final call held out as long as breath allows.

  • First grouping: Tekiah, then Shevarim joined to Teruah, then Tekiah.
  • Second grouping: Tekiah, then Shevarim, then Tekiah.
  • Third grouping: Tekiah, then Teruah, then Tekiah.
  • The close: Tekiah Gedolah, held out to the end of the breath.

The plain blast on either side of every alarm is the pattern of Scripture itself, where the gathering call frames the movement call. Whatever else the tradition added, it kept that much right.

Teacher's Note

Be direct with a class about this. The Bible does not command tekiah, shevarim, teruah, and tekiah gedolah by those names, and it does not command a hundred blasts, and it does not command any particular sequence. Those are the customs of the synagogue, developed after the temple fell, and no believer is under obligation to them. Neither are they forbidden. They are a reasonable and orderly way to blow a horn, and much of the pattern genuinely reflects the two sounds of Numbers ten. Use them freely if they help. Never let anyone bind them on another man's conscience, and never let anyone teach that a blast blown the wrong way has failed before God.

Practical Instruction

XIII. How to Blow the Shofar

A shofar has no reed, no valves, and no mouthpiece in the modern sense. The sound is made entirely by the vibration of your own lips, which the horn then amplifies and shapes. If you have ever played a trumpet, a bugle, or a trombone, you already know the principle. If you have not, the skill is learnable in an afternoon and refined over months.

Choosing a Horn

  • Ram's horn. The horn of Scripture. Curved, generally twelve to twenty inches, higher and brighter in tone, and the easiest for a beginner because it takes less air.
  • Kudu horn. The long spiral horn, often two to four feet, deeper and more resonant. Impressive but harder to sound at first and much harder to control.
  • Ibex or antelope horn. Less common, similar handling to the ram.
  • Avoid a horn with a cracked or roughly cut mouth opening. The rim should be smooth against the lips. A rough edge will cut you and will fight you.
Teacher's Note

Jewish tradition forbids the horn of a cow or an ox, reasoning that the instrument of the golden calf should not stand before God as an accuser. That reasoning is thoughtful and there is no need to argue with it, but it is a rabbinic ruling and not a commandment of Scripture. Scripture names the ram specifically at Jericho and at Sinai, which is reason enough to prefer a ram's horn without needing to add a rule God did not give.

Making the Sound

  1. Buzz without the horn first. Press your lips lightly together, keep them relaxed in the center and firm at the corners, and blow steadily until they vibrate. It should sound like a bee, not like blowing out a candle. Do this for a minute or two before you ever pick up the horn. If you cannot buzz your lips, you will not sound the horn.
  2. Set the horn to the lips. Place the mouth opening firmly against your lips so no air escapes around the edge. Many people find it far easier to place it at the corner of the mouth rather than dead center, because the natural opening in a ram's horn is small and often off to one side. There is no right or wrong here. Find the spot where the seal is complete and the buzz passes through.
  3. Breathe from the belly. Take air deep, low, and full. Do not puff out your cheeks. Do not blow from the throat. The pressure comes from your diaphragm pushing steady air through a small, firm opening in the lips.
  4. Blow through, not at. A common beginner mistake is a hard burst of air that produces nothing but wind noise. The opening between your lips is too wide. Tighten the corners, make the aperture smaller, and let the air move through with steady pressure rather than a blast.
  5. Find the fundamental. The first note you get will be the horn's natural low tone. Get it clean and hold it. That single steady note is the plain blast, and it is more than half of everything Scripture asks of a horn.
  6. Reach the second note. Tighten the lips a little more and speed the air. Most shofars will jump to a second note, usually somewhere between a fifth and an octave above the first. Two clear notes is a full working range for a shofar, and the great majority of blowing is done on one note anyway.
  7. Learn to stop cleanly. A blast should end, not trail off and die. Cut the air with the diaphragm and stop the lip vibration at the same instant. A clean ending is the difference between a call and a noise.
  8. Build the broken sounds. Once the plain note is reliable, practice cutting it into three even segments for the broken call, and then into rapid short bursts for the alarm. The alarm is done with the tongue and the breath together, not by starting and stopping with the lips.
  9. Practice short and often. Ten minutes a day beats an hour once a week. Your lips are muscle and they tire quickly at first. If your tone goes thin and airy, stop for the day. Playing through fatigue teaches bad habits.

Care of the Horn

  • Wipe the mouthpiece after use and let the inside air out and dry. Moisture is what makes a horn smell.
  • Rinse the inside occasionally with warm water and a very little mild soap, then dry it fully. Never boil it and never soak it.
  • Keep it out of direct sun, out of a hot vehicle, and away from heaters. Heat warps and cracks horn.
  • A light coat of mineral oil or beeswax on the outside once in a while keeps it from drying out.
  • Handle it as an instrument, not a relic. It is a tool for the service of God, and it deserves care for the same reason a good Bible deserves a shelf.

A Note for the Assembly

If a horn is going to be sounded in a service, someone should be assigned to it, that person should practice beforehand, and the congregation should be told plainly what the sound means before it is blown. An unexplained blast in a service produces confusion. An explained blast produces reverence. That is not a matter of style. It is the direct principle of Numbers ten, where every call had a known meaning and every hearer knew what he was supposed to do when he heard it.

Wisdom and Warning

XIV. Order, Motive, and the Uncertain Sound

Paul gives us the governing principle for any signal given among God's people, and he takes it straight from the battlefield.

1 Corinthians 14:8 For if the trumpet give an uncertain sound, who shall prepare himself to the battle?

An uncertain sound is a sound nobody can act on. If the call means nothing definite, then the hearing of it accomplishes nothing definite. Paul applies that to speech in the assembly, but the image comes from a real signal on a real field, and the standard runs both ways. Blow with clarity or do not blow.

Then there is the matter of motive, and the Lord Jesus addressed it directly.

Matthew 6:2 Therefore when thou doest thine alms, do not sound a trumpet before thee, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may have glory of men. Verily I say unto you, They have their reward.

A trumpet sounded so men will look at the man blowing it has already received everything it is going to get. This is worth saying plainly, because the shofar has become fashionable in some circles and is sometimes handled as a spectacle or, worse, as a charm. Let three warnings stand.

  • It is not a talisman. Nothing in Scripture attributes power to the horn itself. At Jericho the wall fell because God said it would fall. The horns were obedience, not force. A believer who blows a horn expecting the sound to break something in the spirit realm by its own virtue has crossed from faith into superstition.
  • It is not a performance. If the point of the blast is that people should be impressed with the length of the note or the skill of the blower, the trumpet has been sounded before him in the streets, and he has his reward already.
  • It is not a substitute for the Word. The horn announces. It never teaches. A congregation can hear a hundred blasts and still not know what God requires of them. The sound calls men to assemble. What happens after they assemble is where the actual work is done.
Who May Blow Today

The silver trumpets were placed specifically in the hands of the sons of Aaron, and that priesthood found its completion in Christ, our great High Priest after the order of Melchizedek. The temple, the altar, and the sacrifices those trumpets sounded over were fulfilled in Him once for all.

The horn, however, was never restricted to the priesthood. Ehud blew it, Gideon's three hundred blew it, watchmen on the walls blew it, and the whole nation heard the jubile blast. And the believer today stands in Christ as part of a royal priesthood, made kings and priests unto God. So there is no barrier to a believer sounding a horn at the appointed times, in worship, in warning, or in rejoicing. There is simply no ground for claiming that anyone who does so is performing a temple rite. Christ finished that.

The Fulfillment

XV. The Trump of God

Every trumpet in Scripture has been building toward one sound that has not yet been heard. The first trumpet in the Bible was blown by God on Sinai. The last one will be blown by God as well, and it will not be a warning. It will be a gathering.

Matthew 24:29–31 Immediately after the tribulation of those days shall the sun be darkened, and the moon shall not give her light, and the stars shall fall from heaven, and the powers of the heavens shall be shaken: And then shall appear the sign of the Son of man in heaven: and then shall all the tribes of the earth mourn, and they shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with power and great glory. And he shall send his angels with a great sound of a trumpet, and they shall gather together his elect from the four winds, from one end of heaven to the other.

That is the plain blast of Numbers ten written across the whole earth. Not the alarm that moves the camp, but the gathering call that brings the whole assembly to the door. And it comes, by the Lord's own word, immediately after the tribulation of those days, which means the church that is looking for it must be prepared to endure and not merely to escape.

1 Corinthians 15:51–52 Behold, I shew you a mystery; We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed. 1 Thessalonians 4:16–17 For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first: Then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air: and so shall we ever be with the Lord. Isaiah 27:13 And it shall come to pass in that day, that the great trumpet shall be blown, and they shall come which were ready to perish in the land of Assyria, and the outcasts in the land of Egypt, and shall worship the LORD in the holy mount at Jerusalem. Zechariah 9:14 And the LORD shall be seen over them, and his arrow shall go forth as the lightning: and the Lord GOD shall blow the trumpet, and shall go with whirlwinds of the south.

Now bring the whole study together. At Sinai the people were told that when the trumpet sounded long, they could come up to the mount. For fifteen hundred years that invitation stood as a memory of a mountain nobody could touch. At the jubile the horn proclaimed liberty to men who could not free themselves, and it sounded only after atonement had been made. At every coronation the horn published the reign of the anointed king. At every feast it called an assembly. At every wall it warned a city.

And at the end, one trumpet does all of it at once. Atonement has been made by the blood of Christ. Liberty is proclaimed to every captive. The King is crowned and the kingdoms of this world become the kingdoms of our Lord. The assembly is called from the four winds. And the ancient signal is given at last: when the trumpet soundeth long, they shall come up.

A Word to the Hearer

The horn is going to sound. That is not a question and it does not depend on anyone's opinion about it. The only question that matters is what you will be doing when it does, and whether you know the sound.

If you have never come to Christ, understand what the jubile taught. Liberty was proclaimed on the day of atonement and not one hour before it, because no man is freed until the blood has been applied. Jesus Christ, the Son of God, died for your sins and rose again, and He is the atonement. You are not saved by keeping a feast, by blowing a horn, by good intentions, or by anything you can build with your own hands. For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast. Repent of your sins and trust Him, and the debt is cancelled and you go home to your possession and to your family.

And if you are His already, then take up the watchman's post. The sword is coming on the land and there are people within the sound of your voice who do not know it. God does not hold you accountable for how they respond. He holds you accountable for whether you blow. Blessed is the people that know the joyful sound: they shall walk, O LORD, in the light of thy countenance.
