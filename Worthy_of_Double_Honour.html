A Teaching Guide

Worthy of Double Honour

1 Timothy 5:17 in the Full Counsel of Scripture

✦

Introduction

Paul gives a short but weighty instruction to Timothy about how a local assembly should treat the men who lead and teach it. He says the elders who rule well are worthy of double honour, and he singles out for special mention those who labour in the word and doctrine. That single verse carries the weight of two ideas at once. It speaks of respect, and it speaks of provision, and Scripture never separates the two.

This study traces that principle from the law given at Sinai, through the years of the kingdom and the return from exile, into the words of Jesus Himself, and finally into Paul's own teaching to the churches. By the end we want to understand plainly what double honour means, why the Lord built it into His people from the very beginning, and how a healthy assembly practices it without ever sliding into either greed on one side or neglect on the other.

Section I

What the Scripture Says: 1 Timothy 5:17–18

Everything in this study grows out of one short instruction Paul gave to a young pastor named Timothy, who was overseeing the church at Ephesus.

1 Timothy 5:17–18 Let the elders that rule well be counted worthy of double honour, especially they who labour in the word and doctrine. For the scripture saith, Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn. And, The labourer is worthy of his reward.

Notice how carefully Paul builds his case. He does not simply state an opinion and move on. He grounds the instruction in scripture, and he actually quotes two different sources to prove it. The first line, about the ox, comes straight from the law of Moses. The second line, about the labourer and his reward, is not found anywhere in the Old Testament. It matches, almost word for word, something Jesus Himself said. Paul calls both of these the scripture, which tells us something important about how the earliest church already regarded the words of the Lord Jesus, recorded by the Gospel writers, as carrying the same weight as the law and the prophets.

Notice too that Paul does not say all elders deserve equal treatment regardless of their labour. He says the ones who rule well are worthy of it, and he gives special weight to those who labour in the word and doctrine, meaning those who give themselves to teaching and preaching. Honour here is tied to faithful work, not to office alone.

The Starting Point

Double honour is not Paul's invention. He is drawing together a principle that runs through the entire Bible, from the law, from the pattern of the priesthood, and from the very mouth of Christ, and applying it to elders who labour well in teaching the word.

Section II

The Levites' Portion: Honour Written Into the Law

Long before Paul wrote to Timothy, the Lord had already built a pattern of honour into the life of Israel. When the tribes were given their inheritance in the land, one tribe received none. The tribe of Levi, set apart to serve in the tabernacle and later the temple, was given no farmland of its own.

Deuteronomy 18:1–2 The priests the Levites, and all the tribe of Levi, shall have no part nor inheritance with Israel: they shall eat the offerings of the LORD made by fire, and his inheritance. Therefore shall they have no inheritance among their brethren: the LORD is their inheritance, as he hath said unto them.

Instead of land, the Lord gave the Levites a portion drawn from the service they performed for the whole nation. The people brought their tithes, and those tithes became the Levites' wages for the work of the tabernacle.

Numbers 18:21 And, behold, I have given the children of Levi all the tenth in Israel for an inheritance, for their service which they serve, even the service of the tabernacle of the congregation.

This was never framed as charity. It was framed as fair wages for real work, work that benefited the whole nation even though most of the nation never saw it done. The one tribe who could not farm for itself was fed by the tribes who could, because their labour served everyone.

The Pattern Set at Sinai

From the very founding of Israel's worship, the Lord tied honour for those who serve in holy things to material provision, not to warm feelings alone. That pattern did not end with the Levites. It is the same pattern Paul applies to elders who labour in the word.

Section III

The Ox That Treads the Corn

The first scripture Paul quotes in 1 Timothy 5:18 comes from a short, almost humble command tucked into the law concerning the treatment of animals.

Deuteronomy 25:4 Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn.

In that day, an ox walked over harvested grain to separate it from the stalk, and as it worked, it was allowed to eat some of what it was trampling. Muzzling the animal would have forced it to labour all day surrounded by food it was never permitted to taste. The Lord forbade that, and in doing so He established a principle far bigger than farm animals. The one who labours to produce a benefit has a right to share in that benefit. Withholding it while the work is being done is not thrift. It is injustice.

Paul reaches back to this small agricultural law and applies it directly to men who labour in the word and doctrine. If the Lord cared this much about a working ox, He certainly cares about the men who give their lives to teaching His people. The principle did not begin with elders, but it was always going to land on them.

Why an Ox?

It can feel strange at first that Paul defends the pay of a Christian teacher by quoting a verse about livestock. But that is exactly his point. If God legislated fairness for an animal doing physical labour, how much more does He expect fairness toward a man giving his mind, his time, and his heart to teach sound doctrine to the flock. The lesser case proves the greater one.

Section IV

When the Portion Was Withheld

Scripture does not only tell us what the pattern should be. It also shows us what happens when a nation ignores it. After the return from exile, Nehemiah discovered that the portion promised to the Levites had quietly stopped coming.

Nehemiah 13:10–11 And I perceived that the portions of the Levites had not been given them: for the Levites and the singers, that did the work, were fled every one to his field. Then contended I with the rulers, and said, Why is the house of God forsaken? And I gathered them together, and set them in their place.

Notice the result. It was not that the Levites simply grumbled and kept serving anyway. They left. The men whose calling was to minister in the house of God were forced back into farming just to survive, and the house of God itself was left forsaken because the people had quietly stopped honouring the pattern the Lord had commanded. Neglecting the portion did not save the nation anything. It cost them their worship.

Contrast that with an earlier king who took the opposite approach. When Hezekiah led a great revival, one of his very first actions was to restore proper support for the priests and Levites.

2 Chronicles 31:4 Moreover he commanded the people that dwelt in Jerusalem to give the portion of the priests and the Levites, that they might be encouraged in the law of the LORD.

Hezekiah understood something Nehemiah later had to fight to restore. Faithful teaching and worship do not run on good intentions alone. They run on a people who take seriously their part in supporting the ones who serve them, and when that support is restored, Scripture says plainly it encourages those men in the law of the LORD.

Teacher's Note

This is a useful pairing to teach side by side. Nehemiah shows the cost of neglect, and Hezekiah shows the fruit of faithfulness. Avoid using either passage to guilt a congregation into giving out of fear. The point is simply that Scripture records real consequences, both ways, and asks us to learn from both.

Section V

The Labourer Is Worthy of His Hire

The second scripture Paul quotes in 1 Timothy 5:18, that the labourer is worthy of his reward, is not found in the law of Moses at all. It matches, almost word for word, something Jesus said while sending out His disciples to preach.

Luke 10:7 And in the same house remain, eating and drinking such things as they give: for the labourer is worthy of his hire. Go not from house to house.

Jesus gave nearly the same instruction earlier, when He sent out the twelve.

Matthew 10:9–10 Provide neither gold, nor silver, nor brass in your purses, nor scrip for your journey, neither two coats, neither shoes, nor yet staves: for the workman is worthy of his meat.

Both times, the Lord sent men out to preach with the clear expectation that the people they served would provide for their needs along the way. He did not tell them to labour without support, and He did not treat that support as an unfortunate necessity. He called it what it was, wages rightly earned.

Paul Quoting the Gospel as Scripture

When Paul writes, the scripture saith, and then quotes a line that matches the Lord's own words as Luke recorded them, he is placing the Gospel account on equal footing with the law of Moses. This is a striking early witness to how the apostles themselves regarded the recorded words of Christ. It was not treated as mere memoir or tradition. It was scripture, worthy of the same weight as Deuteronomy.

Section VI

Paul's Full Argument in 1 Corinthians 9

Paul did not only mention this principle in passing to Timothy. He built an entire, extended argument around it when the Corinthians questioned his right to be supported for his ministry.

1 Corinthians 9:7 Who goeth a warfare any time at his own charges? who planteth a vineyard, and eateth not of the fruit thereof? or who feedeth a flock, and eateth not of the milk of the flock?

Paul then reaches for the very same verse about the ox that he later gives to Timothy, and asks his readers plainly whether God's concern was ever really about oxen at all.

1 Corinthians 9:9–10 For it is written in the law of Moses, Thou shalt not muzzle the mouth of the ox that treadeth out the corn. Doth God take care for oxen? Or saith he it altogether for our sakes? For our sakes, no doubt, this is written: that he that ploweth should plow in hope; and that he that thresheth in hope should be partaker of his hope.

Paul's conclusion is direct. If those who serve the altar are fed from the altar under the old order, then those who now preach the gospel under the new covenant have an even clearer right to be supported by the gospel they preach.

1 Corinthians 9:13–14 Do ye not know that they which minister about holy things live of the things of the temple? and they which wait at the altar are partakers with the altar? Even so hath the Lord ordained that they which preach the gospel should live of the gospel.

Notice the phrase, the Lord hath ordained. Paul is not asking for a favor. He is describing a standing instruction from Christ Himself. This is the same conclusion Paul later draws for Timothy, applied specifically to elders who labour in teaching.

The Same Argument, Twice

What Paul teaches briefly in 1 Timothy 5:17–18, he defends at length in 1 Corinthians 9. Both passages rest on the same foundation, the ox that treads the corn, and both arrive at the same conclusion, that those who labour in gospel work are entitled to be supported by it.

Section VII

Two Kinds of Honour, Held Together

It would be easy to read 1 Timothy 5:17 as a verse only about money, or only about respect. Scripture teaches both at once, and refuses to separate them. The honour Paul describes is worth doubling because it carries both weight of esteem and weight of provision together.

The respect side of honour shows up clearly in several places. Paul tells the Thessalonians to esteem their leaders highly, and the writer of Hebrews tells believers to remember and even submit to those who watch over their souls.

1 Thessalonians 5:12–13 And we beseech you, brethren, to know them which labour among you, and are over you in the Lord, and admonish you; And to esteem them very highly in love for their work's sake. And be at peace among yourselves.
Hebrews 13:7, 17 Remember them which have the rule over you, who have spoken unto you the word of God: whose faith follow, considering the end of their conversation... Obey them that have the rule over you, and submit yourselves: for they watch for your souls, as they that must give account, that they may do it with joy, and not with grief: for that is unprofitable for you.

The provision side of honour is just as clear. Paul tells the Galatians plainly to share material good things with those who teach them, and he later thanks the Philippians for doing exactly that.

Galatians 6:6 Let him that is taught in the word communicate unto him that teacheth in all good things.
Philippians 4:15–16 Now ye Philippians know also, that in the beginning of the gospel, when I departed from Macedonia, no church communicated with me as concerning giving and receiving, but ye only. For even in Thessalonica ye sent once and again unto my necessity.

John gives a third example, commending believers who supported traveling teachers of the truth so that the truth itself might advance.

3 John 5, 8 Beloved, thou doest faithfully whatsoever thou doest to the brethren, and to strangers... We therefore ought to receive such, that we might be fellowhelpers to the truth.

One Word, Two Meanings

The Greek word Paul uses for honour in this passage carries both senses at once, worth or esteem, and price or value paid. That is exactly why double honour makes sense as both double respect and double pay. Paul is not choosing between the two meanings. He is using a word built to hold them together, and Scripture backs that up by showing us both kinds of honour in action, esteem freely given and provision faithfully sent.

Neither Alone Is Enough

A congregation that praises its teachers but never supports them, and a congregation that pays its teachers but never respects them, have both fallen short of what Scripture actually asks for. Double honour is respect and provision, given together, to those who labour well.

Section VIII

Guarding the Balance: Neither Greed Nor Neglect

Scripture is honest enough to show us what happens when this pattern breaks down on the side of the one being honoured, not only on the side of the one giving honour. The clearest warning comes from the sons of the priest Eli.

1 Samuel 2:12–13 Now the sons of Eli were sons of Belial; they knew not the LORD. And the priests' custom with the people was, that, when any man offered sacrifice, the priest's servant came, while the flesh was in seething, with a fleshhook of three teeth in his hand.

Rather than receiving the portion the law actually allowed them, Eli's sons took by force whatever they wanted, before the offering had even been properly given to the Lord.

1 Samuel 2:17 Wherefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD: for men abhorred the offering of the LORD.

Their greed did not merely dishonour themselves. It caused the people to despise the offering of the Lord altogether. This is the danger on the other side of double honour. Provision that Scripture rightly commands can be twisted into an excuse for greed by the very men entrusted to receive it.

Peter addresses this directly when he writes to elders, using language that echoes the calling Paul describes to Timothy.

1 Peter 5:2 Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly; not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind.

An elder who labours well in the word is worthy of support, but he is never to labour for the sake of that support, and he is never to demand it, manipulate for it, or measure his ministry by it. Double honour is the church's grateful response to faithful service. It is not a wage an elder is permitted to chase.

The Balance Scripture Asks For

A congregation that neglects the ones who labour among them repeats Nehemiah's mistake. An elder who grasps at honour for himself repeats the mistake of Eli's sons. Faithful practice avoids both, provision given freely by the church, and ministry offered willingly by the elder, never for filthy lucre.

Section IX

Living It Out

What does all of this look like in an ordinary local church? It begins with recognizing that the men who labour faithfully in teaching sound doctrine are doing real work, work that benefits the whole body even when most of the congregation never sees the hours spent in study and prayer behind it.

It means esteeming those men highly and speaking well of their labour, following the pattern given to the Thessalonians. It means submitting to sound teaching rather than resisting it, following the pattern given in Hebrews. And where a church is able, it means giving cheerfully toward the support of those who give their lives to the word, following the pattern Paul lived out among the Galatians, the Philippians, and the Corinthians.

None of this is a formula that guarantees a particular salary or a particular level of praise. It is a principle, rooted in the character of God Himself, who cared for a working ox, provided for a landless tribe, sent His own Son with the promise of provision for His workers, and taught His apostles to expect the same. A healthy congregation honours its faithful teachers, and a faithful teacher never serves for the sake of that honour, but receives it with gratitude when it comes.

A Simple Test

Ask two questions of any local church. Do we esteem and submit to those who labour well among us in the word? And do we see to it, as we are able, that they are not left to labour without support? Where both answers are yes, the pattern of double honour is alive and well.

Conclusion

A People Who Honour Rightly

From the ox that trod the corn, to the tribe of Levi fed from the offerings of Israel, to Nehemiah's grief over a forsaken house of God, to the very words of Jesus sending out His labourers, the witness of Scripture is steady. Those who labour faithfully in holy work are worthy of honour, and that honour is never only a feeling. It is respect given freely, and provision given faithfully, held together as one thing.

Paul asks the church to count its faithful elders worthy of double honour, especially those who labour in the word and doctrine. May we be a people who esteem such men rightly, support them willingly, and never forget that the pattern was set by the Lord long before it ever reached us.

The labourer is worthy of his reward.
