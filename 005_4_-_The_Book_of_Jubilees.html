A Scriptural Study

The Book of Jubilees

Weighing an ancient retelling of Genesis against the word of God

✦

Before We Begin

The Book of Jubilees is less well known than the Book of Enoch, but it comes up often in the same circles, and for believers who hold that God’s Sabbath and appointed times still stand, it can look especially appealing. It claims to be an angel’s own retelling of Genesis, given to Moses on Sinai, and it puts heavy stress on the Sabbath, the feasts, and a fixed calendar. Someone who already believes those things matter could be tempted to reach for Jubilees as extra support.

That temptation is exactly why this book needs careful handling. Agreement on a single point, even an important one, does not make a source trustworthy across the board. This study looks at what Jubilees actually is, what it claims about its own origin, where it genuinely echoes what scripture already teaches, where it adds material scripture never confirms, and why, in spite of its popularity in one ancient community, it was never received as the word of God.

Seven parts follow: what the book is and where it came from, its claim to come from an angel speaking to Moses on Sinai, the ground it actually shares with scripture on the law before Sinai, the calendar dispute at its center, its expansion of the Watchers story, why it was never received as scripture, and finally how to make honest use of it.

Part One

What Is the Book of Jubilees

Jubilees is an ancient Jewish retelling of Genesis and part of Exodus, most likely written in the second century before Christ. Its own Hebrew title, Sefer ha-Yovelim, means the Book of Divisions, and it earned the nickname Lesser Genesis or Little Genesis because it walks back through the same ground Genesis already covers, from creation to the giving of the law, and reshapes it into a different kind of book.

The reshaping is what gives Jubilees its name. Instead of following ordinary years, the author organizes all of history into forty-nine year cycles called jubilees, themselves built from seven-year weeks, and assigns a precise date within that scheme to nearly every event in Genesis. Abraham’s birth, the flood, the binding of Isaac, and dozens of other events are each given an exact place on this calendar. Genesis itself never does this. The dating system belongs entirely to the later author, not to Moses or to the events themselves.

Like the Book of Enoch, Jubilees survives complete only in the ancient Ethiopic language, Ge’ez, and for the same reason. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church and the related Eritrean Orthodox Church are the only Christian bodies that include it in their Old Testament. Every other stream of the faith, Jewish and Christian alike, has left it outside the canon. Fragments in the original Hebrew were also recovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and in fact more copies of Jubilees turned up at Qumran than of almost any other non-canonical work found there, a point worth returning to later.

Bonus

Jubilees and 1 Enoch are close cousins. Jubilees quotes and depends directly on material from the Book of Watchers, the same section of 1 Enoch examined in the companion study on the Book of Enoch, and both books were treasured by the same community at Qumran. If you have already studied that teaching, several pieces here will feel familiar, since Jubilees is, in part, building on Enoch’s foundation rather than starting fresh.

Part Two

Its Claim of Origin: An Angel on Sinai

Jubilees does not present itself as a human author’s commentary on Genesis. It presents itself as direct revelation, dictated to Moses by an angel called the Angel of the Presence during the forty days Moses spent on Mount Sinai. That is a bold claim, and it deserves to be checked against what scripture itself says happened during those forty days.

Exodus 24:15–18 And Moses went up into the mount, and a cloud covered the mount... And the glory of the LORD abode upon mount Sinai, and the cloud covered it six days: and the seventh day he called unto Moses out of the midst of the cloud... And Moses was in the mount forty days and forty nights.

Exodus tells us plainly who spoke to Moses on the mountain. It was the LORD Himself, calling to Moses out of the cloud, not a secondary angel delivering a parallel set of writings. Scripture records what Moses received during that time: the law, the pattern for the tabernacle, and the covenant God made with Israel, all of it written down in the books Moses himself produced under God’s hand. Nowhere does scripture leave room for a second, hidden book handed down at the same time and never mentioned again until it resurfaces centuries later.

Hold This Clearly

Jubilees does not simply claim to be a helpful commentary on Moses. It claims to be revelation on the same level as what Moses actually wrote, delivered at the same moment, by an angel. That is a claim scripture itself gives no support for, and it is the first and clearest reason to treat the book’s contents with real caution rather than as a hidden supplement to the five books Moses gave Israel.

Part Three

What It Gets Right: The Law Before Sinai

It would be dishonest to pretend Jubilees gets nothing right. Its central argument, that Sabbath keeping and God’s commandments were not invented at Sinai but were already known and kept by the patriarchs long before Moses, is a point scripture itself already makes, without any need for help from Jubilees.

Genesis 26:5 Because that Abraham obeyed my voice, and kept my charge, my commandments, my statutes, and my laws. Genesis 2:1–3 Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them... And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made.

Genesis places the Sabbath at creation, long before Abraham, and tells us in plain words that Abraham kept God’s commandments, statutes, and laws generations before Sinai. Jubilees takes this same true principle and runs much further with it, inserting Sabbath keeping and detailed festival observance directly into the lives of Adam, Noah, and the other patriarchs at points where Genesis records no such command at all.

This is the pattern worth noticing throughout the whole book. Jubilees starts from a real, biblical truth and then fills in far more detail than scripture ever gives, presenting the added detail with the same confidence as the truth it started from. The believer who already knows, from Genesis and Exodus alone, that God’s law did not begin at Sinai has no need to borrow that conclusion from Jubilees. Scripture already carries the weight on its own.

Part Four

The 364-Day Calendar Question

The most distinctive feature of Jubilees, and the one that caused the most controversy in its own time, is its insistence on a fixed 364-day solar calendar, exactly fifty-two weeks, arranged so that every feast day and every Sabbath falls on the same day of the week, year after year, without exception.

Genesis 1:14 And God said, Let there be lights in the firmament of the heaven to divide the day from the night; and let them be for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years.

Scripture tells us that God appointed the sun, moon, and stars together to mark seasons, days, and years, but it does not hand down a fixed mathematical formula for calculating the calendar the way Jubilees does. Jubilees goes well beyond what Genesis states, insisting that any calendar tracking the moon is a corruption invented by men, and that only its own 364-day system is the true, created order.

History shows this calendar was never the one Israel actually used. The mainstream Jewish calendar, followed at the Jerusalem temple through the time of Christ, tracked months by the moon, which is why 364 days divides unevenly against a true solar year and drifts out of step with the actual seasons over time unless corrected. The fixed calendar in Jubilees appears to reflect the specific practice of the sectarian community that preserved it in such numbers at Qumran, not the practice of Israel at large or of the temple priesthood.

Teacher’s Note

This is a case where scripture itself does not settle every detail of calendar calculation, so caution is needed on both sides. It would go too far to say Jubilees is proven wrong about every point of its calendar. It also goes too far to treat its 364-day system as revealed truth simply because it appears in an old book that also happens to affirm the Sabbath. The honest position is that scripture establishes the Sabbath and the appointed times themselves as binding, while leaving the fine mechanics of calendar calculation as a matter this book claims far more certainty about than God’s word ever does.

What Scripture Establishes

Confirmed without need of an outside source:
  • The Sabbath instituted at creation
  • Abraham keeping God’s law before Sinai
  • Sun, moon, and stars appointed for seasons
  • The appointed feasts given through Moses

What Jubilees Adds

Claimed with certainty scripture does not give:
  • A fixed 364-day solar calendar
  • Exact jubilee-cycle dates for Genesis events
  • Sabbath and feast keeping placed onto Adam and Noah
  • An angel dictating a second book on Sinai

Part Five

The Watchers, Retold Again

Jubilees also retells the account of the Watchers, the angels who sinned before the flood, drawing directly on the same material found in the Book of Enoch’s Book of Watchers. It adds its own layer on top, describing evil spirits that survive the flood through the descendants of the fallen Watchers and continue to trouble mankind afterward, along with an account of a tenth of these spirits being permitted to remain under a figure named Mastema, while the rest are bound.

2 Peter 2:4 For if God spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment. Jude 1:6 And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day.

As with Enoch, scripture itself already confirms the reality of angels who sinned and now await judgment. What Jubilees adds, a named ruler over a tenth of the spirits, an arrangement for how many were bound and how many were released, and a detailed cause-and-effect story linking these spirits to specific troubles after the flood, comes entirely from this book and its source material, not from anything Moses, Peter, or Jude ever wrote. The same caution given in the companion study on Enoch applies here without needing to be reinvented. Where scripture is silent on the details, silence is still the wiser course.

Part Six

Why It Was Never Received as Scripture

The reasoning here follows much the same path as it does for the Book of Enoch, with one difference worth stating plainly up front. Unlike Enoch, no writer of the New Testament ever quotes Jubilees or attributes a word of it to an inspired source. Jude confirms one line connected to Enoch. No apostle does the same for Jubilees. Whatever claim Jubilees might make for itself, it does not even carry the single point of New Testament contact that Enoch has.

Luke 24:44 These are the words which I spake unto you, while I was yet with you, that all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me.

Jesus described the recognized Hebrew scriptures as the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms. Jubilees was never counted among the books of Moses by Israel at large, and the priesthood and the wider Jewish community that preserved and transmitted the Hebrew scriptures never treated it as such. Its heavy representation among the Dead Sea Scrolls tells us that one particular community, likely a separatist priestly group with its own calendar disputes, valued it highly. It does not tell us that Israel as a whole, or the temple, or the rabbis who followed, ever recognized it as scripture. In fact the community that treasured it most heavily was itself outside the mainstream religious life of Jerusalem, which cuts against rather than for the book’s claim to universal recognition.

2 Timothy 3:16–17 All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.

What God gave through Moses is described as sufficient to furnish the man of God unto every good work. A second angelic book, surfacing only among one sectarian community and never received by Israel at large, by the temple, or by the apostles who quote Moses constantly throughout the New Testament, does not meet that standard. The absence is itself the evidence. If this book truly stood alongside the five books of Moses, some trace of that recognition would appear somewhere in the wider record. It does not.

Part Seven

How to Use This Book Rightly

Jubilees can be read the same way Enoch can, as a genuine window into how one segment of Jewish thought in the centuries before Christ understood the patriarchs, the law, and the calendar. That kind of historical reading has real value and can help a believer understand the world the New Testament was written into, including some of the calendar disputes still echoing in Jesus’ own day.

What must never happen is treating any detail found only in Jubilees, an exact jubilee-year date, the 364-day calendar, the story of Mastema and the spirits, as though it carries the weight of scripture. The Sabbath and the appointed times stand on their own biblical foundation, given through Moses and confirmed throughout the whole of scripture. They do not need Jubilees to prove they matter, and Jubilees should never be allowed to add rules or dates to them that God’s own word never gave.

1 Thessalonians 5:21 Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.

Read it if you are curious, learn what it can honestly teach about that era, and set aside anything it adds beyond what scripture already says. The measure never changes. God’s revealed word is the standard every other writing is tested against, not the other way around.

In Summary

The Book of Jubilees retells familiar ground from Genesis and Exodus, and on its central claim, that God’s law and Sabbath were known and kept long before Sinai, it echoes something scripture already teaches plainly through the account of Abraham. That much needs no outside book to establish it.

But Jubilees claims far more than that single point. It presents itself as a second revelation delivered by an angel on Sinai alongside the words Moses actually wrote, it insists on a calendar system scripture never mandates, and it was never received by Israel, the temple, or the apostles as the word of God. What Genesis and Exodus already reveal about the patriarchs and the law is sufficient, and it is that revealed word, not a jubilee-year count invented centuries later, that we are called to build our faith and our teaching upon.
