A Teaching Guide

Water Baptism

The First Act of Obedience, and Why It Does Not Save

✦

Introduction

Few subjects bring more joy to a new believer, and more confusion to the church, than water baptism. On one side stand those who treat it as a mere afterthought, something a Christian might get around to someday if he feels like it. On the other side stand those who make it the very thing that saves the soul, so that without the water a person is supposedly still lost in his sins. Both errors miss the heart of Scripture. Baptism is neither a thing to be ignored nor a thing that saves. It is the believer's first glad step of obedience, taken in the days right after he comes to Christ.

In this study we will look closely at what baptism is, what it pictures, and why the saved heart longs for it almost at once. Then we will face the question head on. Does the water itself wash away our sins? We will take up the very verses that are used to teach baptismal salvation, and we will answer each one with the plain witness of Scripture. By the end we want one truth settled in every heart, that we are saved by grace through faith in Christ alone, and that baptism is the beautiful fruit of that salvation, never its price.

Section I

What Water Baptism Is

The word baptize carries the plain idea of dipping, plunging, or immersing something fully in water. When John baptized at the Jordan, he chose his location for a simple reason, because there was plenty of water there to immerse people in.

John 3:23 And John also was baptizing in Aenon near to Salim, because there was much water there: and they came, and were baptized.

We see the same picture when Philip baptized the Ethiopian. The two of them went down into the water together, and then came up out of the water. Baptism in Scripture is not a sprinkling of a few drops, but a going down into the water and a coming up again, a full immersion that pictures something far deeper than getting wet.

Acts 8:38–39 And they went down both into the water, both Philip and the eunuch; and he baptized him. And when they were come up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord caught away Philip.

The Plain Meaning

To baptize is to immerse. The believer is lowered down into the water and raised back up out of it. This act is an outward sign that points to an inward reality, and understanding the picture is the key to understanding the whole subject.

Section II

The Command of the Lord

Baptism is not a human tradition that the church invented. The Lord Jesus Himself commanded it. In His final words before ascending to heaven, He sent His followers out to make disciples and to baptize those disciples. Because the Lord commanded it, no believer is free to despise it or set it aside as unimportant.

Matthew 28:19–20 Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you.

Notice the order the Lord gives. First they are taught and made disciples, then they are baptized, and then they are taught to keep His commandments. Baptism falls upon those who have already become disciples. It is the response of a heart that has already turned to Christ, not the doorway a person passes through to first reach Him. The same order appears at Pentecost, where the people first received the word and only then were baptized.

Acts 2:41 Then they that gladly received his word were baptized: and the same day there were added unto them about three thousand souls.

A Command, Not an Option

The Lord commanded baptism, so it is never to be treated lightly. Yet the order He gave is clear. People are first made disciples, and then they are baptized. Baptism is the obedient answer of a heart that already belongs to Christ.

Section III

A Burial and a Rising: The Picture Baptism Paints

Why water, and why this going down and coming up? Because baptism is a living picture of what already happened in the believer's soul when he trusted Christ. Going down into the water pictures a death and a burial. Coming up out of the water pictures a resurrection to new life. The apostle Paul lays this out plainly.

Romans 6:3–4 Know ye not, that so many of us as were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death? Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life.

When a believer is lowered into the water, he is declaring that his old life of sin has died with Christ and is buried. When he is raised up, he is declaring that he now walks in newness of life, raised together with the Lord. Paul says the same thing to the Colossians, joining the burial of baptism to a rising that comes through faith.

Colossians 2:12 Buried with him in baptism, wherein also ye are risen with him through the faith of the operation of God, who hath raised him from the dead.

Read those words with care. The rising comes through the faith of the operation of God. The power is in the faith and in the working of God, not in the water. The water shows the world, in a picture every eye can see, what God has already done inside the heart that believed.

An Outward Picture of an Inward Work

Baptism dramatizes the death, burial, and resurrection of Christ, and the believer's union with Him in all three. The going down is a burial of the old life. The coming up is a rising to new life. The water pictures the work, but it is faith and the power of God that accomplish it.

Section IV

The First Step of Obedience After Conversion

When a person truly comes to Christ, his heart is changed, and a changed heart longs to obey the One who saved it. This is why baptism in the book of Acts comes so quickly, often on the very same day a person believes. There is no long waiting period, no probation, no list of works to finish first. The new believer simply wants to follow his Lord, and baptism is the first glad step he takes.

Consider the Ethiopian. As soon as Philip preached Jesus to him and he believed, he saw water by the road and asked to be baptized without delay.

Acts 8:36–37 And as they went on their way, they came unto a certain water: and the eunuch said, See, here is water; what doth hinder me to be baptized? And Philip said, If thou believest with all thine heart, thou mayest. And he answered and said, I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God.

Hear Philip's one condition. If thou believest with all thine heart, thou mayest. Faith comes first, and baptism follows immediately on the heels of faith. We see the same urgency with the Philippian jailer, who was baptized in the middle of the night, the very hour he believed.

Acts 16:33 And he took them the same hour of the night, and washed their stripes; and was baptized, he and all his, straightway.

This is the right place for baptism in a believer's life. It belongs at the very beginning of the walk, right after conversion, as the first public confession that a person now belongs to Jesus Christ. A saved soul should not put it off, for it is the natural desire of a heart made new.

Fruit, and It Comes Quickly

Baptism follows faith without delay in the New Testament, often the same day or the same hour. It is the first fruit of a converted heart and the believer's first public step of obedience. The saved soul wants to be baptized, not in order to be saved, but because it already has been.

Section V

Saved by Grace, Not by Water

Now we come to the question that divides so many. Does the water of baptism wash away our sins and save the soul? The whole weight of Scripture answers no. Salvation is the gift of God, received by grace through faith, and it is set in deliberate contrast to anything we could do. Baptism, whatever else it is, is something we do. Therefore it cannot be the ground of our salvation.

Ephesians 2:8–9 For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast.

The verse could not be plainer. We are saved by grace, through faith, and the salvation is not of works, so that no man can boast in anything he has done. If baptism saved us, then salvation would be partly of works, and a man could boast that he had been baptized. Paul shuts that door completely.

Paul goes further still. He tells the Corinthians that Christ did not even send him to baptize, but to preach the gospel. If baptism were the act that saves, this would be a shocking thing for an apostle to say. The gospel saves, and baptism is not the gospel.

1 Corinthians 1:17 For Christ sent me not to baptize, but to preach the gospel: not with wisdom of words, lest the cross of Christ should be made of none effect.

Think also of Abraham, the father of the faithful. He was counted righteous by God simply because he believed, long before any ordinance was given to him. Righteousness came to him by faith alone, and the same pattern holds for us.

Romans 4:3, 5 For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness... But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness.

The Foundation That Cannot Move

Salvation is by grace through faith, and not of works. Baptism is a work of obedience, so it cannot be the thing that saves, or grace would no longer be grace. Hold this truth firmly, because every verse we examine next must be read in its light.

Section VI

He That Believeth and Is Baptized: Mark 16:16 Examined

The first verse often raised to teach that baptism saves is the word of the Lord in Mark.

Mark 16:16 He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved; but he that believeth not shall be damned.

At first glance this may seem to tie salvation to baptism. But read the whole verse, especially the second half, and the matter settles itself. The Lord names two things that mark a saved person, believing and being baptized. Then He tells us what brings damnation, and He names only one thing. He does not say, he that is not baptized shall be damned. He says, he that believeth not shall be damned.

This is the key. Condemnation hangs entirely on the absence of faith, never on the absence of baptism. If a lack of baptism damned the soul, the Lord would have said so in the very breath where He spoke of damnation. Instead He pointed to unbelief alone. Baptism is mentioned as the normal companion of true faith, the thing a believer naturally does, but it is unbelief, and unbelief only, that condemns.

A Simple Test

Picture a man who believes but, like the thief on the cross, dies before he can be baptized. By the words of this verse, is he damned? He believed, so he is not under the only condemnation the verse names, which is unbelief. The structure of the sentence itself proves that baptism is the fruit of faith, not the cause of salvation.

The Verdict on Mark 16:16

The Lord ties salvation to faith joined with its natural fruit, and He ties damnation to unbelief alone. Since the lost are condemned only for not believing, baptism cannot be what saves. It accompanies faith, it does not produce salvation.

Section VII

For the Remission of Sins: Acts 2:38 Examined

The second verse commonly raised is Peter's word at Pentecost.

Acts 2:38 Then Peter said unto them, Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost.

Some read the word for here as if it meant in order to obtain, as though baptism were the means by which sins are remitted. But the strongest answer to this is not a debate over a single word. It is the plain account of what God Himself did at the house of Cornelius. There, God poured out the Holy Ghost on the hearers before a single one of them was baptized in water. The Spirit only ever falls upon those who already belong to God, which means these men were saved and accepted while they were still dry.

Acts 10:44–47 While Peter yet spake these words, the Holy Ghost fell on all them which heard the word... For they heard them speak with tongues, and magnify God. Then answered Peter, Can any man forbid water, that these should not be baptized, which have received the Holy Ghost as well as we?

Look carefully at what happened. The Holy Ghost fell while Peter yet spake, before any water was brought. Only afterward did Peter call for them to be baptized. If their sins had not yet been remitted, God would not have given them His Spirit, for the Spirit is the seal of those who are already His. Their baptism followed their salvation. It did not produce it. Peter's words in Acts 2 must agree with what Peter himself saw God do in Acts 10. Baptism is the outward seal that we repent and turn to Christ, and it is bound up with the remission we receive by faith, but it is faith and repentance that lay hold of forgiveness, not the water.

Teacher's Note

You will sometimes hear the argument that the little word translated for in Acts 2:38 can mean because of rather than in order to, much as we say a man is wanted for a crime he has already done. That observation has some merit, yet it rests on a finer point of grammar that a sharp listener may contest. The far stronger and unanswerable proof is the event at Cornelius's house in Acts 10, where God gave the Spirit before baptism. Lean on that plain narrative first, and use the grammatical point only as a secondary support.

The Verdict on Acts 2:38

At Cornelius's house God gave His Spirit to believing hearers before they were ever baptized. Since the Spirit seals only those who already belong to Him, their sins were remitted by faith before the water. Acts 10 interprets Acts 2, and the order is always faith first, then baptism.

Section VIII

Baptism Doth Also Now Save Us: 1 Peter 3:21 Examined

The third verse, and perhaps the one that sounds most convincing at first, is Peter's word about the flood.

1 Peter 3:21 The like figure whereunto even baptism doth also now save us (not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God,) by the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

If we stopped at the first six words, we might think the case was closed. But Peter does not stop there, and we must not either. In the very same breath, by the leading of the Holy Ghost, Peter explains exactly what he means and guards us against the wrong reading. He tells us plainly what does not save, and then what does.

First, he says it is not the putting away of the filth of the flesh. In other words, it is not the water washing dirt from the body. Peter directly denies that the physical act of getting wet has any saving power. Then he tells us what the baptism that saves truly is. It is the answer of a good conscience toward God. The saving reality is the inward turning of a clear conscience to God in faith, not the outward water on the skin. And Peter anchors the whole thing where it belongs, by the resurrection of Jesus Christ. Our salvation rests on the risen Christ, laid hold of by a believing conscience that answers to God.

A Figure, Not a Magic Washing

Peter calls baptism a figure, that is, a picture or a likeness. Just as the ark carried Noah safely through the water of judgment, so baptism pictures the believer brought safely through death into new life in Christ. Peter could not have been clearer that it is not the water removing filth from the flesh that saves, but the believing conscience answering to God on the ground of the resurrection.

The Verdict on 1 Peter 3:21

Peter himself rules out the water as the saving thing, saying it is not the washing of the flesh. What saves is the answer of a good conscience toward God, resting on the resurrection of Christ. The verse that seems to teach baptismal salvation actually denies it in its own words.

Section IX

The Two Witnesses: The Thief and the Centurion

By the mouth of two witnesses every word is established. Scripture gives us two clear accounts that settle the whole matter beyond dispute, one of a man saved without ever being baptized, and one of men saved before they were baptized.

The first witness is the thief on the cross. He had no time and no means to be baptized. He was nailed to a cross and would die within hours. Yet he turned in faith to Jesus, and the Lord promised him paradise that very day.

Luke 23:42–43 And he said unto Jesus, Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom. And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise.

Here is a man assured of heaven by the Lord's own lips, with no baptism at all. If baptism were required to be saved, the Lord could not have made this promise. Faith alone carried the thief into paradise.

The second witness is Cornelius and those gathered in his house. As we have already seen, the Holy Ghost fell on them while they were still hearing the word, before any water touched them.

Acts 10:44, 47 While Peter yet spake these words, the Holy Ghost fell on all them which heard the word... Can any man forbid water, that these should not be baptized, which have received the Holy Ghost as well as we?

One man saved without baptism, and a whole household saved before baptism. Together these two witnesses prove that the water is never the cause of salvation. The thief shows that a soul can be saved with no baptism. Cornelius shows that the Spirit is given before baptism. In both cases faith came first, and the water, where it followed, only confessed what God had already done.

The Verdict of the Witnesses

The thief entered paradise with no baptism at all. The household of Cornelius received the Holy Ghost before their baptism. Two witnesses establish the matter. Salvation is by faith, and baptism follows as the confession of those already saved.

Section X

Why We Are Still Baptized

Having shown so plainly that baptism does not save, we must guard against the opposite error. Some hear that baptism is not required for salvation and wrongly conclude that it does not matter at all. That is not the spirit of Scripture, and it is not the heart of a true disciple. The Lord commanded baptism, and a heart that loves Him will gladly obey.

So why are we baptized? First, in simple obedience to the command of Christ. He told His people to be baptized, and that settles it for anyone who calls Him Lord. Second, as a public confession. Baptism is the believer standing before others and declaring without words that he has died to his old life and risen to follow Jesus. Third, as the answer of a good conscience toward God, that clear and joyful response of a soul that wants to do what is right before its Maker.

And notice the joy that goes with it. When the Ethiopian came up out of the water, he did not go away burdened. He went on his way rejoicing.

Acts 8:39 And when they were come up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord caught away Philip, that the eunuch saw him no more: and he went on his way rejoicing.

This is the true place of baptism in a believer's life. It does not save him, for he was already saved by grace through faith before he stepped into the water. But it is his first act of loving obedience, his public testimony, and a source of deep gladness. To be saved and then to refuse baptism is to begin the Christian walk with a stumble. To be saved and then to be baptized is to take the first joyful step in the long obedient walk of faith.

Fruit, Not Root

Keep the order right and the heart stays free. Baptism is the fruit of salvation, never its root. We are not baptized in order to be saved. We are baptized because we have been saved, and our new heart desires to obey, to confess Christ openly, and to walk in newness of life. Guard this order, and you guard the believer from both careless neglect and anxious legalism.

Conclusion

Saved by Grace, Baptized in Joy

We have traced water baptism from the command of the Lord, through the picture of death and resurrection it paints, to its rightful place as the first glad step of a new believer. And we have faced the hard verses one by one. Mark ties damnation to unbelief alone. Acts 2 is answered by Acts 10, where the Spirit came before the water. First Peter rules out the washing of the flesh with its own words. The thief and the centurion stand as two witnesses that faith, not water, brings a soul to God.

So let no one trust in the water to wash away his sins, for that work belongs to the blood of Christ, received by faith. And let no saved soul despise the water either, for the Lord commanded it, and a loving heart longs to obey. We are saved by grace through faith in Christ alone. Baptism is the beautiful confession of that salvation, taken up quickly, gladly, and in full assurance that the One who saved us has already done the saving work.

By grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God.
