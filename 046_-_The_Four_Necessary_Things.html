The Four Necessary Things

Acts 15, the Jerusalem Council, and the Question of Obedience

✦

Introduction

Few passages in the New Testament are pressed into service against obedience more often than Acts chapter fifteen. The argument is made simply. The apostles gathered at Jerusalem, considered what to require of Gentile believers, and settled on four items. Since God's commandments run to far more than four, the reasoning goes, everything else must have been quietly set aside. Believers today are told they are free from the Sabbath, free from the appointed times, free from the dietary distinctions, free from anything not named in that short list.

That reading collapses the moment we ask what question the council was actually convened to answer. A council answers the question put before it. It does not silently abolish every subject it never discussed. When we read the chapter carefully, watch the men who wrote the letter, and follow what they themselves did afterward, a very different picture emerges. The four things were not a ceiling placed over the Gentiles. They were a doorway opened to them.

I.

The Question Actually Before the Council

Luke tells us exactly what started the dispute, and he tells us in the very first verse of the chapter. Certain men came down from Judaea and began teaching something specific. Read their claim slowly, because everything in the chapter turns on it.

The Charge That Started It

Acts 15:1 And certain men which came down from Judaea taught the brethren, and said, Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved.
Acts 15:5 But there rose up certain of the sect of the Pharisees which believed, saying, That it was needful to circumcise them, and to command them to keep the law of Moses.

Notice the three words at the end of verse one: ye cannot be saved. That is the whole controversy. The question was never whether a Gentile believer ought to walk uprightly, honor his parents, keep himself from idols, or remember the day the Lord set apart. The question was whether a Gentile had to become a circumcised proselyte, entering through the ritual door of conversion, in order to be counted saved at all.

That is a question about the ground of salvation. It is not a question about the shape of a believer's life. The men in verse five wanted circumcision made a requirement for standing before God, and they wanted the Gentiles placed under the full apparatus of proselyte conversion as the price of admission. The council said no. It said no because salvation has never been purchased by any work of man, and grafting a ritual requirement onto grace corrupts the gospel itself.

Key Point

The Jerusalem Council was called to settle how a man is saved, not to settle how a saved man should live. Reading Acts 15 as a list of permitted commandments answers a question the apostles were never asked.

II.

Peter's Testimony and the Yoke

Peter stands up and appeals to what God had already done in the house of Cornelius. He does not argue from theory. He argues from the plain fact that God gave the Holy Ghost to uncircumcised Gentiles who believed, before any ritual step was taken.

Peter Before the Council

Acts 15:7-9 And when there had been much disputing, Peter rose up, and said unto them, Men and brethren, ye know how that a good while ago God made choice among us, that the Gentiles by my mouth should hear the word of the gospel, and believe. And God, which knoweth the hearts, bare them witness, giving them the Holy Ghost, even as he did unto us; And put no difference between us and them, purifying their hearts by faith.
Acts 15:10-11 Now therefore why tempt ye God, to put a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear? But we believe that through the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ we shall be saved, even as they.

Verse eleven is the hinge. Peter is not saying the Gentiles are exempt from something the Jews still carry. He says the opposite. He says we shall be saved even as they, by grace, on the same terms, in the same way. The Jew is not saved by circumcision either. Both stand on grace or neither stands at all.

What Is the Yoke?

Much has been built on this one word. It is worth being careful here, because the yoke cannot mean what many assume it means. If the yoke were simply God's law, we would have Peter contradicting the plain testimony of scripture in a dozen places.

The Character of God's Commandments

1 John 5:3 For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous.
Deuteronomy 30:11 For this commandment which I command thee this day, it is not hidden from thee, neither is it far off.
Psalm 19:7-8 The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart: the commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes.
Matthew 11:29-30 Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.

Scripture does not describe God's instruction as an unbearable weight. It describes it as perfect, right, pure, near at hand, and a joy to the heart. Something else is being named in Acts 15:10. In context, the yoke is the demand made in verses one and five: the requirement that a man earn his standing before God through the ritual gateway of conversion, and carry the whole system as the price of being accepted. That is a load no one has ever borne successfully, because no one has ever kept anything perfectly enough to purchase righteousness. Paul says the same thing when he warns that a man who seeks justification by circumcision has made himself a debtor to do the whole law, and has fallen from grace as a method of salvation.

Teacher's Note

Be honest with students about this point. The word translated yoke is not defined for us in the passage, and sincere believers read it differently. Some take it as the full weight of the sacrificial and priestly system, some as the accumulated traditions layered on top of the written word, some as the burden of works-righteousness itself. What can be said with confidence is what it cannot mean, because scripture will not allow God's own commandments to be called an intolerable burden. Teach the boundary firmly and hold the precise identification loosely.

III.

The Verdict of James and the Verse Everyone Skips

James gives the sentence of the council. Most teaching on this chapter quotes verses nineteen and twenty and stops there. The very next verse is the one that explains the whole decision, and it is almost always left out.

The Sentence of James

Acts 15:19-21 Wherefore my sentence is, that we trouble not them, which from among the Gentiles are turned to God: But that we write unto them, that they abstain from pollutions of idols, and from fornication, and from things strangled, and from blood. For Moses of old time hath in every city them that preach him, being read in the synagogues every sabbath day.

Read verse twenty and verse twenty-one together, because James joined them with the word for. He is giving the reason for his ruling. Write these four things to them, because Moses is preached in every city and read in the synagogues every Sabbath day.

If the council had just abolished the rest of God's instruction for Gentile believers, verse twenty-one is nonsense. Why would James close a ruling that supposedly frees Gentiles from the law by pointing out that the law is taught publicly every single week in every city they live in? That sentence only makes sense one way. The four items are what the new believer needs immediately, before he can sit at the table with anyone. The rest he will learn week by week as he sits and hears, the way any new disciple learns.

Key Point

Verse twenty-one is not an afterthought. It is the reason attached to the ruling. James expected these Gentile converts to be in the assembly every Sabbath, hearing the word taught, growing into the fullness of it over time. He was not writing a permanent four-item constitution. He was clearing the entrance so they could walk in and start learning.

✦ Bonus

Consider the practical situation. A pagan converts. He has grown up eating food from the idol temple, drinking blood in ritual meals, and living in a culture where sexual immorality was woven into worship itself. He cannot yet sit down and share a meal with Jewish believers because everything about his table would offend. The four items address exactly that gap. They are the immediate, urgent break with pagan worship that makes fellowship possible from day one. Everything else comes through teaching, and teaching takes time.

IV.

Where the Four Things Came From

Here is the detail that undoes the popular reading entirely. The four requirements were not newly invented by the apostles. They were drawn straight out of the law of Moses, from the section that specifically governs the stranger who dwells among Israel. James did not replace Torah with a shorter list. He quoted Torah.

The Source of the Four

Leviticus 17:8-9 And thou shalt say unto them, Whatsoever man there be of the house of Israel, or of the strangers which sojourn among you, that offereth a burnt offering or sacrifice, And bringeth it not unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, to offer it unto the LORD; even that man shall be cut off from among his people.
Leviticus 17:10 And whatsoever man there be of the house of Israel, or of the strangers that sojourn among you, that eateth any manner of blood; I will even set my face against that soul that eateth blood, and will cut him off from among his people.
Leviticus 17:13 And whatsoever man there be of the children of Israel, or of the strangers that sojourn among you, which hunteth and catcheth any beast or fowl that may be eaten; he shall even pour out the blood thereof, and cover it with dust.
Leviticus 18:26 Ye shall therefore keep my statutes and my judgments, and shall not commit any of these abominations; neither any of your own nation, nor any stranger that sojourneth among you.

Line them up. Pollutions of idols answers to sacrifice offered away from the Lord and to the worship of other gods. Blood and things strangled answer to the prohibition on eating blood and the requirement to drain it properly. Fornication answers to the whole catalogue of sexual sin in Leviticus eighteen, which closes by binding the stranger to the same standard as the native born.

This is decisive. If the council intended to release Gentiles from the law of Moses, it is a strange thing that every single requirement they issued was lifted from the law of Moses. What they did was point the new convert to the very passage that had always governed the stranger joining himself to the covenant people, and start him there.

Teacher's Note

The parallel with Leviticus seventeen and eighteen is strong and worth teaching, but do not overstate it as though James named the chapter. He did not. What we have is four requirements that correspond closely to four prohibitions binding on the sojourner in that section, in roughly the same order. Present it as a compelling correspondence that fits the evidence, not as a stated citation.

V.

What the Letter Never Mentions

Suppose for a moment the popular reading is correct, and the four items are the complete and exclusive set of obligations placed on Gentile believers. Follow that all the way out and see where it lands.

The letter says nothing about murder. Nothing about theft. Nothing about bearing false witness. Nothing about honoring father and mother. Nothing about coveting. Nothing about taking the Lord's name in vain. If the list is exhaustive, then a Gentile believer in the first century was free to lie, steal, and kill, so long as he stayed away from strangled meat.

No one believes that. Every honest reader admits at once that the moral commandments obviously still applied. But the instant that admission is made, the argument has already been surrendered. The list is no longer exhaustive. It is a starting point, and now the only remaining question is who decides which of the unmentioned commandments carried forward and which did not. Scripture decides, not our preferences.

Key Point

A list that omits murder and theft cannot be a complete statement of Christian duty. Everyone recognizes this instinctively for the commandments they already intend to keep. The same logic applies to the commandments they would rather set down.

And this is where the Sabbath question answers itself. The Sabbath is not omitted from the chapter. It is named in verse twenty-one. James assumes without argument that these Gentile believers will be in the assembly every Sabbath day hearing the word taught. He does not command it because he does not have to. It is simply the assumed rhythm of the community they were joining.

VI.

What the Apostles Did After the Council

If Acts 15 abolished obedience, the men who wrote it never got the message. Watch what Paul does after the council, in the same book, written by the same author.

Paul After Jerusalem

Acts 18:21 But bade them farewell, saying, I must by all means keep this feast that cometh in Jerusalem: but I will return again unto you, if God will. And he sailed from Ephesus.
Acts 20:6 And we sailed away from Philippi after the days of unleavened bread, and came unto them to Troas in five days; where we abode seven days.
Acts 21:23-24 Do therefore this that we say to thee: We have four men which have a vow on them; Them take, and purify thyself with them, and be at charges with them, that they may shave their heads: and all may know that those things, whereof they were informed concerning thee, are nothing; but that thou thyself also walkest orderly, and keepest the law.
Acts 24:14 But this I confess unto thee, that after the way which they call heresy, so worship I the God of my fathers, believing all things which are written in the law and in the prophets.
Acts 25:8 While he answered for himself, Neither against the law of the Jews, neither against the temple, nor yet against Caesar, have I offended any thing at all.

Acts twenty-one deserves particular attention. It happens years after the council. James is present. The elders are present. They tell Paul that a rumor is circulating that he teaches men to forsake Moses, and they instruct him to publicly demonstrate that the rumor is false, that he himself walks orderly and keeps the law. Paul complies. He does not object that the council settled all of that. He does not say the law was abolished at Jerusalem. He goes into the temple and proves the accusation false.

In the very same passage, the elders restate the letter to the Gentiles, and they restate it exactly as it was written, four items and nothing more. They saw no conflict between the letter and Paul demonstrating his own obedience. There is no conflict, because the letter never abolished anything.

Paul on the Law in His Letters

Romans 3:31 Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.
Romans 7:12 Wherefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good.
1 Corinthians 5:7-8 Purge out therefore the old leaven, that ye may be a new lump, as ye are unleavened. For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us: Therefore let us keep the feast, not with old leaven, neither with the leaven of malice and wickedness; but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.
1 Corinthians 7:19 Circumcision is nothing, and uncircumcision is nothing, but the keeping of the commandments of God.

First Corinthians chapter seven verse nineteen is a remarkable sentence when you sit with it. Paul, writing to a Gentile congregation, says circumcision counts for nothing and uncircumcision counts for nothing, and then names what does count: keeping the commandments of God. That verse alone settles the matter. Paul removes the ritual entrance requirement in exactly the way the council did, and in the same breath upholds obedience.

And in First Corinthians five he tells a Gentile church to keep the feast. He does not tell them it was abolished. He tells them how to keep it rightly, with sincerity and truth, because Christ our Passover is sacrificed for us.

VII.

Faith, Fruit, and the Order of Things

All of this rests on getting the order right, and the order matters more than almost anything else in this discussion.

We are saved by grace through faith, and that is not of ourselves. Nothing we do earns it, adds to it, or secures it. The council said so plainly and we say so plainly. But a person who has been saved does not stay the same. Obedience is not the price of the gift. It is the evidence that the gift was received.

Grace and Its Fruit

Ephesians 2:8-10 For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them.
John 14:15 If ye love me, keep my commandments.
1 John 2:4 He that saith, I know him, and keepeth not his commandments, is a liar, and the truth is not in him.
Matthew 5:17-19 Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled. Whosoever therefore shall break one of these least commandments, and shall teach men so, he shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven: but whosoever shall do and teach them, shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven.

Ephesians two is the whole thing in three verses. Verses eight and nine cut off every claim of merit. Verse ten tells us what we were saved for: good works which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them. Salvation is the beginning of obedience, not the end of it.

Growth Takes Time

There is also something pastoral here that the council understood well. New believers are not handed everything at once. They are fed, taught, and grown.

The Pattern of Discipleship

Hebrews 5:12-14 For when for the time ye ought to be teachers, ye have need that one teach you again which be the first principles of the oracles of God; and are become such as have need of milk, and not of strong meat. For every one that useth milk is unskilful in the word of righteousness: for he is a babe. But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil.
1 Peter 2:2 As newborn babes, desire the sincere milk of the word, that ye may grow thereby.

The four things were milk. James said so in his own way when he pointed to the synagogues and the Sabbath reading. Start here, break with the idol temple immediately, and then sit under the word and grow. That is discipleship, and it is exactly how the Lord commissioned it, teaching them to observe all things whatsoever he commanded.

VIII.

Answering the Objection Plainly

Set the pieces side by side and the answer is not difficult.

  • The question asked was about salvation. Verse one says it plainly. Certain men taught that a Gentile could not be saved without circumcision. That is what the council answered, and it answered no.
  • The answer given was about grace. Peter said hearts are purified by faith and that Jew and Gentile alike are saved by the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ. Nothing is added to that.
  • The four requirements came out of the law. They correspond to the very statutes that had always governed the stranger dwelling among the covenant people. Torah was not replaced with a shorter list. A portion of Torah was named as the immediate starting point.
  • The reason given points forward, not backward. Moses is read in the synagogues every Sabbath. The council expected these believers to keep hearing and keep growing.
  • The list is obviously incomplete. It omits murder, theft, false witness, and honoring parents. No one treats it as exhaustive when the omitted commandment is one they already keep.
  • The men who wrote it kept the commandments. Paul kept the feasts, kept the appointed times, purified himself in the temple to prove he walked orderly, and told a Gentile church that what counts is keeping the commandments of God.

So the answer to the question is this. The apostles were not telling Gentile believers to ignore God's commandments. They were removing a false requirement that had been laid on the door of salvation, and they were setting a new convert's feet on the first four steps of a road they expected him to keep walking.

✦ Bonus

There is a mirror image error worth naming, because it is just as dangerous. Some read Acts 15 correctly, see that obedience continues, and then swing so far that they begin treating their own obedience as the thing that secures them. That is the exact error the council condemned, only wearing different clothes. The men in verse five were not wrong to value the commandments. They were wrong to make a commandment the doorway into salvation.

Guard both sides. Grace alone saves. Obedience alone is the evidence. The moment obedience becomes the ground of standing rather than the fruit of it, we have walked right back into Acts 15:1 and called it faithfulness.

Conclusion

The Jerusalem Council did not lower the standard of holiness. It refused to let anyone raise a toll gate in front of the cross. A Gentile coming out of paganism did not need to be surgically converted into a Jew to belong to Christ. He needed to believe, and he was received on the spot, exactly as Cornelius was received.

Then he was told four things, and told where to go every Sabbath to learn the rest. Break with the idol temple. Break with sexual sin. Break with the blood of the pagan table. Come in, sit down, and hear the word taught. That is not a shorter religion. That is a door held open and a lifetime of learning on the other side of it.

So when someone says the apostles freed us from the commandments in Acts fifteen, take them gently to verse twenty-one and let James finish his sentence. He never intended those four things to be the end of the matter. He expected them to be the beginning.
