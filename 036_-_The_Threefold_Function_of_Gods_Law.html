A Core Pronomian Foundation

The Threefold Function of God’s Law

Moral, civil, and ceremonial: which aspects remain binding, which were fulfilled in Christ, and why the common dividing line is not as simple as it seems

✦

The Question Before Us

Sooner or later every serious believer runs into the question. The law of Moses contains commandments about love and theft and murder, and it also contains commandments about sacrifices, priestly garments, and the building of an altar. Are we bound to keep all of it, none of it, or some of it? And if some of it, then how do we tell which parts remain and which parts have ended? This is one of the most practical questions in the whole Bible, and getting it wrong sends a believer into one of two ditches.

To help answer it, teachers have long divided the law into three parts. They call them the moral law, the civil law, and the ceremonial law. The moral law is the eternal standard of right and wrong. The civil law is the body of statutes that governed Israel as a nation. The ceremonial law is the system of sacrifices, priesthood, and ritual that pointed forward to Christ. This division is a useful teaching tool, and we will use it. But we have to use it with care, because it can be turned into a blunt instrument that cuts away things God never cut away.

This teaching does two things at once. First, it shows the real and helpful difference between these three kinds of commandment, so you can sort the law instead of being overwhelmed by it. Second, it shows where the common version of this teaching goes wrong, sweeping the Sabbath, the feasts, and the clean and unclean distinctions into a pile labeled ceremonial and throwing them out, when Scripture treats them differently. The goal is to handle the law the way Christ and the apostles handled it, with a careful hand and not a careless one.

Part One

Where the Threefold Division Comes From

We should be honest from the start about what this division is and what it is not. The Bible never lines up the commandments into three labeled columns and says, here is the moral law, here is the civil law, here is the ceremonial law. The threefold division is a framework that teachers developed over the centuries to make sense of a real pattern they saw in Scripture. The pattern is genuine. The labels are ours. So we lean on the division as a help, not as a verse, and we keep it under the authority of the Scriptures rather than over them.

The pattern itself is not invented. Anyone reading the law can feel that the command not to murder is different in kind from the command about which bull to bring to the altar. One speaks to the conscience of every man in every age. The other speaks to a particular service that pointed to something greater. Scripture itself draws lines like this, even when it does not use our exact words.

Matthew 23:23 Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye pay tithe of mint and anise and cummin, and have omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith: these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone.

Notice that Jesus Himself ranks the commandments. He speaks of the weightier matters of the law, judgment and mercy and faith, and sets them above the careful tithing of garden herbs. He does not abolish the lighter matters; He says these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone. But He plainly teaches that not every commandment carries the same weight. There is an order within the law, a heart and a frame, and a faithful teacher learns to see it.

A Tool, Not a Verse

The threefold division of moral, civil, and ceremonial is a teaching framework, not a label the Bible itself assigns. It is genuinely useful for sorting the commandments, and Scripture does rank the law by weight and purpose. But because the lines are ours and not God’s, we must test every conclusion by the Scriptures and never let a neat category override a clear command.

Part Two

The Moral Law: The Eternal Standard

The first part is the moral law, and it is the simplest to settle. The moral law is the standard of righteousness that flows from the very character of God. Because God does not change, His standard of right and wrong does not change either. The moral law was true before Sinai, it was written in stone at Sinai, and it remains true now. It is summed up in the Ten Commandments and gathered into the two great commands to love God and to love our neighbor.

Matthew 22:37–40 Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.

Here is the heart of the moral law in the words of Christ. Love God, and love your neighbor. Every moral commandment is one of these two loves spelled out in detail. You shall have no other gods, you shall not take His name in vain, you shall keep His day holy, these spell out the love of God. You shall not murder, steal, lie, or covet, these spell out the love of neighbor. This is why the moral law cannot pass away. To abolish it would be to abolish the command to love, and love is the very nature of God.

Romans 13:8–10 Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law. For this, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law.

Paul, long after the cross, still quotes the Ten Commandments as the standard for the believer. He does not say these have been abolished. He says love fulfills them, that is, love does the very thing they command. The moral law tells us what love looks like in action, and the believer with a renewed heart keeps it not as a burden but as the natural shape of love. This part of the law remains in full force, written now upon the heart.

1 John 3:4 Whosoever committeth sin transgresseth also the law: for sin is the transgression of the law.

This single verse settles the matter against anyone who says the moral law is gone. Sin is defined as breaking the law. If the law were abolished, sin would have no meaning, for there would be nothing to transgress. The fact that Scripture still calls us out of sin, written near the end of the New Testament, proves that the standard which defines sin still stands. The moral law abides because the God whose character it reflects abides.

The Moral Law Remains

The moral law flows from the unchanging character of God, is summed up in loving God and neighbor, and is quoted as binding by the apostles long after the cross. It defines what sin is. It cannot pass away without love itself passing away. This part of the law is written on the heart of every believer and remains the standard of a righteous life.

Part Three

The Civil Law: Israel’s National Statutes

The second part is the civil law. These are the statutes that governed Israel as a nation under God, the case laws and penalties that ordered her courts, her property, her agriculture, and her national life. Things like the cities of refuge, the rules for boundary stones, the penalties handed down by Israel’s judges, and the regulations for a king all belong here. These laws applied God’s moral standard to the particular life of one nation in one land at one time.

We have to be careful here, because the civil law is not a third thing floating on its own. The civil statutes are applications of the moral law to a national setting. Behind the law about an unguarded pit or a goring ox stands the moral principle of protecting your neighbor from harm. So when we say the civil law no longer binds us in the same way, we do not mean its wisdom is gone. We mean that the church is not the nation of Israel, and we do not hold the sword of national government the way her judges did.

Romans 13:1–4 Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers. For there is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God. For rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil. Wilt thou then not be afraid of the power? do that which is good, and thou shalt have praise of the same: for he is the minister of God to thee for good. But if thou do that which is evil, be afraid; for he beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God to thee for good, to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil.

The believer now lives scattered among many nations, under many governments, not gathered as one people holding civil authority in one promised land. Paul tells us to be subject to the governing authorities God has placed over us. The civil penalties of Israel were given to Israel’s courts, and we are not those courts. Yet the righteousness behind those laws still teaches us. A wise believer reads Israel’s civil law to learn the justice and mercy of God, and then applies that justice and mercy in his own setting, even though he does not carry out the specific national penalties.

Teacher’s Note

Be careful how you speak about the civil law passing away, because a careless word here causes real confusion. Do not say the civil law was wrong or that its principles are now void. The justice it teaches is the justice of God and never changes. What has changed is the setting. The church is not a nation holding the sword in a promised land, so we do not execute Israel’s national penalties. We still honor the moral principles those penalties protected.

A simple way to put it for a class: the civil law shows us how God applied His unchanging justice to one nation. We learn the justice, we do not run the courtroom.

The Civil Law in Its Place

The civil statutes applied God’s moral standard to Israel as a nation in her land. The church is not that nation and does not hold civil authority the same way, so we do not carry out her specific national penalties. The justice and mercy behind those laws remain a true revelation of God’s character and still instruct the believer.

Part Four

The Ceremonial Law: Shadows Fulfilled in Christ

The third part is the ceremonial law, and here we must be the most precise of all, because this is where the most damage is done by sloppy teaching. The truly ceremonial law is the system of sacrifices, the Aaronic priesthood, the temple service, and the rituals of atonement. All of these pointed forward to Christ. They were shadows cast ahead of time by the coming substance. When the substance arrived, the shadows had finished their appointed work.

Hebrews 10:1 For the law having a shadow of good things to come, and not the very image of the things, can never with those sacrifices which they offered year by year continually make the comers thereunto perfect. Hebrews 10:11–14 And every priest standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins: But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God; From henceforth expecting till his enemies be made his footstool. For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified.

This is the clearest case of fulfillment in all the law. The sacrifices could never take away sin. They were pictures, pointing to the one true sacrifice. When Christ offered Himself once for all and sat down, the picture had served its purpose. The blood of bulls and goats is not offered again, because the thing it pointed to has come. The Aaronic priesthood gives way to the priesthood of Christ. This part of the law is fulfilled and closed, not because it was bad, but because it has been perfectly completed in Him.

Hebrews 9:11–12 But Christ being come an high priest of good things to come, by a greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands, that is to say, not of this building; Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us.

Here is the careful line a pronomian believer walks, and it guards against two errors at once. We do not drag the altar and the priesthood back, as though Christ had not finished His work. To rebuild the sacrificial system would be to deny the once for all offering of Christ. And we do not let the word ceremonial become a basket into which we toss everything we wish to discard. The sacrificial and priestly system is fulfilled. That is what the word ceremonial truly covers.

The Ceremonial Law Fulfilled

The sacrifices, the Aaronic priesthood, and the temple ritual were shadows of Christ. He fulfilled them once for all, and they are closed forever, never to be repeated. We do not rebuild the altar, for the substance has come. This is the true and narrow meaning of the ceremonial law that has ended in Christ.

Part Five

Where the Common Teaching Goes Wrong

Now we reach the heart of the matter, the place where the pronomian believer parts from the common teaching. The usual version of the threefold division runs like this. The moral law stays, the civil and ceremonial laws are gone, and into the ceremonial pile go the Sabbath, the feasts, and the clean and unclean distinctions. With one sweep of the word ceremonial, the appointed times and the dietary commands are swept off the table. This is where the tool becomes a blunt instrument, and we must take it back into a careful hand.

The problem is simple. The Sabbath, the feasts, and the dietary distinctions do not belong to the sacrificial system. They are not the blood of bulls. They are signs and appointed times that carry meaning of their own. To call them ceremonial in the same breath as the altar is to blur two very different things. The sacrifices pointed to the cross and ended at the cross. The Sabbath and the feasts point to a larger story of God’s working that is not yet complete.

Colossians 2:16–17 Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days: Which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ.

Many stop reading after the word sabbath and conclude these days are abolished. But look closely at what Paul actually says. He says these are a shadow of things to come. He does not say they were a shadow that has now vanished. The feasts and the Sabbath still cast their shadow forward, pointing to things yet to come, to the rest that remains and the kingdom not yet fully revealed. Paul’s point is that no man should judge the believer over how he keeps them. He is defending the keeping of these days against the judgment of outsiders, not abolishing them.

Hebrews 4:9–10 There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. For he that is entered into his rest, he also hath ceased from his own works, as God did from his.

Long after the cross, the book of Hebrews says there remains a rest for the people of God, and the word it uses is the word for a Sabbath rest. The Sabbath is not treated as a dead ceremony. It is treated as a sign that still stands and still points forward. The feasts tell the same story. Passover, Unleavened Bread, and Firstfruits were fulfilled in the death and resurrection of Christ, and Pentecost in the pouring out of the Spirit. But the autumn feasts, the Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and Tabernacles, point to things still ahead, to the return of Christ and the gathering of His people. A shadow that still points forward has not finished its work.

Teacher’s Note

This is the most contested part of the teaching, so handle it with both confidence and care. The strongest move is to make the listener slow down on Colossians 2:17. The common teaching races past the present tense. Paul says these days are a shadow of things to come, looking ahead, and the very next argument in Hebrews 4 says a Sabbath rest remains. Let Scripture, not the inherited category, set the conclusion.

Keep the distinction clean. The sacrifices pointed to the cross and ended there. The Sabbath and feasts point past the cross to the kingdom and the return of Christ, so they still stand as signs. Do not let anyone collapse these two into one pile under the single word ceremonial.

Truly Fulfilled and Closed

The animal sacrifices, the Aaronic priesthood, and the temple ritual of atonement. These pointed to the cross and ended at the cross. Christ offered Himself once for all, and they are never repeated.

Signs That Still Stand

The Sabbath, the appointed feasts, and the clean and unclean distinctions. These are signs and appointed times, not blood sacrifices. They carry meaning that still points forward, and Scripture after the cross still treats them as standing.

Why the Distinction Matters So Much

The whole pronomian position turns on this one careful cut. If you let the word ceremonial swallow the Sabbath, the feasts, and the dietary commands, you have thrown out things God never threw out, and you have done it on the strength of a man-made category rather than a clear word of Scripture. If instead you keep the word ceremonial to its true meaning, the sacrificial and priestly system fulfilled in Christ, then the law sorts cleanly. The shadows of the cross have ended in the cross. The signs that point to the kingdom still stand until the kingdom comes.

This is not splitting hairs. It is the difference between handling the law as Christ and the apostles did and handling it by an inherited tradition that overruled the text. Test the category by the Scripture, never the Scripture by the category.

The Whole Counsel

Handling the Law With a Careful Hand

Gather the whole study together and the picture is clear. The threefold division is a real help, as long as we remember the labels are ours and the Scripture is our judge. The moral law flows from the character of God and remains in full force, written now upon the heart, defining sin and describing love. The civil law applied that same moral standard to Israel as a nation, and while the church is not that nation and does not run her courts, the justice and mercy behind those laws still instruct us. The ceremonial law, rightly understood, is the sacrificial and priestly system that pointed to Christ and is now fulfilled and closed in Him, never to be rebuilt.

The error to avoid is the careless sweep that hides the Sabbath, the feasts, and the dietary commands inside the word ceremonial and discards them along with the altar. These are not the blood of bulls. They are signs and appointed times, and Scripture written after the cross still treats them as standing, still pointing forward to a rest that remains and a kingdom yet to come. So the faithful believer holds the law the way his Lord held it. He keeps the moral law as the shape of love. He learns from the civil law the justice of God. He rejoices that the sacrifices are fulfilled in Christ. And he keeps the Sabbath and the feasts as living signs, not as a wage to earn, but as the appointed times of the God who saved him.

Where It All Rests

Sort the law by Scripture, not by a man-made label. The moral law abides because God’s character abides. The civil law teaches us God’s justice though we do not hold Israel’s sword. The sacrifices are fulfilled and closed in Christ, never offered again. And the Sabbath and feasts remain as signs, shadows that still point forward to the kingdom of God.

Do not throw out what God has kept, and do not rebuild what Christ has finished. Handle the law with the careful hand of one who loves both the Lawgiver and His word.

Matthew 5:17–18 Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled. Colossians 2:17 Which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ.
